A Country Stopped Publishing Its Own Unemployment Rate

October 2023. The UK's Office for National Statistics did something a national statistical institute almost never does. It stopped publishing the country's headline labour market statistics.

The Labour Force Survey is the source of the unemployment rate, wage growth, and the numbers the Bank of England sets interest rates on. Response rates had fallen so far that the ONS judged its own estimates unfit to publish.

A replacement returned in February 2024 — carrying the label “official statistics in development”. A formal downgrade from accredited national statistics, and an unusually public admission that the numbers were provisional.

2 min. Let the first sentence land before adding anything. Ask the room: "what would have to go wrong for a government to stop publishing its own unemployment rate?" Take one or two answers, do not correct them, then advance.

~20%

The Bank of England, setting rates for 67 million people, leaned instead on HMRC payroll records and other sources with their own, different blind spots.

During 2024 the Bank delayed rate cuts, partly because official wage growth suggested inflation pressure remained elevated. If the surviving respondents skewed high-income, wage growth was overstated — and borrowing costs may have been held too high, for too long.

Poll: What Actually Went Wrong?

The Bank of England held interest rates high in 2024 partly on wage-growth figures from a survey with a ~20% response rate.

What is the single biggest risk in that?

A) The sample is too small, so the estimate is noisy
B) The people who stopped answering differ from those who kept answering
C) The survey questions were poorly worded
D) No real risk — 20% of a large survey is still thousands of people
2 min. FIRST THING THEY DO IN THIS COURSE. Say out loud: "open the deck link on your phone, tap an answer, results appear on the next slide." Deliberately answerable with zero background — it is a judgment call, not a recall question, so nobody can be wrong in a way that stings on day one. Expect a real D share.

Answer: B — Who Is Missing, Not How Many

A 20% response rate on a large survey still yields plenty of observations, so noise is not the binding problem. The problem is who the 20% are. If the workers who stopped responding differ systematically on the very thing being measured — employment and wages — the estimate is biased, and extra sample size does not fix bias.

Why D is tempting: every “n = 1,000 is enough” example you have seen assumes the sample is random. A collapsing response rate destroys exactly that assumption.

The Three Structures, Named

Cross-sectional data — many units, observed at a single point in time.

Time series data — a single unit, observed across many time periods.

Panel data (also: longitudinal) — the same units, observed across many time periods.

The word doing the work in the third one is “same”. A survey repeated every year with different respondents each round is a pooled cross-section, not a panel.

2 min. The pooled cross-section distinction is the most common question from a sharp student and it is Poll 2's distractor D. Get it on the record here so the poll is fair.

One Picture, Three Shapes

Count the directions the data can move in. One, one, or two.

1 min. Hold on the third panel. The whole reason panel data is the gold standard is that you can hold the unit fixed and watch it change — which is what every later identification strategy needs.

Notation You Will Use All Semester

\( y_i \qquad y_t \qquad y_{it} \)

\( y \) — what you measured (a burger price in dollars)

\( i \) — indexes the unit (a country), \( i = 1,\ldots,n \)

\( t \) — indexes the period (a semester), \( t = 1,\ldots,T \)

\( n, T \) — how many units, how many periods

Read it aloud: the subscripts tell you what varies. One subscript, one direction of variation. Two subscripts, a two-dimensional observation space — and everything in Topics 12–13 depends on which of those you brought.

2 min. Never present this notation bare — the fragment IS the interpretation and it is the only part students need to retain. Write y_it on the board and leave it there for the rest of the hour.

What Each Structure Lets You Ask

Question Cross-section \( y_i \) Time series \( y_t \) Panel \( y_{it} \)
Which country has the dearest burger today? Yes No Yes
Has Switzerland's burger got dearer since 2015? No Yes Yes
Did countries that devalued see burgers get cheaper? No No Yes

The third row is the one only a panel can reach — it needs variation within a country and comparison across countries.

One Dataset, Three Structures

You do not need three datasets. A panel contains the other two — slice it into whichever the question needs.

Common Misconception

“More data always means better analysis.”

A million rows of cross-sectional data cannot answer a question that requires time variation. Adding more countries to a single snapshot does not help you measure how exchange rates change.

Structure beats size. A well-built panel of 57 countries over 25 years is more powerful than a cross-section of 10,000 countries at one moment — because only the panel lets you hold the unit fixed and watch it move.

1 min. This is the sentence Poll 2 tests. Say it, then go straight to the poll without a bridge.

Poll: What Do You Actually Have?

You want to know whether raising the minimum wage in a city changed employment in that city. A colleague hands you a survey of 200,000 workers across 400 cities, all interviewed in March 2026.

What structure is that?

A) Panel — 400 cities is plenty of units
B) Time series — March 2026 is a time period
C) Cross-section — many units, one moment
D) Panel — each worker was asked about several past jobs
1 min. D is the productive error and it should get real votes. Do not signal.

Answer: C — Many Units, One Moment

200,000 rows and 400 cities, all from one instant. Adding units does not add time variation, and the question — did employment change — is a change question.

Why D is the interesting wrong answer: retrospective questions inside one interview feel like time. They are recall, not repeated observation. Panel requires the same units measured again. Same reason a survey repeated yearly with different respondents is a pooled cross-section, not a panel.

2  •  The Big Mac Index

An economic theory you can compute in two lines of pandas

18-minute block. Objective 1.2. This is the spine of the chapter and the thread that returns in Topic 2.

Purchasing Power Parity, In One Burger

A Big Mac costs $5.79 in the United States and CHF 7.20 in Switzerland. If goods markets worked perfectly — no tariffs, no transport costs, no local wage differences — what should the exchange rate be?

The rate that makes the two burgers cost the same: 7.20 / 5.79 = 1.24 francs per dollar

The actual market rate in January 2025 was about 0.90 francs per dollar. The franc buys more burger than it “should”. It is overvalued.

That is the whole theory: exchange rates should adjust until identical goods cost the same everywhere. The Economist built the index on it in 1986. Not a precision instrument — but the data is free, the arithmetic is transparent, and the intuition is immediate.

2 min. Resist naming the formula until the next slide. The arithmetic first, the notation second — students who see 7.20/5.79 before they see P_domestic/P_US never find the formula intimidating.

Two Equations, and What They Say Out Loud

\[ \text{Implied PPP} = \frac{P_{\text{domestic}}}{P_{\text{US}}} \qquad\qquad \text{Valuation}\,(\%) = \frac{\text{Implied PPP} - \text{Spot}}{\text{Spot}} \times 100 \]

The first one asks

What exchange rate would make these two burgers cost the same?

The second one asks

How far is the real world from that rate, in percent?

Sign convention: positive means the local currency is overvalued — the burger is dear relative to what exchange rates would predict. Negative means undervalued — the burger is cheap. \( P_{\text{domestic}} \) is in local currency; Spot is local currency per US dollar.

2 min. Every symbol gets glossed on this slide — do not move on until the sign convention is said out loud, because it is the thing Poll 3 checks and the thing students get backwards on the exam.

Do It By Hand First: Switzerland, January 2025

\[ \text{Implied PPP} = \frac{7.20}{5.79} = 1.2435 \ \text{CHF per USD} \]

\[ \text{Valuation} = \frac{1.2435 - 0.9009}{0.9009} \times 100 = +38.0\% \]

The Swiss franc is 38% overvalued against the dollar on this measure. Swiss burgers are dear because Swiss rents, wages and service costs are high — which is a hint we will pick up in ten minutes.

Where the inputs come from: CHF 7.20 and $5.79 are the January 2025 rows of local_price and the US dollar_price. The 0.9009 is dollar_ex — francs per dollar, the actual market rate that period. Nothing here is a modelling choice; all three are columns.

3 min. Make them compute 7.20/5.79 themselves before you advance the fragment. Phones out is fine. Only after they have a number do you show that pandas does the same arithmetic on 54 rows at once. Verified: spot 0.9009 on 2025-01-01, valuation +38.04%.

Poll: Read the Number

What does that number actually mean?

Answer: C — It Is a Statement About Exchange Rates

The valuation compares the actual spot rate with the implied PPP rate. It is not a claim about inflation, and it is not quite the price gap either.

Why A is nearly right and still wrong: the valuation is computed against dollar_ex, not against the price difference. Taiwan's dollar Big Mac price was $2.28 against $5.79 — a 61% price gap, not 59%. Related, not equal. B reverses the direction and turns a deviation into a forecast.

PPP Breaks Because a Burger Is Mostly Non-Tradeable

Restaurant rent

Local wages

Utilities

Taxes and regulation

Market power, franchise structure

None of these arbitrage across borders, and they vary enormously for structural reasons. A Swiss burger is dear because Switzerland is expensive to operate in — not because the franc is 38% “too strong”.

Common misconception: “the Big Mac Index proves currencies are misvalued.” It illustrates PPP logic. It is a pedagogical tool, not a trading signal. Pakko & Pollard (2003) is the academic treatment of why these deviations persist.

2 min. Anticipated question, and a good one: "why not use something standardised like an iPhone?" Why Python, Specifically

Tech economist job postings converge on five technical competencies:

Production Python or R

Expert SQL (Topic 11)

scikit-learn and ML libraries

Cloud infrastructure

Communicating to non-technical people

Python spans all five. Excel does none of them at scale — and caps out around a million rows. Stata and R handle 1–3 and struggle with 4–5. Python is the one language that runs the whole stack: pandas, statsmodels, scikit-learn, and deployment.

1.5 min. Do not turn this into a language war. The honest framing: R is excellent and widely used; you learn Python here because it is the only one that also does items 4 and 5, and because it is what the job postings ask for.

A DataFrame Is a Spreadsheet With Rules

Rule 1: it enforces types

A price column cannot quietly contain a country name. A spreadsheet lets that happen and says nothing.

Rule 2: every step is written down

Filtering, grouping and transforming happen in reproducible code, not manual clicks.

Another analyst — or you, in eight months, defending a number to a regulator — can rerun your script and get the same answer. No spreadsheet gives you that guarantee.

1.5 min. The "you in eight months" framing lands harder than "reproducibility" as an abstraction. If anyone pushes back with "Excel has audit trails", concede the point and note it does not survive a copy-paste.

Key Concept: Tidy Data

A dataset is tidy when: (1) each variable is a column, (2) each observation is a row, and (3) each type of observational unit is its own table. (Wickham 2014)

The Big Mac Index is tidy: one row per country-date, one column per measured variable — local_price, dollar_ex, dollar_price.

Tidy is not an aesthetic preference. It is the shape pandas, R and every modern tool are optimised for. If your data is not tidy, reshaping it is your first task — not optional cleanup.

1.5 min. Wickham's paper is short and readable; recommend it to anyone who asks for extra reading in week one. This concept is load-bearing for Topics 2-4.

Selection: .loc vs .iloc

# .loc  — LABEL-based: select by name, or by boolean condition
swiss = latest.loc[latest["name"] == "Switzerland", "dollar_price"]

# .iloc — POSITION-based: select by integer position
latest.iloc[:3, :5]

# boolean filtering: which burgers cost more than $7?
expensive = latest.loc[latest["dollar_price"] > 7, ["name", "dollar_price"]]

The rule to memorise: .loc takes labels. .iloc takes positions. Almost every pandas error a beginner hits is one of these used where the other belonged.

2 min. If you have a live Colab open, run these and have students predict each output before you hit shift-enter. Prediction-then-check is worth three times the passive read.

Aggregation and Derived Columns

df["year"]  = df["date"].dt.year
yearly_avg  = df.groupby("year")["dollar_price"].mean()

df_us = df.loc[df["iso_a3"] == "USA"].sort_values("date").copy()
df_us["pct_change"] = df_us["dollar_price"].pct_change() * 100

groupby is split–apply–combine: split the rows into groups, apply a function to each group, combine the answers into one object. It is the single most-used verb in applied work.

1.5 min. Say split-apply-combine out loud and write it on the board. Students who hold that three-word phrase never get lost in groupby syntax.

Run These Before Anything Else

df.info()      # columns, dtypes, non-null counts.  Fewer non-nulls than rows = missing data
df.dtypes      # are prices numbers, or strings that merely look like numbers?
df.describe()  # min/max outliers, impossible negatives, suspicious zeros
df.groupby("date")["name"].nunique()   # if min << max, the panel is unbalanced

This is the economist's equivalent of checking your instruments before an experiment. Three minutes here saves hours downstream. Make it reflexive — every dataset, every time, before a line of analysis.

1.5 min. The last line is the one that pays off at the end of the hour — it is how you discover the panel is unbalanced, which is how you discover Russia is gone.

Reshaping: melt() and pivot_table()

Real economic data rarely arrives tidy. Statistical agencies publish wide tables — countries as columns, years as rows — with nested headers and footnotes embedded as data.

df_wide = df.pivot_table(index="name", columns="date",
                         values="dollar_price", aggfunc="first")     # long -> wide

df_long = (df_wide.reset_index()
                  .melt(id_vars="name", var_name="date", value_name="dollar_price")
                  .dropna(subset=["dollar_price"]))                  # wide -> long (tidy)

pivot_table() spreads a variable across columns — good for display and heatmaps. melt() gathers columns back into rows — required for analysis. You will lean on both in Topics 2–4 with FRED, World Bank and BLS series, all of which ship wide.

1.5 min. Worth saying: the heatmap you will see in ten minutes is a pivot_table. The reshape is not busywork; it is what makes the picture possible.

Common Misconception

“I can just analyse the data in whatever shape I receive it.”

Try groupby("date") on a frame whose dates are column headers. It fails — there is no date column to group on. Wide format breaks most pandas operations, silently or loudly.

When your groupby or merge is not working, the first question is always: is my data tidy?

1 min. This is the single most common help-hours question in weeks 2-4. Planting it now saves everyone time.

Fitness for Purpose: The Pre-Flight Checklist

1

Structure. Does it match the question? To measure change you need time variation.

2

Measurement. Right level? The index is in local nominal currency. Constant dollars need deflation — Topic 2.

3

Coverage. Representative? The index covers ~54 countries where McDonald's operates and reports — excluding most of Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Asia and the Pacific.

Thirty seconds of checklist prevents an analysis built on data that cannot support its own conclusion.

1.5 min. Point 3 is the uncomfortable one: the regions the index excludes are often the ones where PPP comparisons matter most for policy. Say that.

This Pattern Is Everywhere

US Census Bureau, 2020

Withheld the standard one-year American Community Survey products — the most widely used socioeconomic dataset in the country — because non-response bias had compromised the data's integrity. The non-respondents differed systematically in income, housing and demographics.

A retailer's AI scheduler, 6,000 stores

Trained on historical staffing data that never captured the informal shift swaps managers made by hand. Store managers manually overrode 84% of the generated shifts. The algorithm was fine. The data was not.

Publishing the 2020 ACS would have misled every researcher, policymaker and analyst who relied on it. Withholding it was the honest choice — and an expensive one.

Three Mechanisms — MCAR, MAR, MNAR

MechanismMissingness depends onExampleHow bad
MCAR
missing completely at random		 nothing a server crash drops responses Benign. Less power, no bias.
MAR
missing at random		 observed variables only younger people answer less often, but within an age group answering is unrelated to salary Addressable. Condition on what you observe.
MNAR
missing not at random		 the unobserved value itself high earners refuse to state a salary because it is high Dangerous. No amount of observed data fully fixes it.

Read it aloud: MCAR costs you precision. MAR costs you a modelling step. MNAR costs you the ability to know how wrong you are.

2.5 min. The bottom line is the one to repeat twice. Anticipated question: "can't we just impute?" — yes, and imputation assumes MAR; under MNAR it can increase bias while making the output look cleaner. Topic 6 comes back to this as selection bias.

Classify These Three

A country's early rows are absent because The Economist only began surveying it in 2011. → MCAR with respect to valuation — the start date has nothing to do with the price.

Venezuela's gaps during hyperinflation, when a dollar price was not meaningfully measurable. → MNAR — the gap is caused by the extreme value it would have held.

Russia, after February 2022. → MNAR — the cause of the gap is the thing the data measures.

You can never see the missing values. You can always reason about how the data came to be — and that reasoning is the job.

2 min. Take each one cold before revealing the fragment. The Venezuela case is the hardest and the most useful: the value is missing precisely because it was extreme, which is the cleanest MNAR intuition students will get.

Poll: Name the Mechanism

Back to the ONS. Workers with two jobs and long hours are the least likely to answer a labour force survey — and they are also the workers whose hours and earnings the survey is trying to measure.

Which mechanism is that?

A) MCAR — missing completely at random
B) MAR — missing at random
C) MNAR — missing not at random
D) Cannot be classified without seeing the missing values
1 min. This is Poll 1 again with a name attached. Watch whether the B share is large — if so, spend the extra thirty seconds on the "observed vs unobserved" distinction in the reveal.

Answer: C — MNAR

The probability of a response depends on the very quantity the survey measures. That is the definition, and it is why the regulator downgraded the series rather than correcting it — the ONS could acknowledge the risk but could not quantify the bias.

Why B is the near miss: if “works two jobs” were an observed variable you could condition on it and be in MAR territory. But these people never answered, so it is not observed for them. Why D is tempting and wrong: you can never see the missing values — yet you can always reason about the mechanism from how the data came to be.

Three Strategies, In Order of Intellectual Honesty

1

Flag and document (preferred). Record which observations are missing and why; keep a missingness_note column. The gap carries information — do not erase it.

2

Forward-fill (cautious). Carry the last observed value forward. Defensible for slow-moving variables like annual GDP. Never for exchange rates or prices.

3

Drop and document (simplest). Acceptable only if you can demonstrate MCAR — which requires testing, not assertion.

And the fourth thing people actually do, which is not on the list: impute silently. Imputation assumes MAR. Under MNAR it can increase bias while making the output look cleaner.

2 min. "Intellectual honesty" is the ordering principle, not convenience — say that. Topic 6 formalises the fourth item as selection bias.

Survivorship Bias — MNAR's Cousin

March 2026: Anthropic published its AI Economic Index, tracking how users interacted with Claude across task types. High-tenure users looked disproportionately successful at complex, high-value tasks.

But new users who failed to get value abandoned the tool and vanished from the active panel. Analysing only the survivors dramatically overstated the productivity gain for the general population.

Mutual fund returns. Failed funds are liquidated and leave the index, so the surviving average looks better than the experience of investing.

Clinical trial dropout. Patients with side effects leave the study, so the treatment looks safer than it is.

Reproducibility Is Not a Luxury

Whenever a computation involves randomness — sampling, splitting, bootstrapping — set random_state=42. It is this course's convention and it appears in every lab.

Brodeur, Mikola & Cook (2024) tried to reproduce 110 papers from leading economics and political science journals across 5,511 re-analyses:

25%

~70%

The gap between “the code runs” and “the conclusions hold” is wide. That gap is your job.

Key Takeaways

Data is built, not given. Measurement decisions cap what you can ever learn from a dataset.

Three structures, three capabilities. Cross-sections compare, time series track change, panels do both. Structure beats size.

PPP is a theory you can compute. Implied PPP = local price / US price; valuation is the distance from the market rate. Switzerland, Jan 2025: +38.0%.

Missing data is information. MCAR costs precision, MAR costs a modelling step, MNAR costs you knowing how wrong you are.
