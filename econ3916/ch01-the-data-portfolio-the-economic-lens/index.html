The Illusion of Infrastructure

You drive over a suspension bridge without wading into the water to check that the pylons reach bedrock. Infrastructure hands you a binary sense of security — it holds, or it doesn't. Economic data has no such bedrock. Its beams are best guesses, and some are missing entirely.

Every number you meet is what survives three subjective decisions:

1

What to measure. Which concept counts as “unemployed”?

2

How to measure it. Phone survey, admin record, scraped price?

3

What to ignore entirely. Who never got counted at all?

Decision 3 leaves no trace. Nothing in the file tells you what was thrown away before it was written — which is why treating the result as solid steel is the dangerous move.

Wayfair: Did the Ad Cause the Sofa?

Wayfair's Long Term Impact Science team exists to answer one question: did this ad campaign cause the purchase, or was the customer going to buy the sofa anyway?

Standard ML — an engine of correlation

bought a sofa + clicked the ad → pattern found. But the algorithm may simply be very good at targeting people who already had a sofa in the cart. Result: millions burned showing ads to people who had already made up their minds.

Causal ML — an engine of causation

Ad exposure isolated by a randomised controlled trial or a natural experiment → the trigger is mathematically identified. It forces the data to answer a definitive question:

If the ad had never existed, would the sale still have happened?

Same data. Different structure. That difference is a design problem, not a modelling problem.

A Country Stopped Publishing Its Own Unemployment Rate

October 2023. The UK's Office for National Statistics did something a national statistical institute almost never does. It stopped publishing the country's headline labour market statistics.

The Labour Force Survey is the source of the unemployment rate, wage growth, and the numbers the Bank of England sets interest rates on. Response rates had fallen so far that the ONS judged its own estimates unfit to publish.

A replacement returned in February 2024 — carrying the label “official statistics in development”. A formal downgrade from accredited national statistics, and an unusually public admission that the numbers were provisional.

Answer: B — Who Is Missing, Not How Many

Noise is an unsteady hand. Throw enough darts and the errors cancel — the average lands on the bullseye. That is what a bigger sample fixes.

Bias is someone having moved the board while you were blindfolded. Throw a million darts and every one still lands in the same wrong place.

The people who stopped answering were not a random fifth of the country. Large samples fix noise. They do nothing for bias.

The Three Structures, Named

Cross-sectional data — many units, observed at a single point in time.

Time series data — a single unit, observed across many time periods.

Panel data (also: longitudinal) — the same units, observed across many time periods.

The word doing the work in the third one is “same”. A survey repeated every year with different respondents each round is a pooled cross-section, not a panel.

Common Misconception

“More data always means better analysis.”

A million rows of cross-sectional data cannot answer a question that requires time variation. Adding more units to a single frozen instant does not conjure time variation out of thin air.

Structure beats size. A well-built panel of 57 countries over 25 years is more powerful than a cross-section of 10,000 countries at one moment — because only the panel lets you hold the unit fixed and watch it move.

Purchasing Power Parity, In One Burger

A Big Mac costs $5.79 in the United States and CHF 7.20 in Switzerland. If markets were frictionless — no tariffs, no shipping, no local wage gaps — one identical good should cost the same everywhere.

The rate that equalises them: 7.20 / 5.79 = 1.24

The actual market rate in January 2025 was about 0.90 francs per dollar. The franc buys more burger than it “should”. It is overvalued.

That is the whole theory: exchange rates should adjust until identical goods cost the same everywhere. The Economist built the index on it in 1986. Not a precision instrument — but the data is free, the arithmetic is transparent, and the intuition is immediate.

2 min.

Resist naming the formula until the next slide.

  • The arithmetic first, the notation second — students who see 7.20/5.79 before they see P_domestic/P_US never find the formula intimidating.

Two Equations, and What They Say Out Loud

\[ \text{Implied PPP} = \frac{P_{\text{domestic}}}{P_{\text{US}}} \qquad\qquad \text{Valuation}\,(\%) = \frac{\text{Implied PPP} - \text{Spot}}{\text{Spot}} \times 100 \]

The first one asks

What exchange rate would make these two burgers cost the same?

The second one asks

How far is the real world from that rate, in percent?

Sign convention: positive means the local currency is overvalued — the burger is dear relative to what exchange rates would predict. Negative means undervalued — the burger is cheap. \( P_{\text{domestic}} \) is in local currency; Spot is local currency per US dollar.

2 min.

Every symbol gets glossed on this slide — do not move on until the sign convention is said out loud, because it is the thing Poll 3 checks and the thing students get backwards on the exam.

Do It By Hand First: Switzerland, January 2025

\[ \frac{7.20}{5.79} = 1.2435 \]

\[ \frac{1.2435 - 0.9009}{0.9009} \times 100 = +38.0\% \]

The franc is 38% overvalued on this measure. Swiss burgers are dear because Swiss rents, wages and service costs are high — a hint we pick up in ten minutes.

Where the inputs come from: CHF 7.20 and $5.79 are the January 2025 rows of local_price and the US dollar_price. The 0.9009 is dollar_ex — francs per dollar, the actual market rate that period. Nothing here is a modelling choice; all three are columns.

  • Phones out is fine.
  • Only after they have a number do you show that pandas does the same arithmetic on 54 rows at once.
  • Verified: spot 0.9009 on 2025-01-01, valuation +38.04%.

Answer: C — It Is a Statement About Exchange Rates

The valuation compares the actual spot rate with the implied PPP rate. It is not a claim about inflation, and it is not a forecast.

Why A is tempting: the raw index is the price gap — Indonesia's Big Mac was $2.54 against $5.79, and $2.54/$5.79 − 1 = −56.2%, the same number, not a second one. What A gets wrong is produce: the index compares posted prices, and the reason the gap survives is that most of a burger is non-tradeable — rent, wages, local beef. That is the next slide. B reverses the direction and turns a deviation into a forecast.

Key Concept: Tidy Data

A dataset is tidy when: (1) each variable is a column, (2) each observation is a row, and (3) each type of observational unit is its own table. (Wickham 2014)

Statistical agencies publish wide: one column per year since 1920, nested headers, merged cells, footnotes inside data cells. It reads well and scripts on it fail.

Tidy is not an aesthetic preference — it is the shape every modern tool is optimised for. If your data is not tidy, reshaping it is your first, unglamorous job.

1.5 min.

Wickham's paper is short and readable; recommend it to anyone who asks for extra reading in week one.

Selection: .loc vs .iloc

# .loc  — LABEL-based: select by name, or by boolean condition
swiss = latest.loc[latest["name"] == "Switzerland", "dollar_price"]

# .iloc — POSITION-based: select by integer position
latest.iloc[:3, :5]

# boolean filtering: which burgers cost more than $7?

The rule to memorise: .loc takes labels. .iloc takes positions.

Three Mechanisms — MCAR, MAR, MNAR

MechanismMissingness depends onExampleHow bad
MCAR
missing completely at random		 nothing a server crash drops responses Benign. Less power, no bias.
MAR
missing at random		 observed variables only younger people answer less often, but within an age group answering is unrelated to salary Addressable. Condition on what you observe.
MNAR
missing not at random		 the unobserved value itself high earners refuse to state a salary because it is high Dangerous. No amount of observed data fully fixes it.

Read it aloud: MCAR costs you precision. MAR costs you a modelling step. MNAR costs you the ability to know how wrong you are — because the variable is hiding itself.

  • Anticipated question: "can't we just impute?" — yes, and imputation assumes MAR; under MNAR it can increase bias while making the output look cleaner.

Classify These Three

A country's early rows are absent because The Economist only began surveying it in 2011. → MCAR with respect to valuation — the start date has nothing to do with the price.

Russia, after February 2022. → MNAR — the cause of the gap is the thing the data measures.

You can never see the missing values. You can always reason about how the data came to be — and that reasoning is the job.

Three Strategies, In Order of Intellectual Honesty

1

Flag and document (preferred). Record which observations are missing and why; keep a missingness_note column. The gap carries information — do not erase it.

2

Forward-fill (cautious). Carry the last observed value forward. Defensible for slow-moving variables like annual GDP. Never for exchange rates or prices.

3

Drop and document (simplest). Acceptable only if you can demonstrate MCAR — which requires testing, not assertion.

The fourth thing people actually do, which is not on the list: impute silently — the deadliest sin.

It assumes MAR. Under MNAR it increases bias — and removes the one visible clue that anything was wrong.
