Three Words People Use Interchangeably

Sampling frame — the list you actually draw from. The operational answer to “who could possibly end up in my dataset?”

The gap between the first two is where bias lives — and no amount of extra data closes it.

What Buys the Guarantee

Probability sampling, and the three designs you will actually meet

Transition. "We have diagnosed the disease. Now the cure — and it is a property of the procedure, not of the dataset."

What Makes a Sample a “Probability Sample”

Every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of being selected.

That single property is what makes unbiased estimation possible. Note what it is not about:

 It is not about the sample being big.

 It is not about the sample feeling random or arbitrary.

 It is about the selection probabilities being quantities you know.

2 min. The "feeling random" line is worth pressing on. Whoever happens to walk past your table on Huntington Avenue feels arbitrary, and it is not a probability sample — you cannot write down anyone's chance of being there. Arbitrary is not the same as random.

Simple Random Sampling, and What “Unbiased” Means

Every unit has the same chance $\pi_i = n/N$ of being picked. Then:

$$E[\bar{y}] = \bar{Y}$$

$\bar{y}$ — the mean you compute from your $n$ sampled units

$\bar{Y}$ — the true mean over all $N$ units in the population

$E[\cdot]$ — “the average value of, over many repeats”

In plain English: any one sample will miss. But repeat the whole exercise over and over and the sample means centre exactly on the truth — no systematic push in either direction. That is all “unbiased” means, and it is a property of the procedure, not of the one dataset on your laptop.

3 min. The last clause is the one students need and rarely get: unbiasedness is not a certificate you can inspect your data for. You cannot look at a spreadsheet and see whether it is unbiased. You can only ask how it was collected. That is why "how was this collected?" is the first question in every data job you will ever have.

Two Designs That Beat Plain Random

Stratified sampling — sort the population into groups, then sample randomly inside each group.

Like sorting a bag of mixed candy by colour and grabbing a random handful of each. Every flavour is guaranteed to show up.

Cluster sampling — sample whole groups at once, all-or-nothing.

Cheaper when the population is spread across a map. Riskier: neighbours resemble each other, so a cluster carries less information than the same count of scattered individuals.

Why stratified usually wins. Plain random sampling represents every subgroup in expectation. Stratified represents it in this particular sample. When your sample is small or a subgroup is rare, that gap is the whole ballgame.

3 min. Anchor it: the unemployment rate on the news comes from the Current Population Survey, a stratified multistage design over roughly 60,000 households. Sixty thousand, for a country of 330 million, accurate to a couple of tenths of a point. The design is doing that, not the size. This slide is also where stratify=y gets planted — say "hold onto stratified, it comes back as one keyword argument in forty minutes."

The Three Designs, Side by Side

Same population, same number of units selected. Three different guarantees.

2 min. Point at the stratified panel: notice the picks are spread evenly down the page by construction. Then the cluster panel: if the top-left block happens to be a wealthy neighbourhood, you have bought nine observations that all say the same thing. Nine correlated observations are worth much less than nine independent ones — which is the Chapter 5 independence assumption showing up in survey design.

Poll: Which Ingredient Shrinks?

An estimate’s error has three ingredients: how bent the selection is, how variable the outcome is, and how much of the population you missed. You collect ten times more data from the same source. Which shrinks?

A) All three
B) How bent the selection is
C) How much you missed — and only that one
D) None — the error is fixed
1 min. Click to vote. This poll is deliberately placed BEFORE the equation that answers it — they should reason it out in words first, so the algebra on the next section arrives as confirmation rather than as a new thing to memorise.

Answer: C — Only the Third

More data from the same pipe closes the “how much did I miss” gap and touches nothing else. How bent the selection is is a property of the mechanism; how variable the outcome is is a property of the world. Neither knows or cares how many rows you have.

Why A is tempting but wrong: “more data helps” is true and load-bearing everywhere else in this course. It is exactly one term of three, and this lecture exists to fence it off.

2 min. The image to leave them with: pouring more water through a bent pipe does not straighten the pipe. Then hand off to the next section — "there is an equation that says precisely this, and it fits on one line."

One Condition, Four Costumes

Selection, survivorship, nonresponse, self-selection — and the single line underneath all four

Transition. Promise the collapse explicitly: "you were probably taught these as a list of four things to memorise. By the end of this section it is one thing."

The Condition Underneath Everything

Selection bias means the chance of showing up in your data is correlated with the thing you are trying to measure:

$$\text{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$$

$R_i$ — a switch: 1 if unit $i$ made it into your data, 0 if not

$Y_i$ — the outcome you care about for unit $i$

$\text{Cov}$ — do these two move together?

In plain English: if being in the dataset and what the answer would have been travel together at all, your sample mean sits off the population mean — and it stays off no matter how many rows you add.

3 min. Emphasise "at all." This is not a threshold condition where a little correlation is fine. It is a switch: zero, or not zero. The hook's −0.005 was not zero. Also flag the notation debt: R for "recorded", and it is the same R that shows up as a missingness indicator in Chapter 1. That is not a coincidence and the next slides make it explicit.

Meng’s Identity: Error as a Product of Three

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N \;=\; \rho_{R,Y} \;\times\; \sigma_Y \;\times\; \sqrt{\frac{N-n}{n}}$$

$\rho_{R,Y}$

how bent the selection mechanism is

More data: no help.
Property of the mechanism.

$\sigma_Y$

how spread out the outcome is

More data: no help.
Property of the world.

$\sqrt{(N-n)/n}$

how much of the population you missed

More data: yes.
The only one.

In plain English: your error is a product. Random sampling drives the first term to zero, the whole product collapses to zero, and the third term does all the work — that is the happy Topic 5 story. If the first term is not zero, shrinking only the third factor cannot save you.

4 min. Write "error = bent x spread x missed" on the board in words before you touch the symbols. Then ask the poll question again verbally: which one does more data touch? They just voted C, so they will answer in chorus. The multiplication is the whole insight — a product with a non-zero factor cannot be driven to zero by shrinking a different factor.

The Big Data Paradox

For a fixed sample size, the error scales with $\sqrt{N/n}$ — with the size of the population, not just the sample.

So the bigger the world you are trying to describe, the more a small bias costs you. A bent mechanism that is harmless when you are describing a village is catastrophic when you are describing a country.

In the age of big data, quality beats quantity — and it is not close.

This is the result behind the hook: 2.3 million responses reduced to the worth of 400, by a correlation of −0.005, because $N$ was the whole American electorate.

3 min. Anticipate the objection, because a sharp student will raise it: "so is big data useless?" No — big data is superb for prediction, for pattern-finding, for anything where you do not need to generalise to a population you did not observe. It is dangerous exactly when you use it to estimate a population quantity. That distinction comes back in Topic 9 when we compare click logs to A/B tests.

Costume 1: Survivorship Bias

Your data is missing the units that exited — funds that closed, firms that went bankrupt, patients who died, students who dropped out.

Backtest a momentum strategy on today’s Nasdaq 100 members and it looks brilliant. Put the dot-com casualties back in and returns more than halve while maximum drawdowns roughly double.

A 2024 study found that ignoring survivorship overstated median mutual fund alpha by 0.60% a year.

Think like an economist. Before accepting any performance number — fund returns, startup success rates, hospital quality scores, graduation rates — ask: who is missing, and did they leave because of the outcome? If yes, that is $\text{Cov}(R,Y) \neq 0$, and it is Chapter 1’s MNAR wearing a finance suit.

3 min. The classroom version that lands: "has anyone seen the statistic that 90% of startups fail? Where would that number have to come from?" Somebody has to have found the failed companies, and failed companies do not file surveys. Then the MNAR link explicitly: a fund's returns are missing BECAUSE the returns were catastrophic. Missingness caused by the value itself. Same equation.

Topic 1’s Framework, Doing Real Work

Term The nonresponse version Can you fix it?
MCAR Who answers is pure chance Nothing to fix. You just have a smaller sample.
MAR Answering depends on things you can see — age, ZIP, gender Yes. Reweight the groups you can observe.
MNAR Answering depends on the answer itself Not fully. You would have to model why people stayed silent.

Common misconception: “just weight the sample to match the census.” Weighting fixes MAR. It cannot fix MNAR — the young people who did answer may not represent young people at all. Weighting corrects the group proportions and leaves the within-group distortion exactly where it was.

3 min. This table is the diagnostic students will actually reuse, so slow down. The misconception box is the highest-value 30 seconds in the section: weighting is what every polling firm does, it genuinely helps, and it is not a solution. It moves you from "wrong" to "less wrong by an unknown amount."

Economist at Work: The Algorithm That Encoded Race Without Using Race

Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli & Mullainathan (Science, 2019) studied a healthcare algorithm affecting roughly 200 million patients a year. It selected patients for extra care using predicted future healthcare cost as a stand-in for health need.

Black patients had historically had less access to care — so they generated lower costs at the same level of illness. Cost was a bent proxy for need.

26.3%

more active chronic conditions at the same risk score

17.7% → 46.5%

share of flagged patients who were Black, actual vs corrected

84%

of the bias removed by swapping the proxy

Race was never an input. The proxy carried it.

4 min. Open with the question, not the answer: "the algorithm never used race as a variable. How can it be racially biased?" Let them work it. The chain is cost proxies for need, cost also tracks access, access tracks race. Then the general lesson: whenever a model selects who gets something, the selected set is a SAMPLE, and the selection rule is the sampling mechanism. If that rule runs on a bent proxy, you have Cov(R,Y) != 0 with real people on the other end of it.

Four Costumes, One Condition

Selection

The frame excluded people whose answers differ.

Survivorship

They left, because of the outcome.

Nonresponse

They were asked and stayed silent, because of the outcome.

Self-selection

They walked in on their own, because of the outcome.

$$\text{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$$

Different stories about how the switch got flipped. Identical damage.

2 min. This is the promised collapse — call it in. The practical payoff: they no longer need to remember four definitions for the exam, they need one question. "Who is missing, and is their absence related to the answer?" That question generates all four names on demand.

One Hundred Runs: Precise, and Wrong Every Time

Biased, n ≈ 4.38M: mean error +8.30 pts, sd 0.01  •  Random, n = 50,000: mean error −0.02 pts, sd 0.20

3 min. THIS is the lecture. The biased estimator is about twenty times more PRECISE than the random one and wrong by 8.3 points on every single run. The Law of Large Numbers did its job perfectly — it converged. It converged on the wrong number, and the more data it got the more certain it became. Now cash the Topic 4 retrieval question: this is the tight cluster two inches off the bullseye, and if a student answered that at minute 3, credit them by name.

Poll: Why Is Stability Bad News?

Across 100 runs the biased estimate’s errors have a standard deviation of 0.01 points; the random estimate’s is 0.20. The biased one is twenty times more stable. Why is that bad news?

A) It is good news — stable estimates are what we want
B) Stability only reflects the bigger sample; it says nothing about being right
C) The random sample must have a bug

1 min. Click to vote. Expect a real A share even now — "it reproduces" is the everyday test people use for whether a finding is solid, and it is about to fail them. A large A vote here is the honest starting point, not an embarrassment.

Answer: B — Precision and Accuracy Are Different Axes

The biased estimate is stable because 4.38 million observations pin down its own answer very tightly. Its answer is wrong. Worse: that tightness would show up as a narrow confidence interval — so the very mechanism producing the error also produces false reassurance about it.

Why A is tempting but wrong: a number that reproduces across reruns is exactly what people cite as evidence a finding is solid. Reproducibility tests your code. It cannot test your frame.

2 min. Seed Topic 7 here: next week you build confidence intervals by bootstrapping, and this slide is the caveat that comes with them. A bootstrap resamples your data, so a narrow bootstrap interval on a biased sample is a precise measurement of the wrong quantity.

The sample your model learns from.

Stands in for the population your model will meet in deployment.

Titanic: One Keyword Argument

891 passengers, 342 survived — an overall rate of 0.384. Survival was wildly unequal: 63% in 1st class, 47% in 2nd, 24% in 3rd. Dropping rows with a missing Age leaves 714 passengers at a 0.406 rate.

Split Train rate Test rate Test deviation
train_test_split(X, y) 0.410 0.392 −1.46 pts
... , stratify=y) 0.406 0.406 −0.06 pts

One honest caveat. stratify=y balances survival, not passenger class. The stratified test set’s class shares came out closer to the population too — but that is a by-product, not a promise. If you need class balanced, stratify on it.

3 min. Live-code this if the room is with you; the whole thing is two calls. The caveat box is there because the lab's own interpretation question asks students to check whether stratify=y preserved Pclass, and the honest answer is "it did here, and it did not have to." Do not let them generalise from one lucky seed.

A 1.46-Point Hole, Closed to 0.06

It matters most when the class is rare: 2% fraud, 5% defaults, 1% click-through. A naive split can hand you a test set with almost no positive cases at all.

2 min. Titanic at 40% survival is a forgiving case — and it still lost 1.46 points. Ask them to picture the same split on a fraud dataset at 2%: an 80/20 split of 1,000 rows leaves 200 test rows and roughly 4 fraud cases. Your recall metric is then computed on four observations, and it will swing wildly with the seed.

Data Leakage: Three Ways It Gets In

Leakage is information flowing from the test set into training. It is a survey where the pollster already knows the answers.

1. Preprocessing leakage — fitting a scaler or imputer on all the data before splitting.

2. Feature leakage — using a variable that is a consequence of the outcome. Predicting loan default from total_payments: defaulted loans have fewer payments by definition.

3. Temporal leakage — training on 2020–2024 to predict 2019.

Why it belongs in this lecture: leakage breaks the independence of train and test. Topic 5 taught you that independence of draws is what makes the LLN work. Break it and the convergence guarantee dissolves — again.

3 min. Feature leakage is the one that ends careers, so dwell on 2. It produces a model with 99% accuracy that a manager will love and that collapses instantly in production, because the magic column simply does not exist at prediction time. The diagnostic to teach: for every feature, ask "would I actually have this value at the moment I need to make the prediction?"

If you scale then split, your training set is not quite centred, because the scaler was tuned to a dataset containing rows the model is not allowed to see. Topic 6 in a Nutshell

Design, not size, decides whether an estimate is trustworthy. A probability sample buys unbiasedness. A non-probability sample buys nothing, at any scale.

One condition, four costumes. Selection, survivorship, nonresponse and self-selection are all $\text{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$.

Error is a product of three terms — bent, spread, missed — and more data shrinks only the last one.

MCAR / MAR / MNAR is the diagnostic: no bias, correctable by weighting, or genuinely hard.

In ML the training data IS the sample. The split is a sampling design, stratify=y is stratified sampling, and leakage is the violation of sample independence.

1 min. Close on the sentence from the very first slide: sample size cannot cure sample bias. Then the one question that generates the entire lecture on demand — "who is missing, and is their absence related to the answer?"
