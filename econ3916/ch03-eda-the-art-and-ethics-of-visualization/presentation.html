Poll: Same Statistics, Same Data?

A) Essentially identical — those three pin the data down
B) Similar in shape, different only in a few outliers

1 min. Students vote on their devices; the bar chart is on the next slide. A and B are the productive errors — almost everyone has been taught these three statistics as if they were a summary of the data rather than three of its many properties. Do not correct anyone yet.

Answer: C — Completely Different

Reading the Four Panels

I — the textbook case

A linear cloud. The fitted line is the right model and the correlation means what you think it means.

II — a parabola

Deterministic curvature. \(r = 0.816\) and the linear model is simply the wrong functional form.

III — one outlier

Ten points on a perfect line, one off it. That single point tilts the slope away from the pattern the other ten agree on.

IV — one point does all the work

Every x is 8 except one. Delete that observation and the slope is undefined. The fit rests on n = 1.

Three of the four would send you to a different Chapter of this course.

Why This Bites an Economist

1,000 households. Income and consumption spending. The correlation comes back at \(r = 0.72\) — strong, positive, apparently one relationship.

Low-income cluster. Steep slope. Every additional dollar of income is spent.

High-income cluster. Flat slope. Most of an additional dollar is saved.

Section 2

Tufte: Two Numbers for a Chart

Is it efficient? Is it honest? Both have formulas.

The Data-Ink Ratio

$$\text{Data-Ink Ratio} = \frac{\text{Data Ink}}{\text{Total Ink Used in Graphic}}$$

In plain language: of all the ink on the page, what fraction is doing work?

Data ink — the marks that encode numbers. Bars, points, lines.

Everything else — grid, borders, fills, drop shadows, 3-D extrusion, background images.

Runs from 0 (all decoration) to 1 (all data). Tufte's prescription is subtractive: erase non-data ink, then erase redundant data ink.

2 min. Read the fraction aloud as a sentence before anyone tries to compute it — it is a way of seeing, not an exam quantity. Nobody measures ink with a ruler. The value is that it turns "this looks cluttered" into "which of these marks encodes a number?"

Five Numbers, Twice

Both panels carry the same five values and both start at zero. Only the ink differs.

2 min. Ask the room to name the decorations on the left one at a time — hatching, drop shadow, heavy frame, colored ground, and a different color per bar that encodes nothing the labels do not already say. Then ask what was lost on the right. Nothing was.

The Lie Factor

$$\text{Lie Factor} = \frac{\text{Size of effect shown in the graphic}}{\text{Size of effect in the data}}$$

In plain language: how many times bigger is the picture than the fact?

1.0

honest

> 1.5

meaningful exaggeration

> 5.0

serious distortion

< 1.0

understating — also a distortion

2 min. The fourth box is the one students forget. A chart that makes a real crisis look like noise is distorting exactly as much as one that inflates a rounding error — it just serves a different interest.

Section 3

Seven Ways a Chart Lies

Name the mechanism, not the feeling — and learn the tell for each

Three That Distort the Geometry

1. Truncated axis

The baseline moves off zero and small differences fill the frame.

Tell: read the bottom tick before you read the bars.

2. Dual y-axes

Two series, two independent scales, and the author chooses both. Any correlation can be manufactured or erased.

Tell: two axes in different units with no stated rule for aligning them.

3. Area and volume encoding

Double the radius and the area quadruples; in three dimensions the volume goes up eightfold. Bubble charts are the usual offender.

Tell: ask whether the value is the radius or the area. It is almost never stated.

3 min. The dual-axis one is worth a beat: there is no correct alignment of two independent scales, which means the author's choice IS the claim. That is why the participation chart earlier used a common base instead.

Why Bubbles Betray You

Cleveland & McGill (1984) ranked visual encodings by how accurately people read them. Position beats length; length beats area; area beats color. The ranking is empirical, not aesthetic.

2 min. Cleveland and McGill matter here: this is not Tufte's taste, it is measured human perception. People systematically under-read area, which means an area-encoded doubling gets read as somewhere between a doubling and a quadrupling depending on the reader — the chart does not even have one meaning.

Three That Distort the Frame

4. Cherry-picked time window

Start the series after the crash and the crash is gone. The window is a framing decision and it is rarely disclosed.

Tell: ask what happened immediately before the first data point.

5. Metric mislabeling

The geometry is fine. The axis label describes a different variable than the one plotted.

Tell: check the axis name against the variable's actual definition.

6. Missing context

No uncertainty band implies a precision the data may not support. A spike with no note that last month was revised is not false, and it is not honest either.

Tell: ask what is not on the chart.

3 min. On 6, seed forward: you start adding uncertainty bands to your own estimates with bootstrap intervals in Chapter 7 and prediction intervals in Chapter 12. Until then, "no band" is a limitation to state rather than a style choice.

May 2025: A Chart With Perfect Geometry

The Financial Times published a chart titled “Hires per month”, tracking employment at the top 15 U.S. AI companies. It was widely shared.

The line dropped below zero.

Which is impossible. You cannot hire a negative number of people.

The series was net employment change — hires minus departures. Labelling a net flow as a gross flow turned a structural contraction into an apparent hiring slowdown.

2 min. Land the distinction: no axis was truncated, no area was misused, the arithmetic was right. The chart lied in the label. This is the mechanism most likely to survive a careful reader's check, because careful readers check the geometry.

Think Like an Economist

Every chart makes three decisions. Interrogate all three — including when you drew it.

What to show

Which variables? Which time window? Which units? Levels or changes?

How to encode

Position, length, area, color? Linear or log? One axis or two?

What to omit

Missing data? Uncertainty bands? Context variables? Revisions?

The chart is not a window into the data. It is an argument.

When Is Truncation Acceptable?

Not automatically dishonest. Body temperature in Fahrenheit lives in 97–104. A policy rate lives in 0–10%. Starting at zero would spend most of the chart on a region with no data.

The test. Does the truncation make a small change look large? If the change is genuinely large within the relevant range, truncating is informative. If it is small and the chart makes it look dramatic, the chart is misleading.

The practical rule. If the Lie Factor is above 2.0, annotate the chart: “Note: y-axis does not start at zero.”

Disclosure is the floor, not the ceiling.

2 min. Tie back to Discussion 1 question 3 explicitly — several pairs will have argued that the footnote settles it. It does not. It moves the chart from deceptive to merely misleading, which is progress and not absolution.

Common Misconception

“Log scales are dishonest.”

Linear shows absolute change

A $10 rise looks the same going from $20 to $30 as from $100 to $110.

Log shows proportional change

A 50% rise looks the same wherever it starts. Constant growth becomes a straight line.

For GDP, stock indices and population — variables spanning orders of magnitude — log is more honest than linear. For a series in a narrow range, linear carries more information.

The choice follows the question, not a rule.

2 min. Foreshadow Chapter 20: the linear-versus-log choice becomes a modelling decision there, when you decide whether to model levels or log-returns. Today it is an ethics question; there it becomes a specification.

Section 5

The EDA Workflow

You cannot redesign a chart that does not exist yet

Four Steps, In Order

Tukey (1977) called it detective work: pursue clues, form hypotheses, let formal analysis test them later.

Step Question Tools
1. StructureHow many rows, columns, types? Is it tidy?df.shape, df.dtypes, df.head()
2. DistributionsNormal, skewed, bimodal? Long tail?df.hist(), sns.kdeplot()
3. RelationshipsWhat correlates with what? Any curvature?sns.pairplot(), df.corr()
4. AnomaliesOutliers? Impossible values? Duplicates? Holes?df.describe(), box plots, df.duplicated()

Run this before every model in this course — OLS in Chapter 12, random forests in Chapter 19.

2 min. Emphasise the order. Structure before distributions because you cannot read a histogram of a column whose dtype is object. Distributions before relationships because a pair plot of two skewed variables is unreadable. Anomalies last because you need the first three to know what counts as anomalous.

Two Views of One Variable

Histogram

Bins the range and counts. Concrete, intuitive, good at skew, gaps and multiple peaks.

Its tuning knob: bin width. Too few bins loses detail; too many and noise wins.

Kernel density estimate

Places a small Gaussian at every observation and sums them. A smooth estimate of the density.

Its tuning knob: bandwidth. Narrow is noisy; wide can smooth two modes into one.

Plot both. The histogram gives you concrete counts, the KDE gives you the shape. Neither knob has a universally correct setting — they are design decisions, exactly like an axis range.

2 min. Call out the symmetry: the histogram does not escape the KDE's arbitrariness, it just hides it in the bin edges. Anyone who has nudged a bin count until the chart "looked right" has already made an undisclosed design decision.

Choosing the Knob

Sturges — matplotlib's default

$$\text{bins} = \lceil \log_2(n) + 1 \rceil$$

Built for roughly normal data. It gives too few bins for skewed economic data, which is most economic data.

Freedman–Diaconis — the better default here

$$\text{width} = 2 \times \text{IQR} \times n^{-1/3}$$

In plain language: make the bins wider when the data is spread out, and narrower when you have more of it. In seaborn: sns.histplot(data, bins="fd").

Suspect two modes and the KDE looks unimodal? Try bw_adjust=0.5.

2 min. Second designated cut slide. If you keep it, land the closing thought: every visualization parameter is a design choice, exactly like the CPI basket weights in Chapter 2. Report your choices; do not hide them.

The Mean That Describes Nobody

Mean $55,347 — sits in the valley between the two modes. It describes almost nobody in the sample.

Median $41,856 — pulled toward the larger low-income group. Better, and still blind to the second population.

Only the plot shows two populations. This is Anscombe's lesson on a single variable.

2 min. Simulated data, and say so — two normals mixed 600/400. The mechanism is what generalises, and real household income has exactly this problem with more modes and a longer right tail. Chapter 4 is where the median stops being a consolation prize and becomes a principled choice.

Anomalies: The Holes Have Structure

Chapter 1's MCAR / MAR / MNAR is an EDA question, not a theory question. Pivot the panel to countries × years and look at where the holes are.

MNAR

Newly independent states have no data before independence. The data cannot exist — the missingness is caused by the thing being measured.

Closer to MAR

Conflict years and limited statistical capacity produce intermittent gaps, predictable from observed characteristics.

If missingness correlates with the outcome, the analysis is compromised before it starts. That is selection bias, and Chapter 6 formalises it. You can see it here for free.

2 min. Practical warning for the lab: whether you SEE any missingness depends entirely on which countries you sample into the heatmap. Sample the twenty countries with the most observations and every one has all sixty-four years, so the heatmap is blank. The holes are in the countries you did not pick.

Anomalies: Values That Cannot Be

Beyond outliers, EDA catches logically impossible values — entries that violate domain constraints rather than statistical ones.

Negative GDP

a data entry error or a pipeline artifact

Age 150

an encoding error, usually a sentinel value

A percentage of 347

a unit confusion, or not a percentage at all

These hide from summary statistics. 100,000 clean rows and 50 negative-GDP rows barely move the mean — but a model trained on them learns that negative GDP is possible. Run df.describe() and read the min and max rows against your domain knowledge. It takes seconds.

1 min. Also check duplicates: df.duplicated(subset=["country","year"]). A duplicated key in a panel silently double-weights those observations in every mean, every regression and every plot downstream.

Five Takeaways

1

Summary statistics compress, and compression hides. Identical mean, variance and correlation; four different data-generating processes. Plot first.

The Data-Ink Ratio asks what fraction of your ink is doing work. Erase the rest.

The Lie Factor — visual effect over data effect — turns a hunch into a number. 49 for a truncated four-percent year. Above 2.0, annotate.

Every chart makes three decisions: what to show, how to encode, what to omit. Each one is an argument.

5

EDA is a workflow, not a chart. Structure, distributions, relationships, anomalies — before every model in this course.
