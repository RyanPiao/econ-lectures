The Same Four Datasets, Plotted

I — the textbook case

A linear cloud. The fitted line is the right model and the correlation means what you think it means.

II — a parabola

Deterministic curvature. \(r = 0.816\) and the linear model is simply the wrong functional form.

III — one outlier

Ten points on a perfect line, one off it. That single point tilts the slope away from the pattern the other ten agree on.

IV — one point does all the work

Every x is 8 except one. Delete that observation and the slope is undefined. The fit rests on n = 1.

Three of the four would send you to a different chapter of this course.

Section 2

Tufte: Two Numbers for a Chart

Is it efficient? Is it honest? Both have formulas.

The Data-Ink Ratio

Data-Ink Ratio = ink that shows data ÷ all the ink. Erase ink that encodes no number.
$$\text{Data-Ink Ratio} = \frac{\text{Data Ink}}{\text{Total Ink Used in Graphic}}$$

In plain language: of all the ink on the page, what fraction is doing work?

Runs from 0 (all decoration) to 1 (all data). Tufte's prescription is subtractive: erase non-data ink, then erase redundant data ink.

Tufte · data-ink · chartjunk

SAY: Data-Ink Ratio equals ink that shows data divided by all the ink. Erase ink that encodes no number. ⏸ 3 s.

TIME: Mon ~9:41

DON'T SAY: A high data-ink ratio means the chart is honest. (It measures efficiency; the Lie Factor measures honesty.)

BACKGROUND:

  • 2 min.
  • Read the fraction aloud as a sentence before anyone tries to compute it — it is a way of seeing, not an exam quantity.
  • Nobody measures ink with a ruler.
  • The value is that it turns "this looks cluttered" into "which of these marks encodes a number?" The eraser test (added 2026-09-16): go left to right on the red side and ask of each mark "if I erase it, is a number lost?"

IF ASKED: “Do we measure the ink?” → No. It is a way of seeing — 'if I erase this mark, is a number lost?'

The Lie Factor

Lie Factor = size of the effect shown ÷ size of the effect in the data. Honest is about 1.
$$\text{Lie Factor} = \frac{\text{Size of effect shown in the graphic}}{\text{Size of effect in the data}}$$

In plain language: how many times bigger is the picture than the fact?

1.0

honest

> 1.5

meaningful exaggeration

> 5.0

serious distortion

< 1.0

understating — also a distortion

Tufte's own tolerance is 0.95–1.05. The bands above are this course's rules of thumb, not his.

Section 3

Seven Ways a Chart Lies

Name the mechanism, not the feeling — and learn the tell for each

Three That Distort the Geometry

Three lies bend the geometry: a truncated axis, dual y-axes, and area encoding.

1. Truncated axis

The baseline moves off zero and small differences fill the frame.

Tell: read the bottom tick before you read the bars.

2. Dual y-axes

Why Bubbles Betray You

Cleveland & McGill (1984) ranked visual encodings by how accurately people read them. Position beats length; length beats area; area beats color. The ranking is empirical, not aesthetic.

2 min.

Cleveland and McGill matter here: this is not Tufte's taste, it is measured human perception.

  • People systematically under-read area, which means an area-encoded doubling gets read as somewhere between a doubling and a quadrupling depending on the reader — the chart does not even have one meaning.
  • If asked how big the second circle should be: twice the area, so the radius grows by √2 ≈ 1.41, not 2. Doubling the radius gives four times the area (a ball, eight times the volume).

When Is Truncation Acceptable?

Truncation misleads only when it makes a small change look large. Lie Factor above 2: annotate.

Four Steps, In Order

EDA runs in order: structure, distributions, relationships, anomalies — before every model.

Two Views of One Variable

Histogram

Bins the range and counts. Concrete, intuitive, good at skew, gaps and multiple peaks.

Its tuning knob: bin width. Too few bins loses detail; too many and noise wins.

Kernel density estimate

Places a small Gaussian at every observation and sums them. A smooth estimate of the density.

Its tuning knob: bandwidth. Narrow is noisy; wide can smooth two modes into one.

Plot both. The histogram gives you concrete counts, the KDE gives you the shape. Neither knob has a universally correct setting — they are design decisions, exactly like an axis range.

2 min.

Call out the symmetry: the histogram does not escape the KDE's arbitrariness, it just hides it in the bin edges.

  • Anyone who has nudged a bin count until the chart "looked right" has already made an undisclosed design decision.
  • The three curves (added 2026-09-16) are one simulated sample of 400 values - two groups, 240 and 160 - drawn at three bandwidths.
  • The analogy: bandwidth is a camera's focus ring.
  • Too narrow and every speck is sharp; too wide and two faces blur into one.

Choosing the Knob

Sturges — the classic rule (R's default)

$$\text{bins} = \lceil \log_2(n) + 1 \rceil$$

Built for roughly normal data. It gives too few bins for skewed economic data, which is most economic data.

Freedman–Diaconis — the better default here

$$\text{width} = 2 \times \text{IQR} \times n^{-1/3}$$

In plain language: make the bins wider when the data is spread out, and narrower when you have more of it. In seaborn: sns.histplot(data, bins="fd").

Suspect two modes and the KDE looks unimodal? Try bw_adjust=0.5.

SAY: Bin width is a choice you report. For skewed data Freedman–Diaconis is the better default.

Beyond outliers, EDA catches logically impossible values — entries that violate domain constraints rather than statistical ones.

an encoding error, usually a sentinel value

A percentage of 347

a unit confusion, or not a percentage at all
