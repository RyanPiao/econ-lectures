1  •  The Penalty Replaces the Search

You cannot try every model. So stop trying.

Why You Cannot Simply Try Every Model

Chapter 15's recipe: enumerate candidate models, cross-validate each, take the winner. Seven polynomial degrees is seven fits.

With \(p\) candidate variables the number of subsets is \(2^p\).

\(p = 20\)

about a million models. Tedious — but possible.

\(p = 500\)

\(2^{500}\) — a number with 151 digits.

Enumeration is not slow. It is impossible, and no faster computer changes that.

2 min. Write 2^500 on the board and let it sit. Students accept "we can't try everything" abstractly; the number makes it visceral. There are roughly 10^80 atoms in the observable universe, and 2^500 is about 10^150.

The Classical Answers, and Their Three Problems

Forward stepwise starts empty and repeatedly adds whichever variable most improves fit. Backward elimination starts full and drops the least significant. Best subset genuinely enumerates — feasible to about \(p = 30\).

1. Unstable. Change a few observations and a different variable wins round one; the whole path then diverges. Two analysts with 99% overlapping data report different models — the specification becomes a property of the sample rather than of the world.

2. Greedy in a way that misses joint effects. A variable useless alone but valuable alongside another is never added — forward selection only ever evaluates one addition at a time.

3. Every step tests a hypothesis suggested by the data. Chapter 8's multiple-comparisons problem, industrialised.

Regularization fixes 1 and 2 — one convex problem, unique minimum, all coefficients determined at once. It does not fix 3. Hold that thought until Section 6.

2 min. Problem 3 is the one you will spend the last section on. Say explicitly that you are leaving it open on purpose — opening a loop you close at minute 68.

Discussion: Design the Experiment

Prompt B — 1 min

3 min — press 3 for the timer. Take two shares. Best answers name a specific perturbation (bootstrap rows) and a specific record (selection frequency per variable), not "run it again and see". For B: a stable procedure on unstable data would still return a stable ANSWER SET; instability of the procedure and non-identification of the truth are different things, and comparing stepwise against LASSO on the same resamples separates them.

Charge the Model for Complexity

Rather than searching over subsets, change the objective so the optimiser is charged for complexity — then solve one convex problem.

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2}_{\text{fit — as in Ch 12}} + \underbrace{\lambda \, P(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{fee for complexity}} \right\}$$

In words. Keep OLS's objective and add a fee proportional to how big the coefficients are. \(\lambda \geq 0\) is the price per unit of coefficient size; \(P(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) is how you measure "size" — and choosing \(P\) is choosing the method.

\(\lambda = 0\): the fee vanishes — you recover OLS.

\(\lambda \to \infty\): every coefficient driven to zero — the model predicts the mean.

Choosing \(\lambda\) is choosing a point on Chapter 15's bias-variance curve — continuously, rather than by trying a handful of discrete models.

2 min. Present this as a REFRAMING, not an algorithm. The question changes from "which variables should I include?" to "how much total coefficient magnitude am I willing to pay for?" Nothing here is yet Ridge or LASSO — P is still unspecified.

Standardisation Is Mandatory, Not Stylistic

OLS is scale-invariant: measure income in dollars or in thousands of dollars and the fitted values are identical.

Penalised regression is not. The penalty charges by coefficient magnitude. A variable measured in dollars has a coefficient a thousand times smaller than the same variable in thousands — so it is charged a thousandth as much.

Without standardisation the penalty silently prefers whichever variables happen to be measured in large units.

Always standardise — and always inside a pipeline, so the scaler is refitted on each training fold, for exactly Chapter 15's leakage reasons.

make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoCV(cv=5))

1.5 min. This gets one minute here and gets repeated in the lab, because forgetting it produces results that look plausible and are wrong. The take-home Challenge in Lab 16 is exactly this failure, staged. The next poll is the whole reason it earns a slide.

1 min. Students click to vote on their devices; results appear on the next slide. Expect B to lead comfortably — that is the productive error and the reason this poll exists.

Answer: C — experience Is the One That Dies

Because income_last_year is measured in large units, its coefficient is tiny — pennies of wage per dollar of income — so the penalty barely charges for it and it survives. experience, in small units, carries a large coefficient, is charged heavily, and is shrunk away.

Why B is tempting but wrong: "big variable, big penalty" reverses the mechanism. The penalty charges the coefficient, not the variable — and a variable in large units has a small coefficient. Get this backwards and you will misread every unstandardised LASSO you ever see.

2 min. If B led, name it out loud — it is the intuitive-but-wrong model, and correcting it here is cheaper than correcting it in their capstone.

2  •  Ridge: Shrink Everything

One term added to a matrix, and \(p > n\) stops being fatal.

Ridge — the \(L_2\) Penalty

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2 \right\}$$

In words. Charge the sum of squared coefficients — the squared length of the coefficient vector. Squaring means a coefficient twice as large costs four times as much, so the fee leans hard on the largest coefficients and leaves small ones nearly alone.

The intercept is never penalised. Penalising it would make the model sensitive to where you put the origin of \(y\) — which is arbitrary.

1.5 min. The "four times as much" point is what makes Ridge shrink smoothly: near zero, the marginal fee for a squared penalty is itself near zero, so there is no incentive to take the last step to exactly zero. Plant that now — it is the contrast that makes the L1 slide land.

One Term, and an Impossible Problem Becomes Solvable

OLS

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

Ridge

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

Chapter 13 required \((\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})\) to be invertible — which fails under perfect collinearity, and whenever \(p > n\).

Adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) puts \(\lambda\) on every diagonal entry, which shifts every eigenvalue up by \(\lambda\) — so none of them can be zero, and the matrix is invertible for any \(\lambda > 0\), always.

Ridge does not merely help with multicollinearity. It dissolves the condition that made it fatal.

2 min. Put the two forms side by side and circle the lambda-I. This is the most satisfying thirty seconds in the chapter. A problem OLS cannot express an answer to, Ridge answers. Do not derive the eigenvalue claim — state it; it is one line and they can check it.

The Geometry: a Budget You Cannot Exceed

The penalised problem has an equivalent constrained form: minimise the ordinary sum of squared residuals subject to a budget on coefficient size.

$$\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_j \beta_j^2 \le s$$

The unpenalised objective's contours are ellipses centred on the OLS solution.

The \(L_2\) budget \(\sum\beta_j^2 \le s\) is a circle — a sphere in three dimensions, a hypersphere beyond.

The Ridge estimate sits where the expanding ellipses first touch the circle.

A circle has no corners. The point of first contact is almost never on an axis — so Ridge shrinks every coefficient toward zero and sets none exactly to zero. You get all 500 variables, each with a small coefficient.

2 min. Draw it on the board now, roughly. Say out loud: "hold this picture — in about ten minutes I am going to redraw it with one shape changed, and that single change is everything LASSO does." The contrast is what teaches sparsity; showing both at once wastes it.

Why Shrinking Toward a Wrong Value Helps

True coefficient 2.0. Your estimator is unbiased with s.e. 3.0. Unbiased means right on average — and that a typical draw returns 5.1, or −0.4, or 4.3.

Now shrink every estimate by half. It is now biased — it averages 1.0 against a truth of 2.0 — but its s.e. is 1.5.

Squared error punishes the occasional wild estimate far more than the systematic small one. Bias² rose a little; variance fell a lot; their sum fell.

Shrinkage is worthwhile exactly when your estimates are noisy — which in high dimensions they always are.

2 min. This is Chapter 15's decomposition doing the work, with numbers. It also explains why lambda should rise as the problem gets harder: more predictors relative to observations means noisier individual estimates, so more shrinkage is optimal. CV discovers that automatically — which is why the SELECTED lambda is a rough diagnostic of how much signal the data contains.

Where Ridge Shines, and Where It Cannot Help

Right tool when many predictors each contribute a little and they are correlated — it shrinks correlated groups together rather than picking arbitrarily between them.

Wrong tool when most predictors are genuinely irrelevant — and the reason is structural, not about tuning.

Uniform shrinkage cannot manufacture sparsity. No \(\lambda\) makes 490 useless coefficients disappear while leaving 10 useful ones intact.

A Hyperparameter at the Edge of Its Grid Is Information

Offered penalties from \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^{6}\), cross-validation selected the smallest one — \(\lambda = 0.01\), the floor of the grid.

That is not a bug. Faced with a problem Ridge cannot solve, CV correctly reported that more shrinkage does not help: every large \(\lambda\) was worse, so it chose the least-bad option available.

Rule of thumb. When cross-validation selects a value at the edge of your grid, either the grid is too narrow or the method is wrong for the problem. Widening it to \(10^{8}\) changed nothing here — which rules out the first and leaves the second.

A selected hyperparameter sitting at a boundary is information, not a result to report and move past.

1.5 min. This is a transferable habit worth more than the Ridge result itself. Your lab's cell 5 makes the same point in reverse: RidgeCV's default leave-one-out mode slides to whatever alpha floor you typed, which is why the lab passes cv=5 explicitly and then says "neither is at the edge of its grid, so both were actually chosen."

Poll: Counting Zeros

You fit Ridge with a large \(\lambda\) on 500 standardised predictors, 490 of which are pure noise.

How many coefficients come back exactly zero?

A) About 490 — that is what the penalty is for
B) Exactly 10 — the true ones survive
C) Zero, or essentially zero, no matter how large \(\lambda\) gets
D) It depends on \(\lambda\) — large enough \(\lambda\) zeroes them
1 min. Click to vote. Expect D to draw votes from students who half-heard the "lambda to infinity drives everything to zero" line — which is true in the limit and useless in practice.

Answer: C — None of Them

The constraint region is a circle. It has no corners, so the point where the expanding loss contours first touch it is almost never on an axis — and a coefficient is exactly zero only on an axis. All 490 noise variables stay in the model at reduced weight.

Why D is tempting but wrong: \(\lambda\to\infty\) does drive every coefficient toward zero — including the ten real ones. There is no \(\lambda\) that zeroes the 490 while sparing the 10. That is structural, not a tuning problem.

2 min. This is the cleanest possible setup for the next section. Say: "so if you want a shorter list, you need a constraint region with corners" and advance to the divider.

3  •  LASSO: Shrink and Select

One exponent changed. The consequence is qualitative.

LASSO — the \(L_1\) Penalty

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Tibshirani, 1996)

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \right\}$$

One exponent changed, from \(\beta_j^2\) to \(|\beta_j|\).

In words. Charge the sum of absolute coefficients instead of squared ones. Unlike squaring, the absolute value keeps charging at full rate all the way down to zero — so there is a real incentive to take the last step and set a coefficient exactly to zero.

The consequence is qualitative rather than quantitative.

1.5 min. Contrast explicitly with the Ridge slide: for a squared penalty the marginal fee near zero is itself near zero, so nothing pushes a small coefficient the last step. For an absolute-value penalty the marginal fee is constant. That is the intuition; the picture on the next slide is the proof.

The Same Picture, One Shape Changed

Expanding elliptical contours meeting a diamond are very likely to touch at a corner, because corners stick out. And a corner of the diamond is a point where one coordinate is exactly zero.

2.5 min. Ask, before you explain: "where does the growing ellipse hit each shape?" Students see the corner argument immediately, and it is genuinely the reason for everything LASSO does. In p dimensions the region has corners, edges and faces of every dimension, so solutions with ANY number of exact zeros are available. Note LASSO produces sparsity NOT by testing coefficients and dropping insignificant ones — there is no test anywhere in this picture.

Why the False Positives Appear — and Why They Must

Among 490 irrelevant predictors, each independent of the outcome, some will correlate with \(y\) in this sample by chance alone. With 490 draws, a few will correlate quite strongly. LASSO cannot distinguish "correlates because it causes" from "correlates because we got unlucky" — it sees only the sample.

To exclude every lucky noise variable, the penalty would have to exclude anything with a modest sample correlation. But some genuinely relevant variables also have modest sample correlations — particularly those with smaller true coefficients. A penalty aggressive enough to remove all the noise removes real signal along with it.

So the choice is not between a clean list and a dirty one. It is between a short list that misses true variables and a longer one that admits false ones.

And note the design: those 34 false positives occur on independent Gaussian columns — the friendliest possible case. Real economic data, where everything correlates with everything, is worse.

2 min. It is not a tuning failure. LASSO is a SCREENING device — it reliably retains the signal while admitting a controlled amount of noise. If a student asks whether a different lambda fixes it: yes, and the next section shows you exactly what that costs.

Common Misconception

"LASSO tells me which variables matter."

It tells you which variables it kept, which is a different thing. Here it kept 44 to find 10.

And the selection carries a formal caveat: LASSO is only guaranteed to recover the true set of variables when the irrelevant ones are not strongly correlated with the relevant ones — a condition routinely violated by macroeconomic series, asset-pricing factors, and anything with a factor structure.

Treat a LASSO selection as a hypothesis worth investigating — never as a finding.

1.5 min. The formal name for that condition is the irrepresentable condition; you do not need it, but say it once so they recognise it in a paper. The operational instruction is the boxed sentence, and it will be tested again in Poll 4.

Discussion: Is Your Question Sparse or Dense?

A penalty is not a neutral technical device. It encodes a belief about the world.

\(L_1\) believes most coefficients are exactly zero. \(L_2\) believes many are small but nonzero. Neither is a safe default.

Prompt A — 2 min, pairs

Take a question you actually care about — portfolio project, capstone. Write the outcome and roughly how many candidate predictors you could assemble. Then answer, with a reason from the subject matter: do a handful do essentially all the work, or do many matter a little?

Prompt B — 1 min

Now argue against yourself. If you said sparse, what would a dense truth look like here? If you said dense, which three variables would a sparsist claim are doing everything?

The point: this is not a statistical question. It is a question about your subject matter, and you are the one qualified to answer it.

4 min — press 4 for the timer. Take three shares. Name the pattern out loud: problems built from ADMINISTRATIVE data (many weak overlapping measures of the same few things) tend to be dense; problems built from DESIGNED measurement (a few binding constraints, a few dominant sectors) tend to be sparse. Close on the honest position: if you genuinely do not know, say so, use Elastic Net, and report the mixing parameter the data chose — that number is itself a finding.

4  •  Choosing \(\lambda\)

Where the right variables and the right predictions part company.

\(\lambda\) Is a Hyperparameter

\(\lambda\) cannot be estimated by minimising the objective — that would return \(\lambda = 0\), since the unpenalised fit always has the lowest training error.

It is a hyperparameter, and Chapter 15 already gave you the machinery: cross-validation.

The standard approach computes a regularization path — the whole sequence of solutions across a logarithmic grid of \(\lambda\), from large enough to zero every coefficient down to nearly zero — and cross-validates along it. LassoCV and RidgeCV do this in one call, and modern solvers compute the entire path for barely more than the cost of a single fit.

lambda selected by cross-validation: 0.138

1.5 min. "Barely more than the cost of a single fit" is worth saying because students assume a path is expensive and therefore skip it. It is not, and looking at the path is where the next three slides come from.

Three Readings of One Table

1. It is Chapter 15's U-curve, now in \(\lambda\). Training error falls forever (32.191 → 0.005); test error bottoms at \(\lambda = 0.138\) and rises again. And CV chose exactly that value without seeing the test set.

2. Look at the top two rows. At \(\lambda = 2.0\) and \(\lambda = 1.0\), LASSO keeps exactly 10 features and every one is true. Perfect recovery — no misses, no false positives.

3. Those perfect rows are terrible predictors. Test MSE 6.92 at \(\lambda = 1.0\) against 1.54 at the optimum. The model that finds exactly the right variables predicts 4.5× worse than the one keeping 44.

2.5 min. Reveal in order and pause after 3. The resolution: at lambda = 1.0 the ten survivors have been shrunk HEAVILY toward zero — that is what a large penalty does — so the variables are right and the magnitudes are badly attenuated. Loosening the penalty restores the magnitudes and admits noise along with them, and for squared-error prediction that trade is worth making.

Two Objectives, Two Different \(\lambda\)

If you want the right variables

\(\lambda = 1.0\)

10 kept, 10 true, 0 false — and test MSE 6.92

If you want the best predictions

\(\lambda = 0.138\)

44 kept, 34 of them noise — and test MSE 1.54

Selecting the right variables and predicting well are different objectives, optimised at different \(\lambda\) — and here they are in direct conflict. Cross-validation targets prediction, because that is what you asked it for.

If you want the variable list, you are asking a question this procedure was not tuned to answer.

1.5 min. This is the single most important slide in the deck for their capstones. Say it twice. It is also the setup for Section 6 — the reason "LASSO chose my controls" is a category error and not just a technicality.

A large penalty does two things at once — it zeroes some coefficients and it shrinks the survivors. At \(\lambda = 1.0\) the right variables are in the model with magnitudes far too small, so predictions come out systematically flat.

5  •  The Penalty Is a Belief

Change nothing but the truth, and the ranking reverses.

Same \(X\), Same Estimators, Same Grid — Only the Truth Changed

Dense truth: all 500 features matter, each a little. LASSO is now the worst of the three — a third worse than OLS, on data where OLS is already poor.

That framing is wrong and this is the demolition. Note LASSO kept 114 of 500 on the dense truth: it forced 386 zeros onto parameters that are not zero.

A Penalty Encodes a Belief

The \(L_1\) penalty believes

most coefficients are exactly zero.

When true, enormously powerful. When false, it is forcing zeros onto parameters that are not zero — paying in bias for nothing.

The \(L_2\) penalty believes

many coefficients are small but nonzero.

Right whenever effects are diffuse — which describes a great deal of economics, where outcomes are shaped by many weakly-acting factors.

So the choice is not statistical. It is a question about your subject matter: do I believe a small number of these variables do essentially all the work? Sometimes yes — a few genes, a few binding constraints. Often no.

1.5 min. This is the payoff of Discussion 2 — refer back to the answers you heard. If you genuinely do not know, that is a legitimate position, and Elastic Net with a tuned mixing parameter is how you say it honestly.

Elastic Net — Let the Data Choose the Mix

$$\lambda \left( \alpha_{EN} \sum_j |\beta_j| + \tfrac{1 - \alpha_{EN}}{2}\sum_j \beta_j^2 \right)$$

In words. Pay a blend of the two fees. \(\alpha_{EN} = 1\) is pure LASSO, \(\alpha_{EN} = 0\) is pure Ridge. The \(L_1\) part preserves sparsity; the \(L_2\) part supplies a grouping effect, pulling correlated predictors toward each other rather than letting one win arbitrarily.

On the correlated data above it kept 7 of the 8 and spread them far more evenly — the blue bars on the previous slide.

ElasticNetCV(cv=5, l1_ratio=[0.1, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0])

mixing parameter chosen on the sparse design: 1.0

The chosen mixing parameter is itself a result worth reporting. Near 1 says the data looks sparse; near 0, that effects are diffuse.

1.5 min. Passing a LIST of l1_ratio values makes the mix a tuned hyperparameter rather than an assumption. On the sparse design it picks 1.0 — pure LASSO, the correct answer there, and a small reassurance the procedure works. Elastic Net also escapes a hard limit: pure LASSO can select at most n variables before saturating.

Economist at Work — the IMF, 2024

Post-pandemic structural breaks had made the reliable data short while the number of available high-frequency indicators had grown past a hundred. With \(p\) approaching \(n\), the design matrix was rank-deficient: OLS was not merely inaccurate but incomputable.

The team deployed LASSO, Ridge and Elastic Net. The first result was disappointing — on highly collinear macro series, LASSO selected variables that made no economic sense.

What fixed it was not a better algorithm. The economists imposed sign restrictions — consumer sentiment must enter positively, unemployment claims negatively, and so on wherever theory dictates a direction. With those constraints the model outperformed both MIDAS and dynamic-factor benchmarks.

An algorithm turned loose on collinear data finds patterns. An economist decides which patterns are permitted.

1.5 min. The penalty encodes a belief about SPARSITY; sign restrictions encode a belief about DIRECTION. Both are economics entering the estimator, and here the economics is what made the machine learning work. This is the chapter's thesis in operational form — say so.

6  •  What You May Not Do Afterwards

For economists, the most consequential thing in the chapter.

A Workflow That Sounds Completely Reasonable

You have 500 candidate controls. You run LASSO; it keeps 12. You fit ordinary OLS on those 12, report the coefficients with their standard errors and \(p\)-values, and describe the result as a regression with well-chosen controls.

This is invalid, and the invalidity is not subtle.

Take thirty seconds. What is wrong with it?

1.5 min. Present the workflow FIRST and let it sit before you condemn it. Ask the room what is wrong. The failure is not obvious and the discovery is worth the wait — many of them have done exactly this, or will.

You Used the Data Twice

Classical inference assumes the specification was fixed before seeing the data. Chapter 8 built every \(p\)-value on that assumption.

But those 12 variables were chosen because they correlated strongly with the outcome in this sample — that is what LASSO does. Reporting classical standard errors treats a data-driven choice as if theory had made it, and the intervals come out too narrow. Their true coverage is well below nominal.

It is the same error as running 500 hypothesis tests and reporting only the significant ones — which Chapter 8 named. LASSO simply automates the selection and makes it feel principled.

Worse than the manual version in one respect: a researcher running 500 tests by hand knows they did it. LASSO performs the same search silently and returns a tidy list — there is no artefact to audit.

2 min. Regularization is a variance-reduction tool for PREDICTION. It is not a method for choosing controls in a causal study, and treating it as one delivers invalid inference. This is problem 3 from the stepwise slide, still unfixed — close that loop explicitly.

What to Do Instead Depends on What You Want

For prediction

Nothing is wrong. Report cross-validated error and make no claims about individual coefficients.

For a treatment effect

You need post-double selection: run LASSO twice — once predicting the outcome from the controls, once predicting the treatment — and use the union of both sets in an unpenalised regression.

Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen (2014). The machinery is ECON 5200's and Chapter 24's — recognising that you need it is today's.

And it buys less than you would expect. A 2024 World Bank audit re-estimated 780 treatment effects from published randomised trials using post-double selection. Against plain ANCOVA with pre-specified controls it improved standard errors by under 1% — and in over half the cases the treatment equation selected no controls at all.

That second finding is the explanation, not an embarrassment: in a randomised trial assignment is random by construction, so nothing should predict it. The second LASSO has nothing to find.

2 min. Do not derive PDS. Name it, say what it does in one sentence, and flag it as the bridge to Ch 24. The 780-effects number converts an abstract objection into a demonstration — the method is worth LEAST exactly where you can already trust your design, and worth most exactly where you cannot check whether it worked.

What Regularization Does Not Fix

1. A confounded design. Shrinkage reduces variance. It says nothing about bias from an omitted confounder — and it makes it harder to see, because a coefficient shrunk toward zero looks modest and well-behaved whether it is correctly estimated or badly confounded.

2. Causal identification. LASSO selects variables that predict. A variable can predict because it causes the outcome, because it shares a cause, or because it is a consequence of the outcome — Chapter 10's three structures, indistinguishable to any penalty. Hand it a collider and it will keep it.

3. A badly measured target. Chapter 15's noise floor is unmoved by any penalty. If \(y\) is measured with substantial error, the way past that limit is better data, not a better estimator.

And one for reading papers. Regularized coefficients are not comparable across specifications the way OLS coefficients are. Change \(\lambda\), the candidate set, or the scaling, and every coefficient moves — each is the solution to a different constrained problem. Reporting a LASSO coefficient without its \(\lambda\), its standardisation and its candidate set is reporting an uninterpretable number.

1.5 min. Short and deliberately deflationary. Students finish a regularization chapter feeling that high-dimensional problems are now solved; these three reset that. The non-comparability point is the one they will need when reading papers, and it is rarely taught.

Discussion: The 44-Variable List

A colleague sends you this and asks you to sign off before it goes to the policy team.

"We screened 500 candidate indicators with LASSO. It identified the 44 that matter. We then re-ran OLS on those 44; twelve are significant at the 5% level, and we recommend targeting those twelve."

Prompt A — 2 min, pairs

There are three distinct things wrong with that paragraph. Find all three. One is a word, one is a procedure, one is a missing number.

Prompt B — 1 min

Rewrite it so it says only what the analysis supports. You are allowed to keep the LASSO.

3 min — press 3 for the timer. Take two shares, then debrief. The three: (i) "the 44 that matter" — it KEPT 44; on our own data 44 kept meant 10 real and 34 noise. (ii) re-running OLS on the selected set and reporting classical p-values is post-selection inference — invalid, intervals too narrow. (iii) no lambda, no standardisation statement, no candidate set, so those 44 coefficients are uninterpretable and the list would change if any of the three changed. An honest rewrite: "LASSO retained 44 of 500 indicators at lambda = 0.138 on standardised features. We report cross-validated predictive error. The retained list is a set of hypotheses for follow-up, not a finding, and we make no claim about individual coefficients."

Poll: What May You Report?

Which of these is a legitimate thing to report after a cross-validated LASSO?

A) "These 44 variables are the drivers of the outcome"
B) "LASSO retained 44 of 500 predictors at \(\lambda = 0.138\); cross-validated test MSE was 1.54"
C) "Of the 44 selected, twelve are significant at the 5% level"
D) "Because LASSO is a principled algorithm, our controls are well chosen and inference is valid"
1 min. Click to vote. This is the assessment moment for objective 16.4.

Answer: B — Say What the Procedure Did

B reports what the procedure did, the value of the hyperparameter that produced it, and a predictive score — and claims nothing about individual coefficients or about which variables matter.

Why C is tempting but wrong: it is the single most common thing people actually do. Those \(p\)-values were computed on a specification the data chose, so their coverage is well below nominal. The fix is post-double selection — but recognising that you need a fix is today's job.

1.5 min. If A drew votes, that is the misconception slide coming back — worth naming, because it means the "kept, not matters" distinction has not landed yet.

7  •  The Workflow, and Your Lab

Six sections of theory. Here is what to actually do.

A Regularization Workflow

1

Decide what you want before you fit. Prediction, or a coefficient you intend to interpret? Most confusion comes from wanting both and admitting to neither.

2

Standardise, inside a pipeline. One line handles the scale-dependence and the leakage together.

3

Fit Ridge and LASSO both, and let cross-validation compare them. The comparison is itself informative about sparsity.

4

Look at the CV curve, not just its minimum. Flat across an order of magnitude means \(\lambda\) is not identified — say so.

5

Report the number selected, and treat the list as a hypothesis. "Retained 44 of 500" is a finding. "Identified the 44 that matter" is not.

6

If a causal parameter is involved, use post-double selection — where the graduate track picks this up. For 3916: stop at step 5, and know step 6 exists.

2 min. Say step 1 is the one that matters. Students reach for LASSO because it produces a satisfying short list of variables, then interpret that list — which is precisely the trap. Steps 5 and 6 are the corrective, and are worth stating twice.

A smaller version of today's design: \(n = 150\), \(p = 200\), \(k = 5\) true predictors, split 70/30 — 105 training rows against 200 predictors.

Three Takeaways

1

The penalty replaces the search. \(2^{500}\) subsets is impossible; one convex problem with a fee on coefficient size is not. Ridge's \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) even dissolves the invertibility requirement that made \(p > n\) fatal.

2

The penalty is an assumption, not an upgrade. \(L_1\) asserts the truth is sparse, \(L_2\) that it is dense. On a sparse truth LASSO beat OLS 28×; change only the truth and it came last of three.

3

Selection is not the same objective as prediction. The \(\lambda\) that found exactly the right 10 predicted 4.5× worse than the \(\lambda\) that kept 44. Report the list as a hypothesis, and never attach classical \(p\)-values to it.

1 min. Have the room say takeaway 2 back to you. It is the one that survives the semester — and the one most likely to be got wrong in a capstone.
