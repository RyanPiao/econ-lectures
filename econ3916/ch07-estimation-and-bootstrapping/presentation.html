Selection, survivorship, nonresponse, self-selection are the same thing: $\text{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$ — whether you get recorded depends on the answer. Different stories, identical damage.

8.3

standard deviations above its historical mean — US Economic Policy Uncertainty

trade-policy uncertainty: 16

The Fed's fan charts — the shaded bands around GDP, unemployment and inflation projections — are built from about twenty years of past forecast errors. Roughly 80 quarterly observations.

They assume future errors resemble past errors. When they stop resembling them, a band labelled 70% can capture the truth far less often than 70% of the time.

From One Number to a Distribution

Standard deviation describes people. Standard error describes your estimate.

1862: A Forecast Reported to the Last Person

Lincoln’s Second Annual Message to Congress, 1 December 1862. He extrapolated the 1790–1860 growth ratio forward and tabulated the result for every decade.

His projection for 1930

251,680,914

Nine significant figures. Not “about 250 million” — down to the single person.

The 1930 census counted

122,775,046

Over by 128,905,868 — a factor of 2.05×.

The arithmetic was fine. The nine digits were the lie — they claimed a precision the method could not carry. One number cannot tell you how wrong it might be. A number and a band can.

G 4’s fan chart was the same failure from the other side: a band so narrow it implied precision the data could not support. Over-precise point, over-confident interval — 163 years apart.

The answer you want: a range. “Somewhere between 150 and 350 million” would have been less impressive and far more useful — and it would have contained the truth, which his number did not come close to.
Then make the bridge to today explicitly. An individual is unpredictable; aggregate enough of them and the average becomes stable — that is why we sample at all. Standard Deviation Is Not Standard Error

$$\text{SE}(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

$s$ — the sample standard deviation. How spread out the observations are.

$n$ — the sample size. The only thing here you control.

2 min.

Write both words on the board and keep them there all hour.

FROM THE PODCAST — the forest, and it is worth the forty seconds. The standard deviation is the varying heights of every tree — towering redwoods down to new saplings. The standard error is what you get sending “10 different research teams into that exact same forest”, each measuring a random patch of 100 and reporting its average: 40 ft, 42 ft, 39 ft. The spread of those team averages is the standard error.

NEW DIAGRAM — the “in plain language” paragraph is gone. Two number lines on one scale: the people spread across s = 13.640, the estimate pinned at SE = 1.364. Trace the top line, then the bottom, and let the width do the arguing.

The $\sqrt{n}$ Payoff — and Its Price

Every 4× in sample size buys a 2× cut in the standard error. Not a defect of the method — the arithmetic of a square root.

2 min.

The practical sting: doubling a survey budget does not halve its uncertainty.

FROM THE PODCAST — the episode walks the price in money rather than in algebra, and its three numbers are exactly this deck's: at n = 25 the standard error is about 2.7 years; halving it to 1.35 needs not 50 people but 100; halving again to 0.67 needs 400. “You are paying for a 4x increase in data acquisition just to get a 2x increase in precision.” All three follow from s = 13.640, so you can derive any of them live.

Does the Population Size Matter?

Look at the formula again: $s/\sqrt{n}$. There is no $N$ in it. A sample of 200 is as precise for Massachusetts as for the whole country — which feels wrong, and is not.

The exact version carries a correction, $\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$, and it only bites when your sample is a big slice of the population:

200 of 2.9 million → cuts the SE by 0.003%
200 of 20,000 → 0.5%
200 of 2,000 → 5%
200 of 400 → 29%

Below about n/N = 5% you can ignore it, which is almost every survey you will ever read. Above it, you are sampling a sizeable fraction of the thing itself, and the uncertainty really is smaller.

Common Misconception

“Standard error is just standard deviation divided by root $n$.”

True for

The mean of independent observations. That is one statistic.

False for

The median, a ratio, a Gini coefficient, a 90th percentile. Different formula — often none at all.

Hold on to this. The gap between “statistics with a formula” and “statistics without one” is where the rest of the lecture lives.

  • Name the misconception out loud as a sentence a reasonable person would say, then take it apart. Stating it is what makes it falsifiable.
  • The honest version: the bootstrap replaces an assumption with a computation. It does not replace a small sample with a large one.

The Central Limit Theorem

$$\sqrt{n}\,(\bar{X} - \mu) \xrightarrow{\;d\;} N(0, \sigma^2) \qquad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

In plain language: whatever shape the population has — skewed, lumpy, bimodal — the average of a large enough sample behaves like a draw from a normal distribution.

The normal curve is everywhere in statistics not because data is normal, but because averages tend to normality.

Two things it does not say: nothing about individual observations, and it needs finite variance.

The Parametric Interval

And the statistic it cannot reach.

Building a Confidence Interval

$$\text{CI}_{95\%} = \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

In plain language: take the estimate and walk out about two standard errors in each direction. The 1.96 is the width that captures the middle 95% of a normal curve.

Notice what this needs: a standard error, and a claim that the sampling distribution is normal. Both come from the CLT, and both are about the mean.

2 min.

Say the last line slowly — it is the setup for the wall two slides from now.

Everything in this formula is a fact about the mean; nothing in it transfers to another statistic for free.

“There is a 95% probability the true mean lies in this interval.”

“95% of the data falls inside this interval.”

The Wall: What Is the SE of a Median?

Chapter 4 said the median is the better summary for skewed data. The Fed reports median household net worth ($192,900) beside the mean ($1.06 million) for exactly that reason. So: how precise is a median?

$$\text{SE}(\text{median}) = \frac{1}{2\, f(\text{median})} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$$

To use it you need $f(\text{median})$ — the population density at the median. If you knew the population, you would not be estimating anything. This formula exists and is unusable.

3 min.

This is the slide the whole first half was building toward.

FROM THE PODCAST — the sharpest statement of the dead end in either source. “To use the formula you have to plug in f of median, which means you must already know the exact true density of the entire population at the exact true median point. And if you already possessed perfect omniscient knowledge of the entire population's distribution, you wouldn't be running a sample survey of 500 people in the first place.” Then the line to land: the formula demands perfect knowledge to measure the uncertainty of imperfect knowledge.

The Bootstrap

If you cannot derive the sampling distribution, simulate it.

The Algorithm, in English Before Any Code

Why with replacement? Without it you would draw the same $n$ observations every time, just shuffled — zero variability. With replacement, each resample holds about 63.2% unique observations. That is the engine.

Do this with five index cards before showing the slide if you have them.

FROM THE PODCAST — the hat, and it is the same five numbers as the slide. Five workers' commutes: 15, 22, 22, 35, 86, median 22. Write them on slips, put them in a hat, pull one out — “it's a 22. You record the 22. And then, and this is crucially important, you put that slip of paper back into the hat. The hat is fully replenished.” Do the gesture; it is what makes with replacement stick.

NEW DIAGRAM — the five numbered steps are gone. The original sample, then three resamples drawn from it, with medians 22, 35, 15 underneath. Point along one resample, then at its median.

  • Draw one, write it down, put it BACK, draw again.
  • The physical act of putting the card back is what makes "with replacement" stick.

Common Misconception

“The bootstrap creates new data.”

What it does

Reuses your observations in different multiplicities, to estimate how much your statistic would move across real repeated samples.

What it cannot do

Add information. If your sample is biased (Ch 6), every resample is biased identically — and the interval will be confidently centred on the wrong number.

A tight bootstrap interval around a biased estimate is worse than a wide one. It is wrong, and it looks certain.

2 min.

Tie it explicitly back to Meng from the retrieval check.

The bootstrap measures variance, never bias. Students who remember only one caveat should remember this one.

The Resample, In Four Lines

ages = df["age"].values
B = 2_000

# each resample draws len(ages) people WITH replacement, then takes the median
boot_medians = np.array([np.median(rng.choice(ages, size=len(ages), replace=True))
                         for _ in range(B)])

What it prints

Sample median age: 37.0
Bootstrap SE:      0.0000
Bootstrap 95% CI:  [37.0, 37.0]
Distinct bootstrap medians: 1

1 min.

The code behind the collapse — and the output is the collapse itself.

FROM THE PODCAST — the episode supplies the number this slide implies but does not print: through the mathematics of sampling with replacement, any given resample holds roughly 63.2% unique observations from the original data, the other 36.8% being duplicates. That is what replace=True is doing on the middle line, and it is why the four printed lines come out the way they do.

  • Read the output, not the code: SE 0.0000, CI [37.0, 37.0], 1 distinct median.
  • rng.choice(ages, size=len(ages), replace=True) is the whole method. Everything else is bookkeeping.
  • If anyone asks where 63.2% comes from: it is 1 minus 1/e. Worth one sentence, not a derivation.

Pinned, and Not Pinned

Ask scipy for BCa on That, and It Refuses

result = bootstrap((ages,), np.median, method='BCa', ...)

DegenerateDataWarning: The BCa confidence interval cannot be calculated ...
BCa 95% CI: [nan, nan]

Why. BCa estimates its correction by leaving out one observation at a time and watching the statistic move. Here it never moves — so the correction is $0/0$.

A method that refuses to answer is behaving well. The failure mode to fear is the one that returns a plausible number.

It's the algorithm saying, hey, the math breaks here and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.” And the sentence before it is the one to keep: “it refuses to manufacture uncertainty that doesn't exist in the sample structure.”

  • Most students' instinct on seeing nan is to change a setting until the nan goes away.
  • That instinct is exactly backwards here.

When the Bootstrap Fails

1. Very small samples ($n < 15$). The sample cannot represent the population's shape.

2. Extreme quantiles. The 99.9th percentile needs observations out there to resample.

3. Infinite variance. The bootstrap SE does not converge — use subsampling.

4. Dependent data treated as independent. Time series or clusters need the block or cluster bootstrap.

5. Non-smooth statistics. Max, min, range — each depends on a single observation, so resampling behaves badly.

Notice what is not on this list: bias. The bootstrap has no defence against it at all.

Which Interval?

Percentile, basic, BCa — and why scipy's default is right.

Three Ways to Slice the Bootstrap Distribution

MethodHow it slicesUse it when
PercentileTake the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap valuesQuick exploration
BasicReflect the bootstrap distribution around the sample estimateRarely, by hand
BCaPercentile, then shifted to correct for bias and skewAnything consequential

$$\text{CI}^{\text{pct}}_{95\%} = \left[\hat{\theta}^*_{0.025},\; \hat{\theta}^*_{0.975}\right]$$

In plain language: sort your 2,000 bootstrap values and chop off the outer 2.5% at each end. What is left is the interval. BCa's advantage, without the asymptotics: its coverage error shrinks faster as the sample grows.

2 min.

The operational takeaway is one line: BCa is scipy's default, and that default is right.

  • Do not do the O(n^-1) versus O(n^-1/2) notation with this audience — the plain sentence carries it.
  • The plain sentence: percentile trusts the resamples as they fall; BCa corrects them for bias and for skew before reading the ends.
  • Then G37 measures it on the capital-gains mean, so resist settling it here by assertion.
  • G31 is the counterpoint they will remember: the same default refuses to answer when it should.

Economist at Work: Netflix's Quantile Bootstrap

Netflix passed 300 million paid memberships at the end of 2024. For quality metrics like play delay — the wait between pressing play and video starting — the distribution is aggressively right-skewed: most users start instantly, a tail suffers. Averages hide exactly the users you most need to see.

So they evaluate quantile treatment effects — the 50th, 75th and 95th percentiles. Quantiles have no closed-form standard error, so they bootstrap.

To make it affordable they compress each metric into (value, count) pairs and bootstrap the counts — cost becomes independent of sample size.

Across 123 historical A/B tests, bootstrap-informed decision rules would have raised cumulative returns on the north-star metric by an estimated 33%. Note why they bootstrap: not because it beats a formula, but because for a quantile there is no formula to beat.

Netflix Tech Blog, “Reimagining Experimentation Analysis”; Chou et al. (2025), KDD Best Paper, arXiv:2502.08763.

Key Takeaways

SD describes people; SE describes your estimate. Only the SE shrinks with $n$, and only at rate $\sqrt{n}$.

The CLT's arrival time depends on skew, not on 30. For economists' data, “large enough” can mean hundreds.

The bootstrap is for statistics with no formula — medians, quantiles, ratios. Not a repair for one that has a formula: 92.5% vs 91.5%, inside the noise.

A zero bootstrap SE is an answer, not a failure. So is nan from BCa. Read the warning, not the number.

Check a gap against its own error before calling it a finding. Every coverage number today came with a Monte Carlo SE for exactly that reason.

The bootstrap did not make the mean interval better. It made the median interval possible — 93.2% against 66.8%.
