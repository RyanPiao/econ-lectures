April 2025: the Fed's Fan Charts Stopped Working

8.3

standard deviations above its historical mean — US Economic Policy Uncertainty

trade-policy uncertainty: 16

The Fed's fan charts — the shaded bands around GDP, unemployment and inflation projections — are built from about twenty years of past forecast errors. Roughly 80 quarterly observations.

They assume future errors resemble past errors. When they stop resembling them, a band labelled 70% can capture the truth far less often than 70% of the time.

Standard Deviation Is Not Standard Error

$$\text{SE}(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

$s$ — the sample standard deviation. How spread out the observations are.

$n$ — the sample size. The only thing here you control.

Does the Population Size Matter?

Look at the formula again: $s/\sqrt{n}$. There is no $N$ in it. A sample of 200 is as precise for Massachusetts as for the whole country — which feels wrong, and is not.

The exact version carries a correction, $\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$, and it only bites when your sample is a big slice of the population:

200 of 2.9 million → cuts the SE by 0.003%
200 of 20,000 → 0.5%
200 of 2,000 → 5%
200 of 400 → 29%

Below about n/N = 5% you can ignore it, which is almost every survey you will ever read. Above it, you are sampling a sizeable fraction of the thing itself, and the uncertainty really is smaller.

Common Misconception

“Standard error is just standard deviation divided by root $n$.”

True for

The mean of independent observations. That is one statistic.

False for

The median, a ratio, a Gini coefficient, a 90th percentile. Different formula — often none at all.

Hold on to this. The gap between “statistics with a formula” and “statistics without one” is where the rest of the lecture lives.

The Central Limit Theorem

$$\sqrt{n}\,(\bar{X} - \mu) \xrightarrow{\;d\;} N(0, \sigma^2) \qquad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

In plain language: whatever shape the population has — skewed, lumpy, bimodal — the average of a large enough sample behaves like a draw from a normal distribution.

The normal curve is everywhere in statistics not because data is normal, but because averages tend to normality.

Two things it does not say: nothing about individual observations, and it needs finite variance.

The Algorithm, in English Before Any Code

Bradley Efron, 1979: treat your sample as if it were the population, and resample it.

Why with replacement? Without it you would draw the same $n$ observations every time, just shuffled — zero variability. With replacement, each resample holds about 63.2% unique observations. That is the engine.

IF ASKED: where 63.2% comes from. A given row is missed on one draw with probability 1 − 1/n, and on all n draws with (1 − 1/n)n, which approaches 1/e ≈ 0.368 as n grows — so about 63.2% of rows appear in a resample. Common Misconception

“The bootstrap creates new data.”

What it does

Reuses your observations in different multiplicities, to estimate how much your statistic would move across real repeated samples.

What it cannot do

Add information. If your sample is biased (Ch 6), every resample is biased identically — and the interval will be confidently centred on the wrong number.

A tight bootstrap interval around a biased estimate is worse than a wide one. It is wrong, and it looks certain.

Economist at Work: Netflix's Quantile Bootstrap

Netflix passed 300 million paid memberships at the end of 2024. For quality metrics like play delay — the wait between pressing play and video starting — the distribution is aggressively right-skewed: most users start instantly, a tail suffers. Averages hide exactly the users you most need to see.

So they evaluate quantile treatment effects — the 50th, 75th and 95th percentiles. Quantiles have no usable standard-error formula, so they bootstrap.

To make it affordable they compress each metric into (value, count) pairs and bootstrap the counts — cost becomes independent of sample size.

In Netflix's own example, one new encode raised the mean play delay and lowered the median — faster for most members, slower in the tail. Note why they bootstrap: not because it beats a formula, but because for a quantile there is no usable formula to beat.

When the Bootstrap Fails

1. Very small samples ($n < 15$). The sample cannot represent the population's shape.

2. Extreme quantiles. The 99.9th percentile needs observations out there to resample.

3. Infinite variance. The bootstrap SE does not converge — use subsampling.

4. Dependent data treated as independent. Time series or clusters need the block or cluster bootstrap — ignore it and the interval comes out too narrow.

5. Non-smooth statistics. Max, min, range — each depends on a single observation, so resampling behaves badly.

Notice what is not on this list: bias. The bootstrap has no defence against it at all. And a bigger B only cuts simulation noise — it never repairs a broken assumption.
