From One Number to a Distribution

Standard deviation describes people. Standard error describes your estimate.

A Point Estimate Hides a Question

Point estimate

One number, from one sample. A mean, a median, a ratio.

Tells you where you landed.

Sampling distribution

The distribution of that number across every possible sample of the same size.

Tells you how much you could have missed by.

The ACS puts median Massachusetts household income near $96,500. That comes from about 100,000 households out of 2.9 million. A different 100,000 would have given a different number. How different is the entire subject of this lecture.

2 min. Ask the room: "if I told you the average age in this room is 21, what have I not told you?" Push until someone says "how sure are you." That is the sampling distribution, and you can name it once they have said it.

Standard Deviation Is Not Standard Error

$$\text{SE}(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

$s$ — the sample standard deviation. How spread out the observations are.

$n$ — the sample size. The only thing here you control.

In plain language: the standard deviation describes people. The standard error describes our estimate of the average person. They answer different questions, and only one of them shrinks when you collect more data.

2 min. Write both words on the board and keep them there all hour. Every time a number appears later, ask which of the two it is. The lecture's most common student error is reading a bootstrap histogram as if it described people.

2 min. Emphasise the factor of ten. Students who confuse SD with SE are not making a subtle error; they are off by an order of magnitude. Note that we never had to know the population — the formula got there from one sample.

The $\sqrt{n}$ Payoff — and Its Price

Every 4× in sample size buys a 2× cut in the standard error. Not a defect of the method — the arithmetic of a square root.

2 min. The practical sting: doubling a survey budget does not halve its uncertainty. Ask what that implies for someone deciding whether to fund a bigger sample — at some point the money is better spent on reducing bias, which is Chapter 6, and which no amount of n fixes.

Common Misconception

“Standard error is just standard deviation divided by root $n$.”

True for

The mean of independent observations. That is one statistic.

False for

The median, a ratio, a Gini coefficient, a 90th percentile. Different formula — often none at all.

Hold on to this. The gap between “statistics with a formula” and “statistics without one” is where the rest of the lecture lives.

2 min. Do not rush this slide — it is the hinge. If students leave believing the bootstrap is a better version of s/sqrt(n), the coverage section at the end will land as a contradiction instead of as the point.

Answer: B — They Quoted the Wrong Number

13.6 describes how much people differ in age. The precision of the average is $13.64/\sqrt{100} \approx 1.36$ years — ten times tighter. The estimate is not imprecise at all.

Why A is tempting: 13.6 is the only spread number on the page, so it gets used as if it were a margin of error. This exact substitution appears in published work.

The CLT, and How Slowly It Arrives

“n ≥ 30” is folklore, not a theorem.

The Central Limit Theorem

$$\sqrt{n}\,(\bar{X} - \mu) \xrightarrow{\;d\;} N(0, \sigma^2) \qquad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

In plain language: whatever shape the population has — skewed, lumpy, bimodal — the average of a large enough sample behaves like a draw from a normal distribution.

The normal curve is everywhere in statistics not because data is normal, but because averages tend to normality.

Two things it does not say: nothing about individual observations, and it needs finite variance.

2 min. The arrow with the d is "converges in distribution" — say it in words, do not assume they read notation. The two caveats on the right are the ones that come back: the first at the bootstrap histogram, the second at the heavy-tails slide.

The Parametric Interval

And the statistic it cannot reach.

Building a Confidence Interval

$$\text{CI}_{95\%} = \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

In plain language: take the estimate and walk out about two standard errors in each direction. The 1.96 is the width that captures the middle 95% of a normal curve.

Notice what this needs: a standard error, and a claim that the sampling distribution is normal. Both come from the CLT, and both are about the mean.

2 min. Say the last line slowly — it is the setup for the wall two slides from now. Everything in this formula is a fact about the mean; nothing in it transfers to another statistic for free.

What the 95% Is a Statement About

“There is a 95% probability the true mean lies in this interval.”

“95% of the data falls inside this interval.”

“If I repeated this whole procedure many times, about 95% of the intervals I built would contain the true value.”

The true mean is a fixed number. Your interval either contains it or it does not. The 95% is a property of the procedure — which is exactly why you can go and count it.

2 min. Concede that everyone, including you, speaks the wrong way in practice and it is usually harmless. But the counting exercise at the end only makes sense under the correct reading, so it has to be said once, properly.

Poll: What Is the 95% a Property Of?

You report a 95% confidence interval of $[36.49, 40.18]$ for mean age.

The “95%” is a property of what?

A) This interval — it has a 95% chance of being right
B) The data — 95% of observations fall inside it
C) The procedure — 95% of intervals built this way contain the truth
D) The population — 95% of possible populations fit it
1 min. Expect a solid A share even right after the previous slide; that is normal and worth naming rather than treating as a failure to listen.

Answer: C — The Procedure

The truth is fixed; your interval is the random thing. The confidence level describes how the interval-building recipe behaves over many runs.

Why A is tempting: it is how everyone talks, and the intuition it gives is usually fine. But it makes the last section of this lecture unintelligible — you cannot count how often a fixed statement is true.

The Wall: What Is the SE of a Median?

Chapter 4 said the median is the better summary for skewed data. The Fed reports median household net worth ($192,700) beside the mean ($1.06 million) for exactly that reason. So: how precise is a median?

$$\text{SE}(\text{median}) = \frac{1}{2\, f(\text{median})} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$$

In plain language: the median's precision depends on how densely observations pile up right at the median. A tall narrow peak there means nudging the data barely moves it. A flat region there means it swings.

To use it you need $f(\text{median})$ — the population density at the median. If you knew the population, you would not be estimating anything. This formula exists and is unusable.

3 min. This is the slide the whole first half was building toward. Do not soften it: the formula is real, it is in textbooks, and it requires the one thing you can never have. That is the vacuum the bootstrap fills.

The Bootstrap

If you cannot derive the sampling distribution, simulate it.

The Algorithm, in English Before Any Code

1

Start with your sample of $n$ observations.

2

Draw $n$ observations from it with replacement — that is one bootstrap sample.

3

Compute your statistic on it. Any statistic.

4

Repeat $B$ times — 200 for a standard error, 2,000–10,000 for an interval.

5

The spread of those $B$ values is your estimate of the sampling distribution.

Why with replacement? Without it you would draw the same $n$ observations every time, just shuffled — zero variability. With replacement, each resample holds about 63.2% unique observations. That is the engine.

3 min. Do this with five index cards before showing the slide if you have them. Draw one, write it down, put it BACK, draw again. The physical act of putting the card back is what makes "with replacement" stick.

Common Misconception

“The bootstrap creates new data.”

What it does

Reuses your observations in different multiplicities, to estimate how much your statistic would move across real repeated samples.

What it cannot do

Add information. If your sample is biased (Ch 6), every resample is biased identically — and the interval will be confidently centred on the wrong number.

A tight bootstrap interval around a biased estimate is worse than a wide one. It is wrong, and it looks certain.

2 min. Tie it explicitly back to Meng from the retrieval check. The bootstrap measures variance, never bias. Students who remember only one caveat should remember this one.

Now Watch It Collapse — On Purpose

Lab Step 7: bootstrap the median age of all 32,561 respondents, $B = 2{,}000$.

Sample median age: 37.0
Bootstrap SE:      0.0000

This is not a bug. Ages are integers, $n$ is 32,561, and an enormous number of people are exactly 37. The median is pinned. The bootstrap is correctly reporting that there is no variation to find — and it cannot manufacture what is not there.

3 min. Say "this is not a bug" BEFORE anyone has time to worry, exactly as the lab does. Then ask what would have to change for it to move: fewer observations, or a genuinely continuous variable. Both answers are correct and both are useful.

Pinned, and Not Pinned

Same method, same dataset, same $B$. The difference is entirely in the statistic and how much room it has to move.

2 min. The visual contrast does the teaching. Ask: which of these two would you rather be handed by a colleague? The answer is neither — you would want to know which situation you were in, which is exactly what the bootstrap just told you.

Ask scipy for BCa on That, and It Refuses

result = bootstrap((ages,), np.median, method='BCa', ...)

DegenerateDataWarning: The BCa confidence interval cannot be calculated ...
BCa 95% CI: [nan, nan]

Why. BCa estimates its correction by leaving out one observation at a time and watching the statistic move. Here it never moves — so the correction is $0/0$.

A method that refuses to answer is behaving well. The failure mode to fear is the one that returns a plausible number.

2 min. Worth dwelling on for a professional habit: read the warning, not the number. Most students' instinct on seeing nan is to change a setting until the nan goes away. That instinct is exactly backwards here.

Poll: What Does a Bootstrap SE of 0.0000 Mean?

2,000 resamples of 32,561 integer ages returned one distinct median, and a bootstrap standard error of exactly zero.

What is that telling you?

A) The bootstrap failed — use more resamples
B) The median age is known with certainty

1 min. Expect A and B to split most of the room. Both are instructive wrong answers and both get addressed on the reveal.

Answer: C — The Estimator Does Not Move

Why A is wrong: $B$ is not the problem. Run a million resamples and you get the same single number.

Why B is tempting: the interval really is a single point, so “no uncertainty” sounds fair. But it is a statement about this estimator on this sample, not about the world. We remain genuinely uncertain about the median age of the US workforce; what vanished is the sample median's variability under resampling.

The Two Distributions Students Most Often Confuse

When the Bootstrap Fails

1. Very small samples ($n < 15$). The sample cannot represent the population's shape.

2. Extreme quantiles. The 99.9th percentile needs observations out there to resample.

3. Infinite variance. The bootstrap SE does not converge — use subsampling.

4. Dependent data treated as independent. Time series or clusters need the block or cluster bootstrap.

5. Non-smooth statistics. Max, min, range — each depends on a single observation, so resampling behaves badly.

Notice what is not on this list: bias. The bootstrap has no defence against it at all.

2 min. Ask students to name the chapter each failure connects to — 1 and the bias line to Ch 6, 4 forward to the time-series chapters. The fragment is the one to leave on screen while you move on.

Which Interval?

Percentile, basic, BCa — and why scipy's default is right.

Three Ways to Slice the Bootstrap Distribution

MethodHow it slicesUse it when
PercentileTake the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap valuesQuick exploration
BasicReflect the bootstrap distribution around the sample estimateRarely, by hand
BCaPercentile, then shifted to correct for bias and skewAnything consequential

$$\text{CI}^{\text{pct}}_{95\%} = \left[\hat{\theta}^*_{0.025},\; \hat{\theta}^*_{0.975}\right]$$

In plain language: sort your 2,000 bootstrap values and chop off the outer 2.5% at each end. What is left is the interval. BCa's advantage, without the asymptotics: its coverage error shrinks faster as the sample grows.

2 min. Do not do the O(n^-1) versus O(n^-1/2) notation with this audience; the plain sentence carries it. The operational takeaway is one line: BCa is scipy's default, and that default is right.

For the Mean: the Bootstrap Buys Nothing

   mean  parametric:  92.5%   (Monte Carlo SE 1.3%)
   mean   bootstrap:  91.5%   (Monte Carlo SE 1.4%)

It is nominally behind, by 1.0 point, against a Monte Carlo SE of about 1.3 on each estimate. The gap is smaller than its own error bar. Change the seed and the ordering can flip.

Both under-cover slightly, for the same reason: the percentile bootstrap and the CLT interval are both first-order accurate — their coverage errors shrink at the same rate, and at $n = 50$ on skewed data neither has got there.

The bootstrap is not a repair for a statistic whose formula you already have.

3 min. Say the sentence "that gap is smaller than its own error bar" out loud, in those words. This is the correction the course made after an audit — an earlier version of this material claimed the bootstrap won here, and it does not. Being visibly willing to say so is part of the lesson.

For the Median: 28 Points, and It Is Not Noise

 median  parametric:  66.8%   (Monte Carlo SE 2.4%)
 median   bootstrap:  93.2%   (Monte Carlo SE 1.3%)

The mechanism. On right-skewed data the mean sits well above the median, so an interval centred on the sample mean is aimed at the wrong place — and more data does not re-aim it. The bootstrap interval is built around the statistic actually being asked about.

At scale. A firm running 1,000 experiments a year on a right-skewed metric — revenue per user, session length, time to first purchase — reporting medians against a 95% standard would expect about 50 misses. It would get roughly 330.

3 min. Ask: "would you launch on an interval that is wrong a third of the time?" Then land the real claim of the lecture — the bootstrap did not beat the formula, it went where there was no formula. That distinction is what you want them to be able to say back to you.

The Habit This Is Really Teaching

A simulation is an experiment. Its output is an estimate with noise, like any other.

Quoting “92.5% versus 91.5%” as if it established an ordering, when the SE on each is 1.3, would be exactly the overclaiming this chapter is about.

Every coverage number today came printed next to its Monte Carlo SE. That is not decoration. It is what makes the comparison honest.

Before you call a gap a finding, compare it to its own error.

2 min. This is a recurring lesson in this course and it generalises far past the bootstrap — it is the same discipline you will need for cross-validation scores in Ch 15 and for A/B test readouts in Ch 9. Say that it will come back.

Economist at Work: Netflix's Quantile Bootstrap

Netflix passed 300 million paid memberships at the end of 2024. For quality metrics like play delay — the wait between pressing play and video starting — the distribution is aggressively right-skewed: most users start instantly, a tail suffers. Averages hide exactly the users you most need to see.

So they evaluate quantile treatment effects — the 50th, 75th and 95th percentiles. Quantiles have no closed-form standard error, so they bootstrap.

To make it affordable they compress each metric into (value, count) pairs and bootstrap the counts — cost becomes independent of sample size.

Across 123 historical A/B tests, bootstrap-informed decision rules would have raised cumulative returns on the north-star metric by an estimated 33%. Note why they bootstrap: not because it beats a formula, but because for a quantile there is no formula to beat.

Netflix Tech Blog, “Reimagining Experimentation Analysis”; Chou et al. (2025), KDD Best Paper, arXiv:2502.08763.

2 min. The last sentence is the one that matters and it is the whole lecture in a line. If you are short on time, cut the compression detail and keep it.

Key Takeaways

SD describes people; SE describes your estimate. Only the SE shrinks with $n$, and only at rate $\sqrt{n}$.

The CLT's arrival time depends on skew, not on 30. For economists' data, “large enough” can mean hundreds.

The bootstrap is for statistics with no formula — medians, quantiles, ratios. Not a repair for one that has a formula: 92.5% vs 91.5%, inside the noise.

A zero bootstrap SE is an answer, not a failure. So is nan from BCa. Read the warning, not the number.

Check a gap against its own error before calling it a finding. Every coverage number today came with a Monte Carlo SE for exactly that reason.

The bootstrap did not make the mean interval better. It made the median interval possible — 93.2% against 66.8%.

2 min. The closing fragment is the sentence to end on. It is worth more than the version that is not true, and saying so models the thing the course is actually trying to teach.
