Why Not Just Run OLS?

Section 17.1 • Two defects, one cosmetic and one structural

The Linear Probability Model

Code the outcome 1 for recession and 0 otherwise, regress it on the spread, and read the fitted values as probabilities. This is still used, and it has two defects.

Cosmetic

A straight line does not stop at 0 or 1. Extend it far enough and it predicts negative probabilities.

Structural

The variance of a binary variable is \(p(1-p)\) — it depends on its own mean, so the errors are heteroscedastic by construction.

You can patch the second with HC3, exactly as Chapter 14 taught. You cannot patch a negative probability.

2 min.

The heteroscedasticity point is a one-line callback and should be presented as “you already know this one” — the novelty is only that here it is not a property of the data but a property of the outcome variable, unavoidable in every binary regression ever run.

  • Do not linger; the figure on the next slide is the argument.

Not a Hypothetical — 84 Negative Probabilities

Fitted on the real Federal Reserve series, the linear probability model predicts values from −0.067 to 0.242.

84
months assigned a NEGATIVE probability of recession

Not a contrived example. The ordinary result of fitting a line to a variable that takes only two values.

The sigmoid cannot do this. Not because it is better tuned — because it is impossible by construction.

3 min.

Run this live if you have the notebook open; the objection is usually taught as a hypothetical and it is not one.

  • The pale grey band along the bottom is the 487 quiet months and the terracotta points at the top are the 36 recession months — point at that imbalance now, because it is the fact that drives the entire second half of the hour.

The Fix: Keep the Line, Bend the Output

Keep the linear combination of features. Pass it through a function that maps the whole real line into \((0, 1)\).

$$\hat{P}(Y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p)}}$$

In words. The expression inside the exponential is the linear predictor — exactly the \(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) of Chapter 13. The sigmoid wrapped around it is S-shaped: very negative linear predictors map near 0, very positive ones near 1, and zero maps to exactly 0.5.

Nothing about the features changed. Only what we do with their weighted sum.

4 min.

Draw the sigmoid by hand and mark where the linear predictor is minus three, zero and plus three — students remember the drawing, not the formula.

  • Emphasise that the coefficients enter exactly as they always have: this is not a new model of the world, it is the same linear predictor with a wrapper that makes its output legal.

Linear in Log-Odds

Section 17.2 • Three scales, three interpretations, one very common error

The Rearrangement That Explains Everything

Take the sigmoid and solve for the linear predictor:

$$\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p$$

Odds \(= p/(1-p)\). A probability of 0.2 is odds of 0.25 — “one to four.”

The logit is the log of the odds. It is the scale on which this model is linear.

Logistic regression is a linear model. It is linear in the log-odds, not in the probability — and that one fact governs every interpretation you will ever make of it.

3 min.

Do the algebra on the board — it is three lines and it turns “linear in log-odds” from an assertion into a derived fact.

  • Students accept the sentence much more readily once they have watched the sigmoid invert.
  • Then say: a coefficient is therefore a change in log-odds, which nobody on earth thinks in, so we need a second scale.

Exponentiate, and It Becomes Sayable

$$e^{\beta_j} = \text{the factor by which the odds multiply, per one-unit increase in } x_j$$ 
coefficient on spread: -0.8354   (p = 4.88e-08)
odds ratio: exp(-0.8354) = 0.434

A curve one percentage point steeper multiplies the odds of a recession within twelve months by 0.434 — it reduces the odds by 57%.

Note what that is not. It is not a 57% reduction in probability, and it is not 84 percentage points of anything. Odds and probabilities coincide only when both are small.

3 min.

Work it out loud: minus 0.8354, exponentiate, 0.434, “a 57% reduction in odds.”

  • Then have the class say the sentence back before you move on — the poll on the next slide tests exactly this and it is worth their having attempted the words first.
  • This is the sentence you would actually say to a risk committee, so treat it as a performance, not a calculation.

B) A one-point wider spread lowers the probability of recession by 57%

1 min.

Students click to vote; results appear on the next slide.

  • Expect a real split between A and C, and expect some of the room to pick B because they heard “57%” thirty seconds ago and did not hear which scale it was on.
  • That is the point of asking.

Answer: C — the Odds, Not the Probability

A reads a log-odds coefficient as a percentage of probability — the single most common error in applied classification work. B has the right arithmetic on the wrong scale. D swaps the coefficient and its exponential: the log-odds fall by 0.8354, and it is the odds that multiply by 0.434.

This exact confusion is a standard screening question in data-science interviews. Interviewers use it because the wrong answer gets stated confidently.

2 min.

If the A share is large, say so out loud and do not soften it — a confident wrong answer in an interview is worse than an admitted uncertainty, and this is the cheapest possible place to learn that.

  • Then set up the next slide: if a coefficient is not a change in probability, what IS the change in probability?

The Effect on the Probability Is Not Constant

In OLS a coefficient is a constant marginal effect — one more room adds the same amount to predicted value everywhere. Here it is not:

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j} = \beta_j \, p(1-p)$$

In words. The multiplier \(p(1-p)\) is largest at \(p = 0.5\) and vanishes as \(p\) approaches 0 or 1. A given change in the spread moves the probability a great deal for a month that is genuinely uncertain, and hardly at all for one that is already safe.

That is economically sensible, not a mathematical inconvenience. If a recession is already near-certain, more bad news barely changes the forecast. The model's diminishing sensitivity at the extremes matches how information actually works.

3 min.

Students find non-constant marginal effects unsettling until they hear the economic reading, which lands immediately — so give the economics before the algebra if the room looks lost.

  • The practical consequence, and the one that matters for their careers: when you report to a non-technical audience, compute the marginal effect AT A STATED POINT. “At the current spread, a further half-point inversion raises the probability by six points” is usable.
  • A coefficient nobody can convert in their head is not.

Fitting, and Reading the Output

Section 17.3 • No closed form, two libraries, and a variable that adds nothing

There Is No Formula — and It Barely Matters

OLS has a closed form. Logistic regression does not: the estimate is found numerically, by maximum likelihood.

$$L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$$

In words. Each month contributes \(p_i\) if a recession followed and \(1 - p_i\) if none did. Maximising this picks the coefficients under which the data we actually saw is most probable.

One optimum, always. The log-likelihood is globally concave, so there are no local maxima to get trapped in and two analysts get the same answer to many decimals.

But it can fail to converge — and failure is diagnostic: a feature that separates the classes perfectly, wildly different scales, or near-collinearity. Chapter 14's problem, inherited.

A convergence warning is information about your design matrix, not a nuisance to suppress.

3 min.

Keep this at the level of “which coefficients make what we saw most probable.”

  • Do not derive anything.
  • Students sometimes over-read “no closed form” as fragility; the correction is the left box — convex problem, one answer, milliseconds.
  • If someone asks about standard errors, say they come from the curvature of the log-likelihood and that we take them from statsmodels rather than deriving them here.

Two Libraries, Two Purposes — and a Trap

# Inference — standard errors, z-statistics, p-values
logit = sm.Logit(y, X).fit()

# Prediction — the scikit-learn pipeline ecosystem
clf = LogisticRegression(penalty=None, solver='lbfgs').fit(X, y)

penalty=None is not optional if you want to match statsmodels. scikit-learn applies \(L_2\) regularization by default.

Sensible for prediction. Surprising for an economist who expects maximum likelihood and gets a penalised estimate without being told. And worse: the strength parameter is C, which is the inverse of the penalty — small C means heavy regularization. Chapter 16 catalogued three conflicting uses of “alpha”; C is a fourth, running the opposite way to all of them.

2 min.

Say “penalty equals None” out loud twice — the chapter asks for it explicitly and the reason is that students will produce different coefficients in the lab and assume they have made a mistake.

  • Tell them now: if statsmodels and scikit-learn disagree on a logistic fit, the first thing to check is not your data, it is the penalty argument.

Why Accuracy Is the Wrong Score

Section 17.4 • The chapter. The floor is the base rate.

The Reason Is Arithmetic

'always no recession':  accuracy 93.1%
logistic at p >= 0.5:   accuracy 93.1%
recessions caught:      0 of 36

Only 6.9% of months precede a recession, so a model that says “no” every time is right 93.1% of the time for free.

Accuracy's floor is set by the base rate — and with imbalanced classes that floor is high enough to disguise total uselessness.

And 0.5 is a threshold nobody chose. It is a library default, inherited from a two-class problem with a 50% base rate. With a 6.9% base rate the predicted probability rarely climbs above 0.5, so the rule almost never fires.

3 min.

Two separate failures are stacked here and students conflate them, so separate them explicitly: the SCORE is wrong (accuracy) and the CUTOFF is wrong (0.5).

  • Fixing either one alone helps; the chapter fixes both, and the next twenty minutes are one each.
  • Write “base rate 6.9%” somewhere visible and leave it up.

And the Rarer the Event, the Worse It Gets

Application Typical base rate Accuracy of “always no”
Recession within 12 months~7%93%
Consumer loan default2–5%95–98%
Card fraud~0.2%99.8%
Rare disease screening<1%>99%

Prediction is valuable precisely when an event is rare and consequential — which is precisely when accuracy stops working. Accuracy is least informative exactly where classification matters most.

2 min.

The fraud row is the one quoted in interviews: a model that flags nothing achieves 99.8% accuracy, and a great many production systems have been signed off on figures like it.

  • Nobody needs a model to predict a coin flip — that is the sentence that makes the last line land, so say it before you say the last line.

Scored Two Ways

By accuracy, a tie: a rule with no inputs at all matches the Federal Reserve's own indicator.

By the Brier score, the logistic model beats both the do-nothing rule and a model that just reports the base rate every month.

The information was there the whole time. Accuracy destroyed it, by collapsing a well-ordered set of probabilities into a yes/no call at an arbitrary cutoff.

ROC-AUC 0.811: given one month that preceded a recession and one that did not, the model ranks them correctly about 81% of the time.

3 min.

This is the turn of the whole hour and it must not be rushed.

  • Say it in this order: the model is good, the metric was bad.
  • NOT “the model is bad.”
  • The middle Brier bar, 0.0641, is a model that reports 6.9% every single month and never looks at the spread — that is the floor the data imposes, and the logistic model at 0.0615 is genuinely below it.
  • AUC 0.811 is developed properly in Chapter 18; take it here as a discrimination number and do not derive it.

Accuracy is \((TP + TN)/n\) — it adds the two diagonal cells and divides. With a 6.9% base rate the true negatives dominate that sum, so accuracy is almost entirely a measure of how well you identify quiet months. 487 out of 523 is 93.1%.

Any single number computed from this table discards information. Accuracy discards the part you care about.

3 min.

Once they look at four cells rather than one summary, the natural questions change: of the recessions that happened, how many did we catch?

  • Of our alarms, how many were real?
  • Those are two different ratios, they trade against each other, and Chapter 18 develops them as recall and precision.
  • Name them now so the handoff is not a surprise, but do not define them formally today.

Accuracy Is an Improper Score

A scoring rule is strictly proper if it is uniquely optimised by reporting your true beliefs. Brier and log-loss are strictly proper. Accuracy is not — it can be improved by misreporting: shading a genuine 0.45 up to a confident 1 pays off whenever the outcome occurs, and accuracy cannot tell a well-calibrated 0.45 from an overconfident 0.95.

$$\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat{p}_i - y_i\right)^2$$

In words. Squared error, applied to probabilities. It rewards being both discriminating (separating the classes) and calibrated (when you say 30%, it happens 30% of the time). Accuracy rewards only being on the right side of a cutoff.

Score classifiers with Brier or log-loss. Report accuracy as a summary for stakeholders, never as an objective to optimise — and never without the base rate beside it.

3 min.

Keep “strictly proper” informal — the definition they need is the one on the slide: you cannot game it by lying about your confidence.

  • If a student asks why we do not just optimise accuracy directly, the answer is on this slide and it is worth repeating: because the thing that maximises accuracy on this data is a model that never fires.

A Precise Footnote to Chapter 15

Chapter 15 gave you a floor: \(\sigma^2\), the irreducible error, which no model could beat. Classification has a floor too.

With a base rate of 6.9%, a model that only ever reports 6.9% and never looks at the spread scores \(\bar{p}(1-\bar{p}) = \mathbf{0.0641}\) on the Brier score. That floor is set by the data, not by the model — and our logistic fit at 0.0615 is genuinely below it.

Be careful with this. That is a statement about squared error applied to probabilities, which is where the argument is legitimate. It is not a bias–variance decomposition of classification loss: the clean decomposition is squared-error only, and there is no tidy analogue for 0/1 loss.

2 min.

Chapter 15's own closing slide said exactly this — that the decomposition assumed squared error and there is no clean analogue for categorical outcomes — so this is a promise being kept, not a new caveat.

  • If a student asks “so what IS the bias-variance tradeoff for a classifier?”, the honest answer is that the intuition transfers (more flexibility, more variance) and the identity does not.
  • Do not invent one at the board.

Answer: C — 80.1%, the Worst in the Table

Across the five rows of the threshold table, accuracy falls at every step as the model catches more recessions. Catching 26 of 36 costs 94 false alarms over forty years, and every one of them is an accuracy point lost.

Any procedure that tunes a threshold to maximise accuracy on imbalanced data will drive you back to the top row and a model that never fires.

  • Across the five printed rows it falls at every step, and the direction is the point.
  • If a sharp student notices the wiggle in the figure on the next slide, say exactly that — do not claim a monotonicity the picture contradicts.

And It Is Just Arithmetic, Once You Name the Costs

If a false negative costs \(C_{FN}\) and a false positive costs \(C_{FP}\), expected cost is minimised at

$$t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

In words. The optimal threshold depends only on the ratio of the costs — not on their level, not on the sample size, not on the model. If missing a recession is nine times worse than a false alarm, \(t^* = 0.1\): the bottom row of the table, derived rather than guessed.

Lending. A default costs the outstanding principal — thousands. A declined good applicant costs one loan's margin — tens. Ratios of 50:1 are ordinary, implying thresholds near 0.02.

Criminal justice. A false positive means detaining someone who would not have reoffended. The ratio is contested, not obvious — a political question wearing a technical costume.

3 min.

Derive it if you have the board: expected cost equals the false-negative count times its cost plus the false-positive count times its cost, and setting the marginal trade to zero gives the ratio.

  • Two lines, and it converts an apparently arbitrary choice into an economic one.
  • The criminal-justice box is where a value judgement gets made without anyone taking responsibility for it — accepting a default IS taking a position, it is just an unexamined one.

Think Like an Economist

Before choosing a threshold, write down what each error costs — in dollars, or in whatever the decision actually trades.

Most classification work skips this and inherits 0.5 from a library, which silently asserts that false positives and false negatives cost exactly the same. That assertion is almost never true, and it is one an economist is unusually well equipped to interrogate.

The cost ratio is the modelling assumption. The threshold is just its arithmetic consequence.

Someone chooses the threshold. If the analyst does not do it explicitly, the library has already done it for them.

1 min.

Deliver this as the section's closing line, slowly.

  • This is the single most transferable thing in the chapter and the thing that distinguishes an economist from a technician on a modelling team: naming the costs is not a statistical task and the statistics cannot do it for you.

When the Feature Stops Meaning What It Meant

The yield-curve model preceded every U.S. recession for half a century with very few false alarms. Then, from July 2022 to late 2024, the curve inverted for roughly 784 days — the longest inversion in modern history — and nothing arrived. Real GDP growth held at 2.9% in 2023 and 2.8% in 2024.

Answer: D — the Model Was Not Broken

A, B and C are the three tools of the last three chapters — and none of them is the answer. That is the point of asking. Every diagnostic passes. The likelihood was correctly maximised, the coefficient correctly estimated, the probabilities correctly computed.

This is a structural break in the relationship between a feature and the outcome. No amount of better classification technique detects it, and finding it requires knowing what a term premium is.

What Actually Happened

A ten-year yield decomposes into expected future short rates plus a term premium — the extra compensation investors demand for holding duration risk.

Years of quantitative easing compressed that premium dramatically; New York Fed estimates put it under 0.8 percentage points of a 4.77% ten-year yield.

The curve inverted partly because the term premium had been squeezed toward zero, not because markets expected a contraction. The spread was numerically the same. What it indicated was not.

It is not overfitting, leakage, miscalibration or a bad threshold — the four failures this chapter and the last two have taught you to look for.

A model is a claim that a historical relationship will continue. Deciding whether it will is a question about the economy, not about the model.

3 min.

Keep the decomposition qualitative for this course — the ACM estimates exist and are published, and the fact that matters is that the tool existed long before the inversion and using it required someone to ask what the spread was made of.

  • This is the argument for economists in this work, stated as plainly as the book can state it.
  • Deliver the last line slowly and let it be the thesis of the hour.

How You Would Actually Catch It

Undetectable from performance metrics — but not undetectable. Three habits, and all three are economics rather than statistics.

1

Decompose the feature. The spread is expected short rates plus a term premium, and they move independently. Any composite indicator has this property, and watching the components reveals when the composite is being driven by an unusual part.

2

Ask what mechanism the model encodes. “Inverted curve predicts recession” is a correlation. The mechanism is that markets expecting a downturn bid up long bonds in anticipation of rate cuts. Written that way, the question becomes testable.

3

Track the regime, not just the residuals. A model's errors look fine until suddenly they do not, and by then you have been wrong for two years. Ask whether the conditions that made the feature work still hold — you can check that continuously.

None of these is a metric you can compute from the fitted object. They require knowing why the model works.

2 min.

Habit 3 is the one to press: the relevant monitoring question can be answered long before any outcome data arrives to prove you wrong.

  • In 2022 you could have asked whether the term premium was in its historical range and got an answer immediately — you did not have to wait two years for the recession that never came.

Economist at Work — The NY Fed's Model

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York has published a term-spread recession probability for decades. It is deliberately parsimonious — one variable, a logistic link — descending from Estrella and Mishkin (1998).

Parsimony is the point. A model with one interpretable input, published monthly for thirty years, is auditable in a way a hundred-feature ensemble never is.

From mid-2022 it began flashing, and forecasters who took it at face value called for a downturn through 2023 and into 2024. It did not come. The Fed has not discarded the model, and neither should you.

The lesson: a feature's predictive content is a property of the monetary regime that produced it, and regimes change. Asking periodically whether an input still means what it meant is the maintenance task that separates a model in production from a model in a paper.

2 min.

Estrella and Mishkin 1998 in the Review of Economics and Statistics is the citation, with Campbell Harvey's dissertation before it.

  • The phrase to leave them with is “continuous feature validation” — it is a job description as much as a technique, and it is one economists are hired to do on modelling teams.

Beyond Two Categories

Many economic questions are not binary: which of four transport modes a commuter chooses, which of five ratings a bond receives, whether a firm expands, contracts or holds steady.

$$P(Y = j \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j}}{\sum_{m=1}^{J} e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_m}}$$

In words. Multinomial logistic regression fits one linear predictor per category and normalises them into probabilities. One category is fixed as the baseline and the rest are read relative to it — Chapter 13's reference-category logic, moved from a predictor to the outcome. This is McFadden's conditional logit, and it won a Nobel Prize.

Its famous weakness. The red bus / blue bus problem: introduce an identical second bus and the model says people start driving more, because it cannot know the two buses are near-substitutes.

For ordered outcomes — bond ratings, survey scales — ordered logit exploits the ordering with one coefficient vector and a set of cutpoints. Usually the right choice.

2 min, awareness only.

Nothing here is examinable today. The one sentence that matters: do not assume the world is binary because the textbook chapter was.

  • If a student asks about the red-bus problem, the fix is nested or mixed logit, at a cost in complexity, and that is as far as this course goes.

A Workflow, and What Comes Next

Section 17.7 • Six steps, in this order

A Classification Workflow

1

Check the base rate before anything else. One line, and it determines how you must evaluate everything downstream.

2

Fit on the true distribution. Do not resample to balance the classes — it changes the base rate the model estimates and inflates every predicted probability. Use class_weight='balanced' if the loss needs reweighting.

3

Score with a proper rule. Brier or log-loss, beside a discrimination measure. Report accuracy only next to the base rate.

4

Choose the threshold from costs. \(t^* = C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\). The arithmetic is trivial; naming the costs is the hard part and it is not a statistical task.

5

Check calibration if the probability is used as a number — if it feeds an expected-loss or capital formula, being well-ordered is not enough.

6

Ask whether the features still mean what they meant. Schedule the question rather than waiting for the model to embarrass you.

2 min.

Lead with step 1: it is a habit rather than a technique and it costs nothing.

  • Analysts who skip it discover it three weeks later when a stakeholder asks why the model never flags anything.
  • Step 2 will meet resistance because many students have been taught SMOTE as best practice — that is the next slide, so do not fight it here.

Key Takeaways

1. The linear probability model produces impossible predictions — 84 negative probabilities on real Federal Reserve data — and heteroscedastic errors by construction. The logit link fixes both by modelling log-odds linearly.

2. A logistic coefficient is a change in log-odds: −0.8354 means the odds multiply by 0.434, a 57% reduction in odds, and the effect on the probability depends on where you start.

3. Accuracy is an improper score bounded below by the base rate. 93.1%, matching a model that never predicts anything, while catching 0 of 36 — yet Brier 0.0615 and AUC 0.811 show the information is genuinely there.

4. 0.5 is a library default, not a decision. At 0.10 the model catches 26 of 36 at the worst accuracy in the table, and the right cutoff is \(C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\) — a question about costs.

5. A model can be mathematically sound and still fail if a feature stops meaning what it meant — the record 784-day inversion, sustained 31–42% probabilities, no recession, because QE had compressed the term premium.
