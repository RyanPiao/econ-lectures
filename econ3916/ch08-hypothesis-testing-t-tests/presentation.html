1  •  The Logic of Testing

What you assume, what you measure, and the conditional everyone flips

Two Hypotheses — and Which One You Assume

Not "does job training help?" but "is the earnings gap larger than sampling variability alone would produce?"

$$H_0: \mu_{\text{treatment}} - \mu_{\text{control}} = 0 \qquad H_1: \mu_{\text{treatment}} - \mu_{\text{control}} \neq 0$$

Interpretation: the null says the gap you can see in your sample is what random sampling would have produced anyway, with nothing real underneath it.

The courtroom. Null = “innocent.” Data = the evidence. Small p-value = “guilty beyond reasonable doubt.” And an acquittal does not prove innocence — it says the evidence was insufficient.

You never accept the null. You fail to reject it.

3 min. The courtroom analogy is the one students keep. Push the acquittal point hard — "not guilty" and "innocent" are different verdicts, and so are "fail to reject" and "no effect". That distinction is worth two poll questions later.

The p-Value

$$p = P\big(|T| \geq |t_{\text{obs}}| \;\big|\; H_0 \text{ is true}\big)$$

WHERE

\(T\) = the statistic across all possible samples
\(t_{\text{obs}}\) = the one your data produced
absolute values → two-sided

INTERPRETATION

\(p = 0.03\): if the null were true and you re-ran this study many times, you would see something this extreme about 3% of the time. That is all it measures.

\(\alpha\) is fixed before you look at the data. 0.05 by convention. It is a decision rule, not a measure of truth — it controls how often you falsely reject in the long run.

3 min. Read the conditional bar out loud as the word "given". One line only on one-sided tests: it concentrates all rejection probability in one tail, more powerful there, blind the other way; default two-sided in exploratory economics. Do not spend more than 20 seconds on that.

Poll 1 — A Study Reports p = 0.03

Which statement is correct?

A) There is a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true
B) There is a 97% chance the alternative is true
C) If the null were true, data this extreme would occur about 3% of the time
D) The effect is large enough to act on
Students click to vote on their phones. Give it 60 seconds. Expect a healthy split between A and C — A is the single most common error in statistics and some of the room will have been taught it. Do not signal the answer.

Answer: C — and A is the Prosecutor's Fallacy

A and B are the same error twice. The p-value is \(P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\). A and B claim \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\). Flipping that conditional needs Bayes' theorem and a prior for \(H_0\) — which frequentist inference never supplies.

Say it out loud: “5% of people wear a red shirt. This suspect wears a red shirt. Is there a 5% chance he is innocent?” Nobody says yes — and option A is that sentence with different nouns.

The American Statistical Association published a formal statement in 2016 for the sole purpose of stamping on this error (Wasserstein & Lazar).

3 min including the debrief. Run the red-shirt line verbally before you explain anything — it does the work faster than the notation does. D is a different mistake entirely; tell them we spend Section 5 on it and move on.

2  •  Two Errors, and Their Prices

Which mistake is worse is not a statistical question

Every Decision Rule Fails in Two Directions

Type I (\(\alpha\)) — you announce an effect that is not there. You set this rate yourself.

Type II (\(\beta\)) — you miss an effect that is there. \(1-\beta\) is power, and Section 6 is about buying it.

You choose \(\alpha\) with a keystroke. \(\beta\) is chosen for you by your sample size, the true effect, and the noise.

2 min. The asymmetry in who chooses what is the point of this slide: alpha is a decision, beta is a consequence. Students who understand that sentence do not later ask "can I just lower alpha to get more power?"

Which Error Is Worse? An Economic Question

Clinical trials

A false positive approves a useless or dangerous drug. People are harmed. So trials often run \(\alpha = 0.01\) or stricter.

A button-colour A/B test

A false positive ships a harmless feature. The cost is almost nothing. \(\alpha = 0.05\) or even \(0.10\) is defensible.

Think like an economist. Before any test: what does a false positive cost me relative to a false negative? In fraud detection, missing real fraud is catastrophic — so you accept more false alarms. \(\alpha\) should encode that asymmetry, not default blindly to 0.05.

2 min. Resist the urge to give a rule. The whole point is that there is no rule — there is a cost ratio, and it is domain knowledge, not statistics. This sets up the discussion on the next slide.

4  •  Two Samples: Welch's, and the Three Assumptions

The test you will actually use — and the two problems it does not touch

The LaLonde Question

In the 1970s the U.S. Department of Labor funded the National Supported Work Demonstration — a randomized job-training program for disadvantaged workers. Did the training raise earnings?

LaLonde's 1986 analysis became one of the most replicated studies in economics. It begins with a two-sample t-test.

Data Snapshot — LaLonde NSW. N = 445 (185 treated, 260 control). Outcome: real 1978 earnings. Dehejia & Wahba (2002) version, served by NBER. You run this yourself in Lab 8.

This dataset returns in Ch 9 (as an A/B test), Ch 10 (DAGs) and Ch 24 (double machine learning) — each time with sharper tools.

2 min. Emphasise "randomized" — and then say we are deliberately NOT using that fact today. The t-test does not know about randomisation; next week's chapter is about what randomisation buys you on top. That is the Ch 9 bridge.

Look at the Data First

Both distributions are heavily right-skewed with a pile-up at zero — exactly the shape Chapter 4 warned you about, where the mean is a poor summary.

What protects the t-test here is not normal data. It is \(n > 30\) in both groups, so Chapter 7's CLT applies to the sampling distribution of the mean — the only thing that has to be normal.

Note the standard deviations: $7,867 against $5,484. Not remotely equal.

3 min. The distinction between "the data is normal" and "the sampling distribution of the mean is normal" is the single most valuable sentence in this section — say it twice. Point at the box plot medians ($4,232 and $3,139) sitting well below both means: that is the skew, visible.

Welch's t-Statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

WHERE

\(\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2\) sample means
\(s_1^2, s_2^2\) sample variances
\(n_1, n_2\) sample sizes

INTERPRETATION

Top = the difference you observed. Bottom = how much that difference would bounce around across repeated samples. So \(t\) counts how many standard errors from zero your result sits.

Rule of thumb: always use Welch's. When variances are equal it agrees with Student's to several decimals. When they are not, Student's misleads. In scipy: equal_var=False.

3 min. Read the denominator aloud as "the standard error of the difference" before you say anything about degrees of freedom. Welch adjusts df downward via Satterthwaite when variances differ, which makes the p-value more conservative — one sentence, do not derive it.

The Three Assumptions

1. Independence

Violated by classmates, repeated measures, spatial clustering. Effective \(n\) shrinks, the standard error is too small, and the p-value looks better than it is.

2. Normality

Of the sampling distribution of the mean, not the data. \(n>30\) per group usually delivers it. For small \(n\) it bites — recall the mean's breakdown point of \(1/n\) from Ch 4.

3. Equal variance

Student's assumes \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\). Wages, prices and returns almost never do. Ch 4 diagnostic: if one group's IQR is more than twice the other's, do not assume it.

2 min. Do NOT say which one Welch's fixes — the poll on the next slide is built on that. If a student asks, tell them to hold the answer for 60 seconds.

Poll 3 — What Does Welch's Actually Fix?

The LaLonde earnings data is strongly right-skewed, the two groups have very different variances, and everyone was recruited through the same handful of local programs. You switch to Welch's t-test. Which problem have you solved?

A) All three — Welch's is the robust t-test
B) The unequal variances only
C) The skewness only
D) The dependence only
45 seconds. A is the popular wrong answer and it is worth naming as such — "robust" is doing a lot of unearned work in that sentence.

Answer: B — Variance, and Nothing Else

Welch's repairs assumption 3 and nothing else.

Skewness is handled by sample size — or by trimming, permutation, or a bootstrap test (Ch 7's machinery).

Which assumption is hardest to check? Most say normality — but you can plot that. Independence is the hard one: you usually cannot see it in the data at all. You have to know how the data was made. That is Chapter 6, returning.

5  •  How Big?

The p-value answers “is it real?” — nothing answers “does it matter?” unless you ask

A p-Value That Answered the Wrong Half

2023: the FDA grants traditional approval to Leqembi (lecanemab) for Alzheimer's. The Phase 3 Clarity AD trial enrolled 1,795 patients and reported an effect on the CDR-SB cognitive scale with \(p < 0.001\).

0.45

points, on an 18-point scale

A difference most clinicians struggle to detect in a patient. The drug also carries real risks — brain swelling and bleeding (ARIA) in a meaningful fraction of patients.

A microscopic p-value confirmed the difference was not noise. It said nothing about whether the difference mattered.

2 min. Reveal 0.45 before the interpretation and let the room do the arithmetic against 18. The null was soundly rejected and that is simply the wrong question to have asked alone.

Cohen's d

$$d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_{\text{pooled}}}, \qquad s_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}}$$

INTERPRETATION

d measures the gap between the groups in standard deviations. Because it divides by spread rather than by \(\sqrt{n}\), it does not grow just because you collected more data.

0.2

small

0.5

medium

0.8

large

Rough guides, not thresholds — a “small” effect on lifetime earnings can still be a lot of money.

2 min. The sentence that matters is "it does not grow just because you collected more data" — contrast directly with the t-statistic, whose denominator has the square root of n in it. That contrast is the whole reason both numbers exist.

So How Big Was $1,794?

Pooled std:  $6,579.54
Cohen's d:   0.2727

(pingouin agrees: 0.2727)

The $1,794 gap is about 0.27 standard deviations of earnings — small by the conventional benchmarks: just past the 0.2 line, well short of 0.5.

At $500 per participant, a $1,794 gain is an excellent return.

At $10,000 per participant, it is not.

Statistical significance ≠ practical significance. With enough data any non-zero effect becomes significant — ten million users can detect a 0.001% change in click-through at \(p<0.01\). The question is never “is the effect exactly zero?” It never is. The question is “is it big enough to act on?”

3 min. Say the punchline explicitly: the p-value tells you the difference is real, the effect size and the price tell you whether it matters. Neither number is optional. Report an effect size beside every p-value for the rest of your career.

6  •  Power: the Null Worth Trusting

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — unless you were powered to find it

Statistical Power

$$\text{Power} = 1 - \beta = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ is true})$$

Interpretation: the probability your study actually catches a real effect. Convention: aim for \(\geq 0.80\).

Power has four levers. Fix three, solve for the fourth:

Effect size

bigger is easier

Sample size

more data, less noise

\(\alpha\)

stricter costs power

Variability

noisier needs more

2 min. Point at the alpha box and connect back to Section 2: this is the price of the strict alpha a clinical trial chooses. Nothing is free — buying protection from Type I error spends Type II error.

Power Is Not an Abstraction — It Is That Spike

No effect → p-values come out flat. Every value equally likely, and exactly \(\alpha\) of them fall under 0.05 by construction.

Real effect → the distribution collapses toward zero. The share under 0.05 is the power — 71.2% here, at d = 0.5 with 50 per group.

2 min. 2,000 simulated t-tests in each panel; you rebuild this yourself in the Lab 8 Extension. The left panel is the single best argument for why multiple comparisons are dangerous, and you will reuse it in twelve minutes: flat means one in twenty lands under 0.05 no matter what.

How Much Data Do You Need?

$$n \approx \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \,(s_1^2 + s_2^2)}{\delta^2}$$

WHERE

\(z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96\) at \(\alpha=0.05\)  •  \(z_\beta = 0.84\) for 80% power  •  \(\delta\) = the minimum detectable effect you care about

\(\delta\) is squared, in the denominator.
Halve the effect you want to catch and you quadruple the data you need.

That one fact explains most of the underpowered literature in the social sciences.

2 min. Do not derive anything. Ask them to look at where delta sits and say what happens when it halves — someone will get "four times" and that is the whole slide. This is also why power analysis must happen BEFORE collection: discovering you were underpowered afterwards is too late.

The Economics of Experimentation

To reach 80% power, per group:

d = 0.8  →  26

d = 0.5  →  64

d = 0.273  →  212

d = 0.2  →  394

Four times the effect size, one-fifteenth the data.

Poll 4 — The Colleague at Your Desk

7  •  Multiplicity and the Replication Crisis

Episode 4: the finding that was never there

Test Twenty Things and Something Will Light Up

$$P(\text{at least one false positive}) = 1 - (1-\alpha)^m = 1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64$$

Interpretation: with 20 independent tests and no real effect anywhere, you have a 64% chance of finding at least one “significant” result. With 100 tests: 99.4%.

Remember the flat histogram from Section 6 — under the null, one p-value in twenty lands below 0.05 by construction. Run enough tests and that is not a risk. It is arithmetic.

Netflix, KDD 2025 best paper. At thousands of concurrent experiments, dozens of false positives per cycle are certain. Blindly shipping every “significant winner” delivered 20–40% less aggregate impact than the experiments promised — so they now use 90% of units to select a winner and the remaining 10% to measure it. Chapter 6's train-test logic, applied to business decisions.

2 min. Tie back to the left panel of the p-value figure explicitly — students who saw flat-means-uniform two sections ago get this instantly. Netflix gets one slide only; Chapter 7 already spent a full section on them.

Two Corrections

Bonferroni

Test each hypothesis at \(\alpha/m\). With 20 tests: \(p < 0.0025\).

Controls the family-wise error rate — the chance of even one false positive. Very conservative.

Benjamini-Hochberg

Rank the p-values; find the largest \(k\) with \(p_{(k)} \leq \frac{k}{m}\alpha\); reject the first \(k\).

Controls the false discovery rate — the expected share of your discoveries that are wrong.

In English. Bonferroni says “I will tolerate no false findings at all, and I accept that I will miss real ones.” BH says “I accept that maybe 5% of what I report is wrong, in exchange for finding much more of what is real.”

2 min. Show BH as a threshold, not as a ranking algorithm — the step-up procedure is a lab exercise, not a lecture derivation. The two English sentences are what students should leave with.

The deeper point is that neither correction can tell you whether five was the right number of tests to count — that is a pre-data decision, and it is the one that actually protects you.

Why Published Findings Fail

Ioannidis (2005) argued mathematically, not empirically, that most published research findings are false: low base rates of true effects, power of 50–80%, and publication bias toward positive results together mean that among \(p<0.05\) results, more than half can be wrong.

Then the evidence arrived.

of 100 psychology experiments replicated
Open Science Collaboration, 2015

61%

of 18 AER / QJE studies replicated — at ~66% of the original effect size
Camerer et al., 2016

The Camel: p-Hacking's Fingerprint

Brodeur, Cook & Heyes (2020) examined 21,000 hypothesis tests across 25 economics journals.

They found a two-humped “camel” in the distribution of z-statistics: a dip just above \(z=1.64\), a spike just past \(z=1.96\).

Test statistics that should have landed just short of the line somehow migrated across it.

Figure is a schematic of the reported shape — not a plot of their 21,000 tests.

2 min. Ask "why would z-statistics cluster just past 1.96 but thin out just below it?" and let them answer. They get to "because people kept trying until they crossed the line" within about fifteen seconds. Misconception: “p-hacking is fraud.” Mostly it is not. Which controls, how to treat outliers, which subgroups to examine, when to stop collecting — each is a researcher degree of freedom. Try ten specifications and report the one under 0.05 and your true Type I rate is far above 5%, with no intent to deceive at any point. Pre-registration is the defence.

Chapter 8 in a Nutshell

Hypothesis testing is a decision framework, not a truth machine. A small p-value says the data would be surprising under the null — it does not prove the alternative, and it says nothing about whether the effect matters.

The t-test counts how many standard errors the difference sits from zero. Use Welch's by default — and know it repairs unequal variance and nothing else.

Cohen's d answers “how big?”; the p-value answers “is it real?” You need both. With enough data, a trivial effect is significant.

Power analysis happens before data collection. An underpowered null is not a finding. Spotify's ~12% win rate against a ~64% learning rate is what adequate power buys.

Multiple comparisons manufacture findings. BH is the balanced correction — but on our own subgroups it kept the same single result Bonferroni did, and the biggest dollar effect died.

2 min. Read only the bolded clause of each. If you are behind schedule, this is the slide to compress, not the exit ticket.
