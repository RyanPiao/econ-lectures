1  •  The Logic of Testing

What you assume, what you measure, and the conditional everyone flips

Two Hypotheses — and Which One You Assume

Not "does job training help?" but "is the earnings gap larger than sampling variability alone would produce?"

$$H_0: \mu_{\text{treatment}} - \mu_{\text{control}} = 0 \qquad H_1: \mu_{\text{treatment}} - \mu_{\text{control}} \neq 0$$

Interpretation: the null says the gap you can see in your sample is what random sampling would have produced anyway, with nothing real underneath it.

The courtroom. Null = “innocent.” Data = the evidence. Small p-value = “guilty beyond reasonable doubt.” And an acquittal does not prove innocence — it says the evidence was insufficient.

You never accept the null. You fail to reject it.

3 min.

The courtroom analogy is the one students keep.

  • Push the acquittal point hard — "not guilty" and "innocent" are not the same thing, and neither are "fail to reject" and "no effect".
  • That distinction is worth two poll questions later.

The p-Value

$$p = P\big(|T| \geq |t_{\text{obs}}| \;\big|\; H_0 \text{ is true}\big)$$

IF THE NULL IS TRUE

INTERPRETATION

A small \(p\) says: if the null were true and you re-ran this study many times, data this extreme would rarely turn up. That is all it measures.

\(\alpha\) is fixed before you look at the data. 0.05 by convention. It is a decision rule, not a measure of truth — it controls how often you falsely reject in the long run.

Poll 1 — A Study Reports p = 0.03

Which statement is correct?

A) There is a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true
B) There is a 97% chance the alternative is true
C) If the null were true, data this extreme would occur about 3% of the time

Students click to vote on their phones.

  • Do not signal the answer.

Answer: C — and A is the Prosecutor's Fallacy

A and B are the same error twice. The p-value is \(P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\). A and B claim \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\). Flipping that conditional needs Bayes' theorem and a prior for \(H_0\) — which frequentist inference never supplies.

Say it out loud: “5% of people wear a red shirt. This suspect wears a red shirt. Is there a 5% chance he is innocent?” Nobody says yes — and option A is that sentence with different nouns.

The American Statistical Association published a formal statement in 2016 for the sole purpose of stamping on this error (Wasserstein & Lazar).

2  •  Two Errors, and Their Prices

Which mistake is worse is not a statistical question

Every Decision Rule Fails in Two Directions

Type I (\(\alpha\)) — you announce an effect that is not there. You set this rate yourself.

Type II (\(\beta\)) — you miss an effect that is there. \(1-\beta\) is power, and Section 6 is about how to get more of it.

You choose \(\alpha\) with a keystroke. \(\beta\) is chosen for you by your sample size, the true effect, and the noise.

2 min.

The asymmetry in who chooses what is the point of this slide: alpha is a decision, beta is a consequence.

  • Students who understand that sentence do not later ask "can I just lower alpha to get more power?"

Which Error Is Worse? An Economic Question

Clinical trials

A false positive approves a useless or dangerous drug. People are harmed. So trials often run \(\alpha = 0.01\) or stricter.

A button-colour A/B test

A false positive launches a harmless feature. The cost is almost nothing. \(\alpha = 0.05\) or even \(0.10\) is defensible.

Think like an economist. Before any test: what does a false positive cost me relative to a false negative? In fraud detection, missing real fraud is catastrophic — so you accept more false alarms. \(\alpha\) should encode that asymmetry, not default blindly to 0.05.

2 min.

Resist the urge to give a rule. The whole point is that there is no rule — there is a cost ratio, and it is domain knowledge, not statistics.

  • This sets up the discussion on the next slide.

Welch's t-Statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

WHERE

\(\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2\) sample means
\(s_1^2, s_2^2\) sample variances
\(n_1, n_2\) sample sizes

INTERPRETATION

Top = the difference you observed. Bottom = how much that difference would bounce around across repeated samples. So \(t\) counts how many standard errors from zero your result sits.

Always use Welch's (equal_var=False): with equal variances it agrees with Student's, with unequal ones Student's misleads.

3 min.

Read the denominator aloud as "the standard error of the difference" before you say anything about degrees of freedom.

NEW DIAGRAM — Lab 8's own spreads on one axis: the treated group (n = 185) is the noisier one, SD $7,867 against $5,484. Then the two standard errors: pooled $633 gives t = 2.84, Welch $671 gives t = 2.67. Same direction here, but pooling understated the uncertainty.

  • Welch adjusts df downward via Satterthwaite when variances differ, which makes the p-value more conservative — one sentence, do not derive it.

The Three Assumptions

1. Independence

Violated by classmates, repeated measures, spatial clustering. Effective \(n\) shrinks, the standard error is too small, and the p-value looks better than it is.

2. Normality

Of the sampling distribution of the mean, not the data. \(n>30\) per group usually delivers it. For small \(n\) it bites — recall the mean's breakdown point of \(1/n\) from Ch 4.

3. Equal variance

Student's assumes \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\). Wages, prices and returns almost never do. Ch 4 diagnostic: if one group's IQR is more than twice the other's, do not assume it.

The p-value answers “is it real?” — nothing answers “does it matter?” unless you ask

A p-Value That Answered the Wrong Half

2023: the FDA grants traditional approval to Leqembi (lecanemab) for Alzheimer's. The Phase 3 Clarity AD trial enrolled 1,795 patients and reported an effect on the CDR-SB cognitive scale with \(p < 0.001\).

A difference most clinicians struggle to detect in a patient. The drug also carries real risks — brain swelling and bleeding (ARIA) in a meaningful fraction of patients.

Cohen's d

$$d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_{\text{pooled}}}, \qquad s_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}}$$

d measures the gap in standard deviations. Because it divides by spread rather than by \(\sqrt{n}\), it does not grow just because you collected more data.

0.2

small

0.5

medium

0.8

large

Rough guides, not thresholds — a “small” effect on lifetime earnings can still be a lot of money.

2 min.

The key sentence is "it does not grow just because you collected more data" — contrast directly with the t-statistic, whose denominator has the square root of n in it.

So How Big Was $1,794?

Pooled std:  $6,579.54
Cohen's d:   0.2727

(pingouin agrees: 0.2727)

The $1,794 gap is about 0.27 standard deviations of earnings — small by the conventional benchmarks: just past the 0.2 line, well short of 0.5.

At $500 per participant, a $1,794 gain is an excellent return.

At $10,000 per participant, it is not.

Statistical significance ≠ practical significance. With enough data any non-zero effect becomes significant — ten million users can detect a 1% relative change in click-through at \(p<0.01\). No effect is ever exactly zero, so the useful question is whether it is big enough to act on.

6  •  Power: When Can You Trust a Null Result?

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — unless you were powered to find it

Statistical Power

$$\text{Power} = 1 - \beta = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ is true})$$

Interpretation: the probability your study actually catches a real effect. Convention: aim for \(\geq 0.80\).

Power has four levers. Fix three, solve for the fourth:

Effect size

bigger is easier

Sample size

more data, less noise

\(\alpha\)

stricter costs power

Variability

noisier needs more

2 min.

Point at the alpha box and connect back to Section 2: this is the price of the strict alpha a clinical trial chooses.

  • A lower Type I error rate comes at the cost of a higher Type II error rate.

Power Is the Share of p-Values Under 0.05

No effect → p-values come out flat. Every value equally likely, and exactly \(\alpha\) of them fall under 0.05 by construction.

Real effect → the distribution collapses toward zero. The share under 0.05 is the power — 71.2% here, at d = 0.5 with 50 per group.

2 min. 2,000 simulated t-tests in each panel; you rebuild this yourself in the Lab 8 Extension.

The left panel is the single best argument for why multiple comparisons are dangerous, and you will reuse it in twelve minutes: flat means one in twenty falls under 0.05 no matter what.

How Much Data Do You Need?

$$n \approx \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \,(s_1^2 + s_2^2)}{\delta^2}$$

WHERE

\(z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96\) at \(\alpha=0.05\)  •  \(z_\beta = 0.84\) for 80% power  •  \(\delta\) = the minimum detectable effect you care about

\(\delta\) is squared, in the denominator.
Halve the effect you want to catch and you quadruple the data you need.

That one fact explains most of the underpowered literature in the social sciences.

2 min.

Do not derive anything.

Ask them to look at where delta sits and say what happens when it halves — someone will get "four times" and that is the point of the slide.

  • This is also why power analysis must happen BEFORE collection: discovering you were underpowered afterwards is too late.

The Economics of Experimentation

To reach 80% power, per group:

d = 0.8  →  26

d = 0.5  →  64

d = 0.273  →  212

d = 0.2  →  394

Four times the effect size, one-fifteenth the data.

7  •  Multiplicity and the Replication Crisis

Example 4: the finding that was never there

Test Twenty Things and Something Will Light Up

$$P(\text{at least one false positive}) = 1 - (1-\alpha)^m = 1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64$$

Remember the flat histogram from Section 6: under the null, one p-value in twenty lands below 0.05 by construction.

Netflix (Chou et al., KDD 2025 Applied Data Science best paper). Winners picked from many weak experiments overstate what they deliver, because noise helped pick them. The fix is to select the winner on one part of the data and measure it on another. Chapter 6's train-test logic, applied to business decisions.

Two Corrections

Bonferroni

Test each hypothesis at \(\alpha/m\). With 20 tests: \(p < 0.0025\).

Controls the family-wise error rate — the chance of even one false positive. Very conservative.

Benjamini-Hochberg

Rank the p-values; find the largest \(k\) with \(p_{(k)} \leq \frac{k}{m}\alpha\); reject the first \(k\).

Controls the false discovery rate — the expected share of your discoveries that are wrong.

In English. Bonferroni says “I will tolerate no false findings at all, and I accept that I will miss real ones.” BH says “I accept that maybe 5% of what I report is wrong, in exchange for finding much more of what is real.”

2 min.

Show BH as a threshold, not as a ranking algorithm — the step-up procedure is a lab exercise, not a lecture derivation.

The two English sentences are what students should leave with.

Not the usual textbook result. BH is generally more permissive than Bonferroni — but here it keeps the same single finding.

HS Diploma+ had the largest raw difference of all five ($3,192) and, of the two that looked significant, the smaller samples (54 and 43). Its p = 0.0302 must clear \(\tfrac{2}{5}\times 0.05 = 0.02\) under BH. It does not.

This is the most important slide in Section 7 and it deliberately contradicts the usual textbook pattern.

  • Be precise about what that does and does not break: Bonferroni is still valid, because it controls FWER under arbitrary dependence — it assumes nothing about independence.
  • BH is the one leaning on a condition: its FDR guarantee holds under independence or positive regression dependence (PRDS), and under arbitrary dependence you would reach for Benjamini-Yekutieli instead.
  • The deeper point is that neither correction can tell you whether five was the right number of tests to count — that is a pre-data decision, and it is the one that actually protects you.

The Camel: p-Hacking's Fingerprint

Brodeur, Lé, Sangnier & Zylberberg (2016) examined 50,000 tests in the AER, JPE and QJE (2005–11).

They found a two-humped “camel” in the distribution of z-statistics: a valley between about \(z=1.2\) and \(1.65\), and a bump just past \(z=1.96\). Brodeur, Cook & Heyes (2020) found the same pattern in 21,000+ tests across 25 journals, worst for IV and DID.

Test statistics that should have landed just short of the line somehow migrated across it.

Chapter 8 in a Nutshell

Hypothesis testing is a rule for making decisions. It does not tell you what is true. A small p-value says the data would be surprising under the null — it does not prove the alternative, and it says nothing about whether the effect matters.

The t-test counts how many standard errors the difference sits from zero. Use Welch's by default — and know it repairs unequal variance and nothing else.

Cohen's d answers “how big?”; the p-value answers “is it real?” You need both. With enough data, a trivial effect is significant.

Power analysis happens before data collection. An underpowered null is not a finding. Spotify's ~12% win rate against a ~64% learning rate comes from adequate power.

Multiple comparisons manufacture findings. BH is the balanced correction — but on our own subgroups it kept the same single result Bonferroni did, and the biggest dollar effect did not survive.
