1  •  The Box-Jenkins Machine

Momentum, correction, and the loop you are not allowed to skip

AR(\(p\)) — Momentum

$$Y_t = c + \phi_1 Y_{t-1} + \phi_2 Y_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p Y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t$$

In words: each \(\phi_i\) says how much of the value \(i\) months ago carries into today. A large \(\phi_1\) means a slow, persistent series — where it was going, it keeps going.

\(\varepsilon_t\) is white noise — mean zero, constant variance, no memory.

Stationarity has a formal root condition. You will not check it by hand — ADF/KPSS from Ch 20 already did.

2 min. Driving analogy: AR is momentum. Where you were going a moment ago determines where you are now. Resist deriving the characteristic polynomial — mention it exists and point at Ch 20.

MA(\(q\)) — Correction

$$Y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}$$

In words: the \(\theta_j\) act on past surprises, not past values. An MA(1) with \(\theta_1 = 0.7\) says today carries 70% of yesterday's shock — and by the day after, that shock is gone entirely.

MA processes have short memory by construction. The effect of any single shock disappears completely after \(q\) periods. That is the whole difference from AR, where it decays but never quite vanishes.

New notation this chapter: \(\theta\) for MA coefficients, \(B\) for the backshift operator.

2 min. Steering correction: you adjust based on how far off course your last position was. The "gone after q periods" property is the thing to make them repeat back.

One Shock, Two Mechanisms

Simulated, \(n = 90\), seed 7. This is the shape of the two mechanisms — not a claim about CPI.

2 min. Point at the orange line in each panel, not the blue. Ask: "how many periods until the shock is gone?" AR: roughly twenty. MA: two. That is the entire intuition and it is worth the slide.

ARIMA(\(p, d, q\)) — Put Them Together

$$\phi(B)\,(1 - B)^d\, Y_t = \theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t$$

In words: difference the series \(d\) times until it is stationary, then model what is left as momentum plus correction.

\(B\) is the backshift operator: \(BY_t = Y_{t-1}\). It is Chapter 20's lag operator \(L\) wearing a different letter.

\(\phi(B) = 1 - \phi_1 B - \cdots - \phi_p B^p\) and \(\theta(B) = 1 + \theta_1 B + \cdots + \theta_q B^q\) are just compact bookkeeping.

The \(d\) is not a tuning parameter. It is the answer ADF and KPSS already gave you.

3 min. Slow down on the last line. Students routinely treat d as something to grid-search; it is a decision they already made in Ch 20. Differencing is measuring speed instead of position — position keeps changing, speed can be constant.

Anticipated Q: "why not just take the lowest AIC?" → AIC assumes the model class is correctly specified; breaks, outliers and odd seasonality violate that. ACF/PACF catches what AIC misses.

3  •  Step 3: Diagnose

The step everyone skips, and the trap waiting for anyone who does not

The Ljung-Box Test

$$Q = T(T+2) \sum_{k=1}^{h} \frac{\hat{\rho}_k^2}{T-k}$$

In words: add up the squared autocorrelations of the residuals out to lag \(h\), weighted for sample size. If the model captured everything, the residuals are white noise and \(Q\) is small.

\(p > 0.05\) — fail to reject. No detectable structure left.

\(p < 0.05\) — reject. Structure remains → the model is misspecified → back to step 1.

If the test fails on the squared residuals instead, that is volatility clustering — the variance itself is moving. Beyond us today.

3 min. Note the direction of the test is the opposite of what students expect from a t-test: here a big p-value is the good news. Say that explicitly, it prevents a very common misreading on the exam.

Now Run It. Twice.

pmdarima's resid() returns the raw first observation as element 0, not a residual. On log CPI that element is 4.84595, against genuine residuals near 0.0016.

Ljung-Box \(p\) lag 6 lag 12 lag 18 lag 24
resid() 1.0000 1.0000 1.0000 1.0000
resid()[1:] 0.5803 0.0034 0.0013 0.0022

One contaminating element lifts the residual standard deviation from 0.0024 to 0.2376 — about a hundredfold — which crushes every autocorrelation coefficient toward zero.

3 min. This is the highest-value slide in the deck for lab success. They WILL hit this. The lab now slices and carries a comment explaining why, but they need to understand it, not just copy it.

Same Model, Same Data, Opposite Verdict

1 min. Let the picture do the work. The bottom panel is on a log scale so the tiny p-values are visible — say that, or someone will read the bar heights as effect sizes.

So What Is the Verdict?

ARIMA(0, 1, 2) is not adequate. It is clean at short lags and rejects hard from lag 12 onward.

Lag 12. Lag 18. Lag 24. Multiples of twelve — exactly the −0.18 PACF spike we saw and walked past twenty minutes ago. There is an annual pattern left in the residuals of a series that has already been seasonally adjusted.

A seasonal term is the obvious next iteration. That is what “cycle” means — the diagnostic sent us back to step 1, and we go.

2 min. Resist the urge to actually fit the SARIMA here — there is not time and the point is the loop, not the model. Note that a good diagnostic is one that tells you what to try next, and this one did.

4  •  SARIMA, Briefly

The same machine, running again at lag \(m\)

SARIMA(\(p,d,q\))(\(P,D,Q\))[\(m\)]

$$\phi(B)\,\Phi(B^m)\,(1-B)^d\,(1-B^m)^D\, Y_t = \theta(B)\,\Theta(B^m)\,\varepsilon_t$$

In words: the uppercase letters are the same AR / I / MA machinery running again at lag \(m\) instead of lag 1. For monthly data \(m = 12\), and \((1-B^{12})Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-12}\) subtracts last year's same month.

Think of it as month fixed effects — except estimated from the data's own autocorrelation instead of imposed as dummies.

Ch 20's STL pulled seasonality out before modelling. SARIMA estimates it jointly with trend and noise.

Our diagnostic just told us we need this: rejection concentrated at lags 12, 18, 24.

4 min. Do not derive this. One pass over the equation naming which letters are which, then straight to the two boxes. The payoff line at the bottom is what earns the slide — SARIMA arrives here because the data asked for it, not because it is next in the textbook.

5  •  Prophet

A different bet entirely: forecasting as regression

Meta, 2017: A Headcount Problem

Millions of business series, no analyst time for per-series ACF inspection. Taylor & Letham reframed forecasting as a regression problem.

$$y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + \varepsilon_t$$

\(g(t)\)
trend, with changepoints

\(s(t)\)
Fourier seasonality

\(h(t)\)
holiday effects

\(\varepsilon_t\)
noise

In words: the same three pieces Chapter 20 decomposed — but inside the forecasting model rather than as preprocessing.

3 min. Frame the motivation honestly: Prophet was not built to beat ARIMA on accuracy, it was built so that one analyst could ship a million forecasts. That framing makes its limitations predictable rather than surprising.

Changepoints Are LASSO in Disguise

Prophet lays down candidate trend breaks on a uniform grid, then applies a sparse Laplace (L1) prior that shrinks most break magnitudes to exactly zero. Only the real ones survive.

That is Chapter 16. LASSO shrinks regression coefficients to zero; Prophet shrinks trend slopes to zero. Same penalty, different object.

The tuning knob is changepoint_prior_scale. Lower means a stiffer prior and a smoother trend; higher lets the trend bend more freely. Our lab uses the default, 0.05.

2 min. Students who did Ch 16 will get this instantly — call on one of them to say what L1 does that L2 does not (drives coefficients to exactly zero rather than merely small). That is why the surviving changepoints are a short list rather than 25 tiny ones.

The Changepoints Stop in 2017

They span 1991-02 to 2017-08 and stop. Prophet places candidates only in the first 80% of the training window — so on a series training to 2024, nothing after 2017 can ever get one.

2 min. This is a design decision, not a bug: Prophet avoids the last 20% deliberately so that a handful of end-of-sample points cannot swing the extrapolated trend. The cost of that safety is exactly what the next slide names.

You Have Just Rebuilt Target

The single largest trend break in this sample is the 2021–22 inflation surge. Prophet's changepoint mechanism structurally cannot place a changepoint there.

It absorbs the surge into the slope of its last segment instead — and then extends that slope forward.

A model trained through a regime change, quietly extrapolating the regime it happened to end in. That is the August 2022 earnings call, reproduced on your laptop with forty lines of Python.

3 min. This is the emotional peak of the lecture — the loop you opened in the hook. Say the last sentence and then stop talking for three seconds. Then note the honest qualifier: Prophet is not uniquely guilty here, ARIMA fitted on the same window has the same blind spot. What differs is that Prophet advertises changepoint detection, so people trust it further than they should.

Prophet's Own Limitations

1

Trend extrapolation. It extends the current trend, linearly or logistically. A break after training ends is invisible to it by construction.

2

Speed. Nixtla's StatsForecast reports up to 20× faster automated ARIMA fitting. At millions of series, that is the whole ballgame.

3

Installation friction. The PyStan → CmdStanPy migration caused persistent Colab dependency conflicts. Many teams moved to NeuralProphet or StatsForecast.

Sean Taylor, a co-creator, acknowledged in a 2023 retrospective that Prophet was validated on only a few Facebook use cases before release. It is still the best entry point for forecasting many series quickly with interpretable components — the beginning of the journey, not the end.

2 min. The last italic line matters: this is not a hit piece. Prophet earned its place. The lesson is that every tool's limitations are legible from what it was built to do.

Discussion — Pairs, 3 Minutes

Before you pick a model, answer this: are you trying to understand the data-generating process, or to produce the best forecast?

Give me one economics task where you would take ARIMA's interpretable \(\phi\) and \(\theta\) over a lower forecast error — and one where you would take the lower error and not care what the model thinks it is doing.

This is a genuine fork in applied work, not a ranking. Both branches are respectable.

6  •  Compared to What?

The question we have been postponing since the forecast slide

MAPE, and Why Not

$$\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{t=1}^{h}\left|\frac{y_t - \hat{y}_t}{y_t}\right| \times 100$$

In words: the average percentage miss. “We were off by 3% on average.” Intuitive, universally used in business, and broken three ways.

1. Undefined at zero. Divide by \(y_t = 0\) and it explodes. Common in intermittent demand.

2. Asymmetric. It punishes over-forecasting far harder than under-forecasting.

3. Scale-dependent. 5% on GDP is not comparable to 5% on unemployment claims.

Flaw 2 is the dangerous one, and it is worth a picture.

3 min. Most of them will meet MAPE in an internship this summer, presented without caveat. Flaws 1 and 3 are easy; flaw 2 is the one that silently changes business outcomes.

The Asymmetry, Drawn

Under-forecasting is capped at 100% — the worst you can do is predict 0. Over-forecasting is unbounded: predict 300 against 100 and you score 200%; predict 1,000 and you score 900%.

2 min. Then the consequence: a model tuned to minimise MAPE learns to forecast low, systematically. In an inventory or staffing setting that is chronic under-stocking, produced by the metric rather than by the data.

Poll — The Metric Bites Back

A retailer tunes its demand model to minimise MAPE. Six months later, stores are systematically short of stock. Most likely mechanical cause?

A) The model is underfitting — it needs more parameters
B) MAPE penalises over-forecasting harder, so the model learned to forecast low
C) MAPE is undefined at zero demand, so those days were dropped
D) The training data ended before the demand shift
1 min. Click to vote. C and D are both real problems in forecasting — that is what makes them good distractors. Neither produces a systematic one-directional bias, which is the word in the stem doing the work.

Answer: B — The Metric Taught It To

The word doing the work in the question is systematically. C and D are real problems, but they produce noise or a one-off miss — not a persistent lean in one direction. Only the asymmetry does that.

2 min. Generalise once: your loss function is a statement about which errors you are willing to make. Choose it before you choose the model.

MASE — The Economist's Scorecard

$$\text{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{h}\sum_{t=1}^{h}\left|e_t\right|}{\frac{1}{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}\left|Y_t - Y_{t-m}\right|}$$

In words: your average error, divided by the average error of the dumbest defensible forecast — “predict the same value as \(m\) periods ago.” The denominator is the whole idea.

< 1
beats naive

= 1
ties naive

> 1
loses to naive

Scale-free, defined at zero, symmetric. MASE = 0.6 means your errors are 40% smaller than naive's. Standard metric of the M4 and M5 competitions — M5 alone was 5,558 teams forecasting 42,840 Walmart series.

3 min. Write the formula on the board. Make them say the interpretation ladder out loud. The three cards are the version they need on the exam; the caveat that undoes the third card arrives in two slides, and that ordering is deliberate.

A 24-step-ahead forecast is a far harder problem than the one-step-ahead errors it is being divided by. For MASE to mean “beats naive,” numerator and denominator must sit on the same horizon.

2 min. This is the single most likely sentence for them to get wrong in the lab write-up, which is why it gets a poll rather than a bullet. The lab's Part 6 markdown says the same thing — tell them to read it.

The Fair Comparison

Chapter 15's cross-validation, with the shuffling removed. Chapter 20 told you why random \(k\)-fold leaks the future into the training set.

Expanding-window backtest: refit at every origin → forecast one step → expand the window → repeat. Now numerator and denominator sit on the same horizon, and MASE < 1 genuinely means better than predicting last month's number.

The cost is 24 auto_arima fits plus 24 Prophet fits — which is why the lab stops short of it and flags it as the first extension to reach for if you take the analysis further.

The point is not which model wins on this dataset. It is that you now have a principled framework for asking.

2 min. Tie the three chapters together explicitly: Ch 15 gave the idea, Ch 20 gave the temporal constraint, Ch 21 gives the metric. If they remember one operational thing from today, it is "never evaluate a forecast without a benchmark on the same horizon."

7  •  What to Take Away

Five ways to get this wrong, five things to remember

Five Pitfalls

1. ARIMA on data with an undetected structural break — the parameters become a blend of two regimes and fit neither.

2. Skipping residual diagnostics — or, now, reading them without checking what is in the residual vector.

3. Overfitting with high orders. ARIMA(5,2,3) always fits training better and forecasts worse.

4. Extrapolating a Prophet trend far ahead. It projects the current slope indefinitely; saturation, diminishing returns and policy are invisible to it.

5. Comparing across scales without MASE. RMSE 100 on GDP and 0.5 on unemployment tells you nothing about relative performance.

2 min. Read 2 and 4 slowly — both were demonstrated live today rather than asserted. That is what makes them stick.

Chapter 21 in a Nutshell

ARIMA is momentum (\(\phi\)) plus correction (\(\theta\)), after differencing \(d\) times — and \(d\) came from Chapter 20, not from a grid search.

Box-Jenkins is a loop, not a function call. auto_arima is step 2. Our own model failed step 3 and told us what to fit next.

Prophet is regression with L1-shrunk changepoints — LASSO on trend slopes — and its changepoints stop at 80% of training.

MASE benchmarks against the naive forecast — and only means “beats naive” when the horizons match.

No single model wins everywhere. Backtest on your own data, and keep the naive benchmark in the room.

2 min. If you are running short, this slide and the exit ticket are the two you must not cut. Everything else has a home in the lab.
