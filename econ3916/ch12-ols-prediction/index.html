The Bridge

You have seen regression as a tool for estimating a parameter you care about — the coefficient IS the answer. Today we use the identical machinery for a different job: producing a number for an observation we have not seen.

Nothing about the algebra changes. What changes is the scorecard.

Chapter 10 was about the first job: if the coefficient is the answer, which variables you condition on decides whether the answer is right, and a DAG tells you which. This chapter is the second job, where that entire apparatus becomes beside the point.

When you only want a good \(\hat{y}\), you do not care whether a predictor is a confounder, a mediator, or a collider. You care whether it helps you predict.

1 min. Transition line: "So the first question in applied work is never which method. It is which job." Then advance to Section 1.

Two Jobs, One Machine

Section 12.1

Every Regression Answers One of Two Questions

"What is the effect of \(X\) on \(Y\)?"

A number attached to a variable. Does an extra year of schooling raise earnings, and by how much?

The coefficient is the deliverable. Randomisation, backdoor paths, adjustment sets — all of Chapters 9 and 10 exists to make that one number trustworthy.

"What will \(Y\) be?"

A number attached to an observation. What will this house sell for? Will this loan default? What is GDP this quarter, given the official figure arrives in thirty days?

No coefficient matters. Only the predicted value does.

Shmueli (2010) named it — to explain or to predict. Mullainathan & Spiess (2017) brought it to economists as a \(\hat{\beta}\) problem versus a \(\hat{y}\) problem. Kleinberg et al. (2015) made the policy case: many consequential decisions are prediction problems wearing causal clothing.

Whether to replace a roof before it fails is a prediction problem. Whether a subsidy caused a firm to hire is not.

Common Misconception

"A model that predicts well must have found real causal relationships."

Prediction requires only a stable statistical association between features and outcome. It does not require, and does not establish, that intervening on a feature would change the outcome.

A model can forecast hospital costs superbly using variables that are consequences of the illness rather than causes of the cost — and be worthless for deciding what treatment to fund.

Chapter 10's whole apparatus exists because these are different questions. Today's job is to make you fluent in the second one without forgetting the first.

The Line of Best Fit

Section 12.2

"Best" Needs a Definition — and the Definition Is a Choice

You have a scatterplot and you want a line through it. Infinitely many lines are available. OLS makes a specific choice: minimise the sum of the squared vertical distances.

Vertical

Error in the outcome only, not perpendicular distance — we predict \(y\) from \(x\), and errors in \(x\) are a different problem.

Squared

So positives and negatives cannot cancel — and so large errors are penalised disproportionately.

Sum

Total error across the sample, not any single point.

Ch 4 callback. Moving the line to reduce a distance of 10 saves 100 units of penalty; reducing a distance of 1 saves 1. That is exactly why the arithmetic mean has a breakdown point of \(1/n\). OLS is the mean's approach to fitting a line, and it is fragile in the same way and for the same reason.

3 min. Draw two candidate lines on the board through three points and ask which is better before defining anything. The point is that "best" was never obvious; we chose.

The Fit, and What the Fit Already Tells You

Chapter 3's rule: a regression coefficient is a summary statistic, so plot it before you trust it.

1 min. Point at the band of dots along the top of panel A. Ask what it is before telling them. Somebody will say "the cap" — that is the hook into Section 3's residual work.

Now the Algebra That Produced It

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i$$

Reading: outcome equals a line, plus everything the line does not capture. \(\beta_0\) intercept, \(\beta_1\) true slope, \(\varepsilon_i\) the error, \(n\) the number of observations.

$$(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1) = \arg\min_{b_0, b_1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i\right)^2$$

Reading: out of every line you could draw, take the one whose squared vertical misses add to the least. The hat means "estimated from data"; \(\beta\) without a hat is the unknown truth. \(b_0, b_1\) are candidates being searched over.

$$\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i, \qquad e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$$

Say this out loud — it is a standard interview question. The error \(\varepsilon_i\) is the unobservable population quantity. The residual \(e_i\) is what you can actually compute once you have estimates. Different objects, and conflating them is a reliable way to confuse yourself later.

4 min. Every equation gets its sentence before you move on — never leave math bare on this slide. The error/residual distinction is the one students blur every single year; make them say it back.

The Closed Form

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}$$

Reading: the slope is how much \(x\) and \(y\) move together, divided by how much \(x\) moves on its own — covariance over variance. The intercept is then whatever it takes to drag the line through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).

Key Concept — the OLS line always passes through the sample means.

Among all straight lines, OLS picks the one whose squared vertical gaps sum to the least. Squaring makes large errors dominate, which is why OLS inherits the outlier sensitivity you diagnosed in the sample mean in Chapter 4.

Measuring Fit — and Why It Is Not Enough

Section 12.3

Two Answers, Two Different Questions

\(R^2\) — a relative question

How much better is this than the dumbest possible model — the one that ignores \(x\) and predicts \(\bar{y}\) for everybody? Unitless; between 0 and 1 for any model with an intercept.

"Better than nothing?"

RMSE — an absolute question

On average, by how much is the model wrong? Measured in the outcome's own units — dollars, if you are predicting dollars — with no upper bound.

"Wrong by how much?"

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2, \qquad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\text{MSE}} \qquad\qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$

Reading: MSE is the average squared miss; square-root it and you are back in the outcome's units. \(R^2\) is your squared error divided by the dumb baseline's, subtracted from one. \(R^2 = 0.5\) means half the baseline's squared error; \(R^2 = 0\) means no better than the mean. On test data \(R^2\) can go negative — which sounds impossible until you meet an overfitted model.

3 min. When someone reports one of these and you need the other, ask. Then the pivot line: "MSE is the sum of squared residuals divided by n — so OLS is BY CONSTRUCTION the linear model that minimises in-sample MSE. Hold that; it explains what is about to go wrong."

The Fact That Motivates Everything Else

Adding any predictor to a regression cannot decrease \(R^2\).

Not "usually does not." Cannot.

The proof is one line. OLS minimises squared error over a set of candidate models. Adding a variable strictly enlarges that set — the old model is still available, with a coefficient of zero on the new variable. The minimum over a larger set cannot be larger. So \(R^2\) weakly increases.

This holds whether the new predictor is median income, the price of tea, or a column of random numbers. Add enough random columns and \(R^2\) climbs toward 1.

A criterion that improves when you add noise is not a criterion. It is a description of how flexibly the model can trace the data you handed it.

And the Spread Is Not Constant

Variability grows by about 27% from the cheapest neighbourhoods to the most expensive. Expensive areas are harder to predict, so a single RMSE for the whole dataset overstates accuracy where the stakes are largest. The phenomenon has a name — heteroscedasticity — and Chapter 14 handles it properly. Today's narrower and more important lesson: report error by segment, not just in aggregate.

The Test Set

Section 12.4 — the centrepiece

If In-Sample Error Is the Wrong Scorecard

The fix is direct: hold some data back, fit on the rest, and measure error on the part the model never saw.

You have met this. Chapter 6 introduced the train–test split as a sampling design decision and warned about leakage as a violation of sample independence. Now it becomes the central instrument of the rest of the book.

The data you fit on is the training set. The data you hold back is the test set. Nothing from the test set may influence fitting in any way — not scaling, not imputation, not feature selection, not your choice of model.

A model that has memorised the particular noise in those particular observations does well on them and badly on new draws — because the noise does not repeat.

A model that has captured genuine structure does about as well on both — because the structure does repeat.

Comparing the two errors therefore tells you which kind of model you have.

3 min. The two boxes are the whole logic of held-out evaluation; make sure both sentences are said aloud. Then the numbers.

Making a Model Overfit

Add polynomial terms — \(x,\ x^2,\ x^3,\ \ldots\) — increasing flexibility one degree at a time, and watch both errors.

Xs, ys = X_train.iloc[:60], y_train.iloc[:60]   # a deliberately small sample

for degree in [1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9]:
    pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(),
                         PolynomialFeatures(degree, include_bias=False),
                         LinearRegression()).fit(Xs, ys)

The 60-row sample is not a cheat. Overfitting is a relationship between model flexibility and data quantity — a small sample makes the effect visible quickly. And the StandardScaler is load-bearing: without it the high-degree terms are so ill-conditioned that the solver stops finding the true minimum and the demonstration misleads.

Every In-Sample Diagnostic Selects the Worst Model Here

The high-degree model bends to pass close to each of those 60 training points — including the part of each point that is noise. Noise does not repeat in the test set, so the bends do not help there. They hurt, because a wiggly curve extrapolates wildly the moment it meets an \(x\) outside its training range.

Key Concept — Out-of-Sample Error Is the Scorecard

In-sample error measures how well a model traces data it has already seen, and it can always be driven down by adding flexibility. Out-of-sample error measures how well it generalises, and it cannot be gamed the same way.

When the two diverge, the model is memorising rather than learning.

Report test error

Select models on test error

Be suspicious of anything reported only in-sample

You have just watched a real tension play out: more flexibility buys better in-sample fit and, past some point, worse out-of-sample performance. Hold on to the shape of those two columns — it is the single most important picture in applied machine learning.

Economist at Work — GDPNow, Atlanta Fed

The BEA publishes its first estimate of quarterly GDP about a month after the quarter ends. For a central bank setting rates, or a desk positioning for a release, a thirty-day blind spot is expensive. GDPNow closes it — and the approach is deliberately unglamorous.

The method

Decompose GDP into the same 13 subcomponents the BEA itself uses. Apply linear bridge equations — regressions linking data released at different frequencies — to map incoming monthly indicators onto each component. Aggregate back up. Recalculate as each release lands, with no discretionary human adjustment.

The transparency

They publish the working paper specifying every equation, a document listing every modification since 2014, and a spreadsheet with the raw data, the intermediate forecasts, and the release calendar. You can reproduce the number yourself.

One of the most closely watched macro forecasts in the world is a structured system of linear regressions, evaluated relentlessly out-of-sample and published in full. Linear models did not survive into 2026 because nothing better exists. They survived because on noisy, structured, moderately sized economic data a rigid functional form generalises well, runs in microseconds, and can be explained to a policymaker — and the fanciest available model is not automatically the best one.

How Wrong, and How Sure?

Section 12.5

A Prediction Without an Uncertainty Statement Is Half a Deliverable

But there are two distinct uncertainties, and confusing them is one of the most common errors in applied forecasting.

"What is the average house value for block groups with median income of $50,000?"

A question about a population average. Your uncertainty comes only from not knowing the line exactly. With enough data it becomes very small.

→ a confidence interval

"What will this particular block group's median house value be?"

Two things go wrong. You still do not know the line exactly — and even if you did, this block group would scatter around it, because \(\varepsilon_i\) is not zero for individual observations.

→ a prediction interval

The prediction interval is always wider, and it is almost always the one a decision-maker actually needs. Nobody prices a specific house off the conditional mean. Same distinction Chapter 7 drew bootstrapping a sample mean: uncertainty about a parameter is not variability in the underlying data.

3 min. statsmodels gives both, which is why it is worth keeping alongside scikit-learn. Say that; it answers the question students always ask about why we teach two libraries.

2 min. Both intervals are correct. They answer different questions. That is the whole slide.

The Same Two Intervals, Drawn to Scale

The prediction interval is wide for a reason no amount of additional data will fix. It is wide because median income alone does not determine house value. More data shrinks the CI toward zero and leaves the PI essentially where it is. To narrow that, you need better features — which is Chapter 13.

Poll 4 — Which Number Does the Bank Get?

A bank asks you for a number to decide how much to lend against one specific property in a block group with $50,000 median income. Which do you hand them?

A) The point prediction, $254,129 — they asked for a number
B) The 95% CI, $252,649 to $255,609 — the tighter, more useful interval
C) The 95% PI, $90,218 to $418,040
D) Neither interval — collect more data first, then the CI
1 min. B is the expensive wrong answer and D is the second trap. Let them vote before you say either.

Answer: C — the Prediction Interval

The bank is lending against one property, not against the average of all block groups with that income. That is the prediction-interval question by definition.

B is the tempting and expensive error — a real interval, correctly computed, answering a question nobody asked. D is the second trap: more data shrinks the CI and barely touches the PI, so waiting buys you nothing here.

And if C's width makes the loan undecidable — that is a finding, not a failure. It tells the bank this model is not precise enough to price a single property, which is exactly what they needed to know.

What the Intercept Is, and Is Not

The California model returned an intercept of 0.4509 — about $45,090. Read literally, that is its prediction for a block group whose median income is exactly zero.

No such block group exists. The lowest median income anywhere in the data is 0.4999 — about $5,000 — and the mean is 3.87, roughly $38,700. So $45,090 is not a prediction about any place in California. It is the value the fitted line happens to take when extended back to a point well outside the range of the data.

Extrapolating a line beyond where you observed data is exactly what the degree-9 polynomial did so catastrophically — and the intercept is a mild, permanent instance of it sitting in every regression output you will ever read.

The standard fix: centre the feature — replace \(x_i\) with \(x_i - \bar{x}\) — and the intercept becomes the predicted outcome at the average income of $38,700, a real interpretable quantity about a kind of place that exists. Nothing else changes: not the slope, not any prediction. It matters more in Chapter 13.

Chapter 3's lesson again: a number in your output is not automatically a fact about the world. It is a fact about the model, and translating between the two is your job rather than the software's.

3 min. Mechanically the intercept is what makes the line pass through the sample means and forces residuals to average to zero; interpretively it is usually a nuisance parameter. It becomes interpretable only when zero is a value the feature actually takes AND you have data near it.

Is Your RMSE Any Good?

RMSE has units and no ceiling, so a raw RMSE is uninterpretable on its own. Always compare against something.

Against the naive baseline

Predict \(\bar{y}\) for everyone. If your model cannot beat it, it has no value. This is exactly what \(R^2\) reports — the honest case for \(R^2\) as a companion to RMSE, not a replacement.

Against the outcome's spread

RMSE of $83,700 against a standard deviation of $115,000 says the model has absorbed some but not most of the variation.

Against the decision

The one that matters and the one nobody teaches. If a $50,000 error costs nothing and a $200,000 error is catastrophic, RMSE may be the wrong loss function entirely.

Naive baseline (predict the mean): 1.1449
Our model:                         0.8421
Improvement:                       26.4%

A 26% reduction in typical error from a single predictor. Worth having, and a long way from solved.

Discussion: The Lender's Asymmetry

Squared error treats a $100,000 over-prediction and a $100,000 under-prediction as equally bad.

A lender who over-estimates lends too much against a property and is exposed when the borrower defaults. A lender who under-estimates loses a customer to a competitor.

Prompt A

Are those costs equal? Which is worse, and roughly by how much?

Prompt B

If they are not equal, what should change — the model, the metric, or the reported quantity? Name one concrete change.

1 min think → 2 min pairs → 1 min share

4 min, press 4. Over-prediction is far more expensive for a secured lender, so the loss is asymmetric and RMSE optimises for a symmetric world the lender does not live in. Concrete changes they reach: an asymmetric (quantile / pinball) loss; report a low quantile rather than the mean; evaluate only on the segment that will be acted on; report the PI as the deliverable. Someone usually notes that residual spread rises 27% in expensive neighbourhoods so aggregate RMSE hides the errors that matter most for large loans — take that one and run with it.

"A Better Model Is One With Lower RMSE"

Lower RMSE on the same test set with the same loss function is genuinely better. But RMSE is a choice, and it encodes an assumption that over- and under-prediction are equally costly and that all observations matter equally. Whenever those assumptions are wrong — and in economic applications they usually are — the model with the lowest RMSE is not the model that produces the best decisions.

Choose the metric before you choose the model, and choose it from the decision rather than from habit.

1. The metric follows the decision

Asymmetric costs → asymmetric loss. Only the top decile acted on → evaluate the top decile.

2. Aggregate error hides what matters

The 27% spread rise across quintiles is averaged away by one headline RMSE.

3. The interval is often the deliverable

"$254,000" invites false confidence. "$254,000, and we would not be surprised by anything between $90,000 and $418,000" is honest — and more useful.

Frequently the prediction is not precise enough to act on — and discovering that is a real result rather than a failure.

2 min. Carry these three into every chapter that follows. They are the professional half of the course.

Key Takeaways

1

A regression can estimate a parameter or generate a prediction; the algebra is identical and almost everything else differs — which variables belong, which metric decides success, what you report.

2

OLS minimises squared vertical distances, which gives it a closed form, forces it through the sample means, and makes it as outlier-sensitive as the arithmetic mean.

3

In-sample \(R^2\) cannot decrease when a predictor is added — not even a column of random noise — so in-sample fit measures flexibility as much as accuracy. Adjusted \(R^2\) is not the fix.

4

Holding out a test set reveals what in-sample error conceals: training error falls monotonically while test error eventually explodes, and every in-sample diagnostic will select the worst model in that sequence.

5

A prediction needs an uncertainty statement, and the prediction interval is far wider than the confidence interval — more data shrinks the second and barely touches the first.
