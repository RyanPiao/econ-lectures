What Changes Today

Chapters 12–19 all assumed rows were independent draws from one distribution. You shuffled freely in cross-validation and in the bootstrap. Today we drop that.

The constraint

You may no longer shuffle. Shuffling lets a model see the future — and its error metric will look wonderful right up until deployment.

The enrichment

Section 1

The Grammar of Time Series

Three components, one operator, and a picture of memory

13 min for this section. Board work: draw trend + seasonal wave + noise cloud stacking into the observed series. That sketch is the conceptual anchor for the whole hour.

Every Series Is Three Things

Trend \(T_t\)

The long-run direction. Is the economy growing? Is ridership declining?

Seasonality \(S_t\)

A repeating pattern at a fixed period. Retail spikes in December. Boston home sales peak in spring.

Residual \(R_t\)

Everything left over: noise, plus any structure you missed.

3 min. Point at the top panel and ask what they see; then reveal the three panels below it as the answer. The observed panel is deliberately the messiest — that is the point.

Additive or Multiplicative?

Additive

$$Y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

Reading: what you observe is a long-run level, plus a predictable seasonal offset, plus unpredictable noise. Estimate \(T_t\) and \(S_t\) well and \(R_t\) should look like white noise.

Multiplicative

$$Y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t$$

Reading: use this when the seasonal swing scales with the level — a 10% holiday bump is a bigger dollar swing when sales are bigger.

How to choose: look at the raw plot. Constant-height zig-zag → additive. Zig-zag that grows with the line → multiplicative. The shortcut: take logs, which turns multiplication into addition, then use the additive machinery. Retail sales: multiplicative. Unemployment claims: additive.

3 min. Board sketch: two little series side by side, one with constant seasonal amplitude and one with growing amplitude. Students who see this once rarely get the choice wrong again.

The Lag Operator

$$Ly_t = y_{t-1}, \qquad L^k y_t = y_{t-k}$$

Reading: \(L\) just means “look one period back.” It is bookkeeping notation, nothing more.

Why bother

It makes differencing compact: \(y_t - Ly_t = (1-L)y_t\). One symbol, one clear meaning.

Where you meet it next

Chapter 21. \(L\) is the backbone of ARIMA notation — the AR, the I and the MA are all written in it.

1 min. Do not linger. Undergraduates over-invest in the notation and under-invest in the ACF two slides from now.

Autocorrelation — Measuring Memory

$$\rho(k) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,\, Y_{t-k})}{\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)}$$

Reading: how strongly does today resemble \(k\) periods ago? \(\rho(0) = 1\) always — a series is perfectly correlated with itself — and \(|\rho(k)| \leq 1\).

If \(\rho(1) = 0.95\), yesterday explains about \(0.95^2 \approx 90\%\) of today's variance. That is typical of GDP or employment — macro series have long memories.

Slow decay across many lags → long memory → almost certainly non-stationary

Sharp drop to near zero after a lag or two → likely stationary

3 min. The two signatures on the bottom row are the takeaway, not the formula. Students will read hundreds of ACF plots and derive the estimator zero times.

Two Signatures, One Series

Levels: still 0.77 at lag 10. The series remembers two and a half years back.

First difference: inside the band by lag 3. Memory gone.

Key Concept — Temporal Dependence

Time series observations are not independent. Each value depends on its predecessors. That single fact invalidates three things you have been doing all semester:

Random shuffling in cross-validation — Chapter 15

Standard bootstrap resampling — Chapter 7

The i.i.d. error assumption of OLS — Chapter 12

From here on, every method must respect chronological order.

Section 2

Decomposition

Taking the series apart — and finding out what was actually news

15 min. If you run one thing live today, run the STL cell. The four-panel plot is the most valuable image in the deck.

Classical Decomposition, in One Slide

For an additive model with seasonal period \(m\), estimate the trend with a centered moving average:

$$\hat{T}_t = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=-\lfloor m/2 \rfloor}^{\lfloor m/2 \rfloor} y_{t+j}$$

Reading: average over one full seasonal cycle. Anything that repeats within the cycle averages out; what survives is the trend. For monthly data with annual seasonality, \(m = 12\).

2. Detrend
\(y_t - \hat{T}_t\)

3. Seasonal
average the detrended values within each season — all the Januaries, all the Februaries

4. Residual
\(\hat{R}_t = y_t - \hat{T}_t - \hat{S}_t\)

3 min. Four steps, one formula. They will run this in Lab Part 2, so the goal here is that the four steps are recognisable, not memorised.

Three Reasons Classical Is Not Enough

1. The seasonal pattern is frozen

Classical forces every December to carry the same factor. But executed on RSXFSN, STL shows the peak-to-trough seasonal swing narrowing from 28.3 percentage points in 2001 to 23.7 in 2024 — online retail flattened the holiday spike. Classical cannot see that.

2. Outliers contaminate the trend

A moving average has no defence against April 2020. The shock gets averaged into the trend, and then the residual is wrong on both sides of it.

3. One seasonal period only

Hourly data with both a daily and a weekly cycle is out of reach.

2 min. Failure 1 is the one they can see with their own eyes on the next slide. Failures 2 and 3 you can state and move on.

Frozen vs. Drifting

Same series, same period. The left panel reports one answer for 26 years; the right panel lets the answer move.

2 min. Executed on FRED RSXFSN, 319 monthly observations. Ask them what the right-hand panel implies for a retailer's December staffing model built in 2005 and never revisited. Also flag the grey note in the left panel: March is +1.0%, ABOVE trend — students routinely assume the post-holiday slump runs through spring, and it does not.

STL — Seasonal-Trend Decomposition Using LOESS

STL (Cleveland et al. 1990) fits both the trend and the seasonal component with locally weighted regression, so both are allowed to drift gradually.

stl = STL(np.log(retail), period=12, robust=True).fit()

Why np.log

Not decoration. STL is additive; retail sales are multiplicative. \(\log(T \cdot S \cdot R) = \log T + \log S + \log R\), so the log converts one into the other.

Why robust=True

It downweights outliers, so a catastrophic month cannot drag the trend down with it. Critical for economic data, where recessions and pandemics are not rare.

3 min. Run this live if you can — it is three lines and the plot is the payoff. If the FRED pull is slow, have the figure ready as a fallback.

The trend barely moved

Debrief: It Is a Modelling Claim, Not a Statistic

robust=True says

“April 2020 was an aberration. The long-run level did not change.”

robust=False says

“The level itself moved. Carry that forward.”

For 2027 retail sales you want robust. Sales recovered, so the shock was transient — and the observed series proves it, climbing well past its pre-COVID level.

The opposite case is a genuine regime change. MBTA subway ridership never came back. Putting 2020 in the residual there would leave you forecasting a level that no longer exists.

Choosing robust is choosing whether you believe the shock was permanent. Make that choice out loud.

Land the fragment. This is the first time in the course a parameter is openly an assertion about the world rather than a tuning knob; name that shift.

Common Misconception

“Seasonal adjustment removes all time-series patterns.”

It removes the seasonal component — and nothing else. A seasonally adjusted GDP series is still \(I(1)\) and still strongly autocorrelated.

The rule: test stationarity after seasonal adjustment, not instead of it. Only the STL residual approximates white noise, and only when the decomposition is well specified.

1 min. This misconception costs students real marks: they see "SA" in a FRED series name and skip the ADF test. It also matters for Lab Part 7 — BOXRHTNSA versus BOXRHTSA.

Section 3

Stationarity

The prerequisite for every forecast you will ever make

17 min — the densest section of the hour. Slow down. The 2x2 table gets its own slide and its own board sketch.

Weak Stationarity — Three Conditions

1. \(E[Y_t] = \mu\) for all \(t\) — the mean does not drift

2. \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t) = \sigma^2\) for all \(t\) — the variance does not explode

3. \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-k}) = \gamma(k)\) — autocovariance depends only on the distance \(k\), never on when you look

Reading: stationarity means the series' rules are the same in 1995 and in 2025. Every forecasting model learns rules from the past and projects them forward. If the rules keep changing, the model is learning things that expire.

3 min. Condition 3 is the one they skip. Give it a concrete gloss: "how much does January tell you about February" must be the same answer in every year of the sample.

Why This Has a Dollar Cost

The Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall measures from Chapter 5 are computed straight from a historical return distribution.

That computation assumes the distribution is the same going forward as it was going backward. Which is a stationarity assumption, whether or not anyone writes it down.

2008. VaR models calibrated on the low-volatility “Great Moderation” were blindsided when volatility tripled. The models were not badly implemented. They were correctly implemented on an assumption that had just stopped being true.

Stationarity is not a technicality you clear before the interesting part. It is where the risk lives.

2 min. Callback to Chapter 5. Keep it short — the job here is to convert stationarity from a box-ticking exercise into something with consequences.

The Random Walk — and Its Cure

$$Y_t = Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathrm{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$$

Reading: today equals yesterday plus a shock. There is no level to revert to — the series wanders. And its variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t) = t\sigma^2\), which grows without bound. Condition 2 fails.

$$\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1} = (1-L)Y_t = \varepsilon_t$$

Reading: difference it once and the wandering cancels, leaving pure white noise. A series that becomes stationary after \(d\) differences is integrated of order \(d\), written \(I(d)\).

Real GDP in levels is \(I(1)\). Quarterly GDP growth is \(I(0)\).

3 min. The two equations are one idea read forwards and backwards. If a series is built by accumulating shocks, differencing un-accumulates it.

The Same Series, Twice

2 min. FRED GDPC1, 1990 Q1 to 2026 Q2. Both boxes are executed output, not illustrations. The 2020 pair is worth naming: the largest quarterly fall and the largest quarterly rise in the sample are adjacent.

ADF — Testing for a Unit Root

$$\Delta Y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p}\delta_j \Delta Y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t$$

\(H_0: \gamma = 0\)
unit root → non-stationary

\(H_1: \gamma < 0\)
mean-reverting → stationary

Reading: if \(\gamma < 0\), a high value today pulls tomorrow down — there is a level to come back to. Reject the null and you have evidence of stationarity. The lagged differences are there only to soak up autocorrelation.

Watch out: the statistic does not follow a \(t\)-distribution. Compare against Dickey-Fuller critical values — statsmodels reports them for you, so read them rather than eyeballing 1.96.

3 min. Do not derive the distribution. Do insist on the last box — students who compare an ADF statistic to 1.96 get the wrong answer with total confidence.

KPSS — and Why You Run Both

ADF

\(H_0\): unit root
\(H_1\): stationary

KPSS

\(H_0\): stationary
\(H_1\): unit root

Opposite nulls, deliberately. “Failed to reject” is not “confirmed” — it can just mean a low-powered test on a short sample. Two tests pointing the same way is evidence; one test pointing anywhere is a hint.

This is the same logic as reporting precision and recall in Chapter 18. One number can be made to look good on its own.

2 min. The Chapter 18 callback earns its place — students already accept that a single classification metric is manipulable, and this is the same move.

Poll: Both Tests Reject

You run both tests on a series.

Answer: C — Detrend

Both rejecting is not a contradiction — it is a third diagnosis. A trend-stationary series reverts to a deterministic trend, so ADF finds mean reversion while KPSS correctly finds a non-constant mean. Remove the trend and what is left is stationary.

A would over-difference: you induce negative autocorrelation and inflate the forecast variance. B throws away half the evidence. D discards the most informative outcome — the joint pattern IS the answer, which is the entire reason to run two tests.

Debrief: Disagreement Is a Finding

1. The sample spans regimes. 1960 onwards contains the Great Inflation, the Volcker disinflation and the Great Moderation. GDP growth is not one process across those — mean and variance both shift. And dropping 2020 does not fix it: the 1960 sample still fails at 0.762, \(p = 0.010\). This is not a COVID artifact.

2. Three reasons. A structural break inside the differenced series; a changing variance (the Great Moderation is literally a variance break); a remaining deterministic trend. Over- or under-differencing is the fourth.

3. Pick one and say so. Shorten the sample to a single regime, model the break explicitly (Section 4), or report results regime by regime.

The wrong move is to report the ADF result and quietly not mention KPSS. Test disagreement is a finding, not an inconvenience.

Land the fragment. Then say the sentence that transitions the whole lecture: everything in this section assumed the rules were constant. What if they are not?

Common Misconception

“My model is fine on non-stationary data — I included a time trend.”

True for a deterministic trend. If the series really does revert to a straight line, a time trend term handles it.

False for a stochastic trend. With a unit root, adding \(t\) to the regression produces a spurious regression: inflated \(R^2\), invalid \(t\)-statistics, and no predictive power at all.

The ADF/KPSS pair tells you which case you are in. Test; never assume.

1 min. Spurious regression is the reason this matters: two independent random walks will show an R-squared near 0.9 roughly as often as not.

Section 4

Structural Breaks

When the rules themselves change

15 min. The Nvidia/Peloton pair is the best 90 seconds in the deck. Show both BEFORE the poll and do not label which is which.

Four Kinds of Break

1. Level shift. MBTA weekday ridership fell from ~1.2M trips to a trough near 142,000 in spring 2020; by 2025 it had recovered to roughly three-quarters of its pre-pandemic level.

2. Trend break. U.S. labor productivity grew ~2.5%/yr 1995–2005, then ~1.2% 2005–2019. Same direction, different slope.

3. Variance break. Equity volatility ran ~15% annualized, then exceeded 80% in 2008 and 2020.

4. Parameter break. Post-COVID, unemployment hit 3.4% while inflation stayed above 3% — the Phillips Curve relationship moved.

3 min. A break is a PERMANENT change in the data-generating process — not a spike, not an outlier. Say that before you walk the four panels, or students will file all four under "big number."

Breaks Interact With Survivorship Bias

Chapter 6 callback. Analyze only the banks that survived 2008 and your parameters describe survivors, not the population.

Trained on surviving banks

Shows lower pre-crisis risk. The riskiest institutions are simply absent from the sample.

Trained on all banks

Shows the real distribution — including the tail that failed.

The break did not just change the parameters. It removed observations selectively, biasing everything estimated afterwards. A break is a sampling event as well as a parameter event.

2 min. This is the subtlest idea in Section 4 and worth the time — it connects two chapters students file separately.

Two Companies Whose Revenue Exploded

Both more than doubled in two years. Only one of them was a change in the growth rate.

2 min. Do NOT say which is which. Show the two panels, state the sentence at the bottom, and go straight to the poll. Nvidia figures are data-center revenue in Nvidia fiscal quarters — FY2024 Q4 is calendar Q4 2023.

A Detail That Silently Corrupts Event Studies

Nvidia's fiscal year ends in late January. So FY2024 Q4 is calendar Q4 2023 — roughly a full year earlier than the label suggests.

Line that series up against a calendar-quarter macro series without adjusting, and every “before” observation you thought you had is an “after”. Your break date moves by four quarters and nothing in the output complains.

State which calendar a series is on before you difference it.

1 min. Small slide, real defect. Every year at least one final project misaligns fiscal and calendar quarters; this is the inoculation.

Poll: Trend Break or Level Shift?

A trend break means the growth rate permanently changed. A level shift means the level jumped temporarily around an unchanged growth path.

Nvidia and Peloton — which is which?

A) Nvidia = trend break, Peloton = level shift
B) Nvidia = level shift, Peloton = trend break

1 min. Vote before any labelling. C usually takes a real share, and it is the answer that cost Peloton $400 million.

Answer: A — Nvidia Trend, Peloton Level

Nvidia's break came from a persistent exogenous shock — AI compute demand did not revert, and the new slope held. Peloton's was demand pulled forward under a temporary constraint; when gyms reopened the level fell back toward the old path, and the company posted a $2.8B net loss in fiscal 2022 with $400M sunk into a factory it did not need.

C is the expensive trap. The size of a move says nothing about its permanence — and that exact confusion built the factory. D is the counsel of despair: decomposition gives you evidence in real time. If STL's trend takes a new slope, it is a trend break. If the trend returns to its old trajectory and the move sits in the residual, it was a level shift.

The Chow Test — a Break at a Known Date

$$F = \frac{(\mathrm{RSS}_R - \mathrm{RSS}_U)/k}{\mathrm{RSS}_U/(n - 2k)}$$

\(\mathrm{RSS}_R\) — one pooled regression across the whole sample (restricted: no break)

\(\mathrm{RSS}_U = \mathrm{RSS}_1 + \mathrm{RSS}_2\) — two separate regressions, before and after \(\tau\)

Reading: if splitting the sample fits much better than pooling it, the parameters differ across \(\tau\). A large \(F\) is evidence of a break. \(k\) is the number of parameters, \(n\) the total observations.

The catch: you must name \(\tau\) in advance. Search over \(\tau\) for the biggest \(F\) and the null distribution no longer applies.

3 min. The catch box is the pivot into PELT and into Discussion 3. Naming the date in advance is what makes Chow strong; searching for it is what makes an algorithm necessary.

Unknown Dates: Bai-Perron and PELT

Bai-Perron (1998) extends Chow to unknown dates, testing sequentially for multiple breaks. Worth knowing: Hall et al. (2025) show it loses power when breaks sit near the ends of the sample — which is exactly where COVID sits.

gdp_growth = gdp.pct_change().dropna() * 100
algo = rpt.Pelt(model="rbf").fit(gdp_growth.values)
breakpoints = algo.predict(pen=1)

model="rbf" detects shifts in the whole distribution — mean and variance. Which is what a recession does.

pen is the penalty for adding a break. Higher → fewer, more confident breaks. It is not chosen for you.

The algorithm found the breaks at the penalty you chose. Same series, same code: 28 breaks, or none.

Economist at Work — Boston Fed

When central banks called the 2021 inflation surge “transitory,” the failure was one of decomposition, not of data. Traditional models treated headline inflation as one noisy series and could not separate demand-pull from supply-push.

The Sign-Restricted Dynamic Factor Model decomposes total PCE inflation into six latent components: common demand for goods, common demand for services, common supply of goods, common supply of services, long-term expectations, and idiosyncratic category noise.

The identifying trick is theory. A positive demand shock raises prices and quantities; a negative supply shock raises prices but lowers quantities. Those sign restrictions are enough to tell the six components apart.

The decomposition showed inflation had moved from isolated supply-chain disruption to broad, persistent demand — and that read shaped the timing of monetary tightening. (Leiva-León et al. 2025, Boston Fed WP 25-5)

The same three-line idea you ran on retail sales — at policy scale.

2 min. This closes the hook. The Fed missed in 2021; the fix was better decomposition, and it was built ten minutes down the Orange Line.

Section 5

Never Shuffle

How to stop lying to yourself about accuracy

6 min. Frame this as the payoff, not the appendix: now that you know the rules can change, here is how you validate honestly.

Random \(k\)-Fold on a Time Series Is Data Leakage

Standard 5-fold CV assigns months to folds at random. Fold 3 might train on January 2020 and test on December 2019.

The model “learns” from January and “predicts” December. It is predicting the past from the future. The error looks wonderful because the model is cheating.

Chapter 6 callback: shuffling a time series is a form of data leakage. Future information contaminates the training set. Cross-validation still exists for time series — it just has to respect time.

2 min. Say the concrete fold explicitly. "Leakage" as an abstraction does not land; "trains on January, predicts December" does.

Two Honest Alternatives

Expanding window. Train on \(t = 1 \ldots T_0\), test on the next \(h\); then train on \(t = 1 \ldots T_0 + h\), test on the \(h\) after that. All history is used.

Sliding window. Fixed-length training set that moves forward, dropping the oldest data as it goes.

2 min. The one rule both obey: the training set always precedes the test set chronologically. Everything else is a design choice.

Slide rather than expand when old data describes a different regime. If a break occurred in 2020, training on 2015–2019 may hurt more than it helps. Hespeler et al. (2025) find sliding windows beat expanding ones exactly under structural breaks.

2 min. Land the last box: Sections 3, 4 and 5 are one argument, not three. The break test tells you which window to use.

Common Misconception

“I can just do a simple 80/20 split on my time series.”

One split evaluates your model on exactly one future period — which might be unusually easy or unusually hard, and you have no way to tell which.

Time series CV generates multiple folds, each preserving order, so you can estimate the variance of your forecast error rather than one point. Same logic as \(k\)-fold in Chapter 15, adapted for time.

1 min. Note that an 80/20 split on a 2015-2025 series tests entirely on 2023-2025 — the post-break regime. Whether that flatters or punishes the model is pure luck.

Five Things to Carry Out

1. Time series violates the independence assumption behind everything in Ch 12–19. Order matters.

2. Decomposition separates signal from noise. Robust STL puts shocks in the residual instead of bending the trend. Only the residual is news.

3. Stationarity is the prerequisite. ADF and KPSS have opposite nulls — run both, read the 2×2 table. When they disagree, that is the finding.

4. Structural breaks invalidate trained models. Report your penalty. Use Chow for a date you named in advance.

5. Never shuffle. Expanding or sliding windows, always.
