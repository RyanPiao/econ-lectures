From a Line to a Plane

Section 1  •  LO 13.1, 13.2  •  13 min

What 0.4179 Actually Says

Chapter 12's model. Compare two block groups whose incomes differ by $10,000 — how much do their house values differ? It bundles in everything that travels with income: newer houses, more rooms, better locations, different densities.

Add average rooms. The income coefficient now answers a different question: compare two block groups whose incomes differ by $10,000 but which have the same average number of rooms.

A multivariate coefficient is a partial effect. Not “the effect of income” — the effect of income holding the other variables in this particular model fixed. Change the model and you change what the coefficient means.

3 minutes. This is the single most important sentence in the chapter and everything else follows from it. Say the key-result box twice, once fast and once slow. Note that we have not written a single equation yet — intuition before formalism is deliberate.

The Geometry Changes

One predictor fits a line through a 2-D scatter. That was Chapter 12.

Two predictors fit a plane through a 3-D cloud.

Ten predictors fit a ten-dimensional hyperplane, which nobody can picture — and which behaves exactly like the plane in every way that matters.

The mathematics does not care how many dimensions you use. Your ability to check the result by looking at it does.

2 minutes. Draw the plane on the board too — students need to see it built once, even though it stops being drawable immediately afterwards. Point at the figure and say: reading the income slope means walking along one floor axis with your foot fixed on the other.

The Formalism, Once

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i \]

Same line as Chapter 12, more slopes. Each \(\beta_j\) is one predictor's own slope.

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \]

X is the design matrix — a spreadsheet. One row per block group, one column per predictor, plus a leading column of ones for the intercept.

\[ \boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \]

Minimises the same sum of squared residuals as Ch 12. The inverse is what “solve for all the slopes at once” looks like. You will never compute it by hand.

Watch the inverse. It exists only if no column of \(\mathbf{X}\) is an exact linear combination of the others. That condition is about to matter twice — exactly, on the next slide, and nearly, in Section 6.

3 minutes. Every equation gets its plain-language line and none of them gets manipulated. Do not prove anything. The only thing to land is that the inverse can fail, because that sets up both the dummy trap and multicollinearity.

The Dummy Variable Trap

Most real controls are categorical — a zip code, an industry, a job level. A \(k\)-level category becomes \(k\) binary indicator columns, and you may include only \(k-1\) of them.

Why, mechanically

All \(k\) indicators sum to a column of ones for every row — which is the intercept column. \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) becomes singular. No unique solution exists.

Not a convention. An arithmetic impossibility.

The fix

Drop one level. It becomes the reference category, and every remaining coefficient is a difference from that baseline — never an absolute level.

statsmodels: C(gender)
sklearn: OneHotEncoder(drop='first')

Version gotcha. sparse_output, not sparse. The old argument now raises rather than warning, so every tutorial written before the change fails outright on your machine.

2 minutes and no more. "The matrix is singular" is a satisfying, concrete reason and a guaranteed interview question — that is why it earns 2 minutes. Do NOT put encoder code on the board. Flag the sparse_output change out loud; students will hit old tutorials this week.

More Rooms, Lower Value?

Holding income constant, block groups with more rooms per household have lower median house values. The coefficient is −0.0381.

Read literally

Absurd. Nobody pays less for a bigger house.

Read as a partial effect

Among neighbourhoods at the same median income, the ones with more rooms per household are the more rural, less dense, cheaper-land areas.

Write this down. A partial effect is not a ceteris paribus experiment unless you can hold the other variables fixed in the world, not just in the model. You cannot add a room to a house without changing what kind of neighbourhood it is.

2 minutes. This is the single most common misreading of regression output in professional work. The coefficient is not telling you what a bedroom is worth — it is telling you what a neighbourhood with more rooms at the same income is worth, and those are different questions.

Omitted Variable Bias

Section 2  •  LO 13.3  •  10 min

What a Missing Variable Does

Leave out a variable that matters and the variables you did include absorb its effect.

If tenure raises wages, and men in your sample have longer tenure, then a regression of wages on gender alone attributes part of the tenure story to gender. There is nothing else in the model to hold it.

Condition 1

The omitted variable must affect the outcome.

Condition 2

It must be correlated with the predictor you care about.

Both must hold. Fail either and there is no bias — which is why not every unmeasured variable is a threat. Students over-correct after Chapter 10 and start treating every missing column as fatal. Most are not.

2 minutes. The reassurance in the key-result box matters as much as the warning — this course has spent a chapter making them afraid of unmeasured confounders and the two-condition test is what makes the fear operational instead of paralysing.

The Formula — and Why It Is Useful

True model \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \varepsilon_i\). You estimate the short regression, omitting \(x_2\). Then:

\[ \hat{\beta}_1^{\text{short}} \;\xrightarrow{\;p\;}\; \beta_1 + \underbrace{\beta_2\,\delta}_{\text{bias}} \]

\(\beta_2\) — how much the omitted variable matters to the outcome.

\(\delta\) — how strongly it travels with your predictor (slope of the omitted variable on the included one).

Two signs multiply. So you can predict the direction of your error before you have the data — from an argument about the world alone.

2 minutes. Interpret every symbol out loud. Then immediately go to the poll — do NOT show the numbers first. This is the chapter's own instruction and it works: the room votes wrong.

Poll 2: Sign It Before You See It

Women in this simulated Greater Boston workforce have shorter tenure. Tenure raises pay. We regress wage on female alone, omitting tenure.

The measured gender gap will look —

A) Smaller than the truth — some of the “gap” is really tenure, and leaving tenure out lets the model take credit for it
B) Larger than the truth
C) Unchanged — tenure is not part of the gender effect
D) Larger or smaller — the formula cannot sign it
Count the votes out loud before advancing. The chapter records that most rooms say "smaller". Ask one A-voter to defend it before the reveal — their reasoning is worth hearing, because it is a real intuition and not a straw man.

Answer: B — Larger

Estimate −8,412 against a truth of −6,000.

Sign it: \(\beta_2 > 0\) (tenure raises wages) and \(\delta < 0\) (women have less tenure), so \(\beta_2\delta < 0\) — the bias pushes the coefficient down, i.e. more negative, i.e. a bigger apparent gap.

A is the majority answer in most rooms. Students assume omitted variables always flatter a result — that a real effect shrinks once you control properly. It goes whichever way the two signs send it.

D is wrong for a specific reason: the formula signs the bias perfectly once you commit to the two signs. What you cannot do is sign it without an argument about the world.

Name the misconception explicitly. This is the assessment moment for LO 13.3 — if the room votes A and then can re-derive B from the two signs, the objective is met.

The Bias Is the Formula

Simulated Greater Boston payroll, n = 5,000. Built with a true gender effect of −$6,000 and tenure worth $1,800 a year.

\(\beta_2 \times \delta\)

\(= 1802.5 \times (-1.3899)\)

\(= -2{,}505\)

short − long = −2,505

They agree exactly. The bias is not roughly the formula — it is the formula. This is an identity, not a rule of thumb.

2 minutes. The exactness surprises people; let it. Note that the long regression lands on minus 5,907 against a truth of minus 6,000 — that residual gap is ordinary sampling noise, not bias, and it is worth naming so nobody thinks the "long" model is also broken.

Think Like an Economist

Before running a regression, list the variables you cannot measure. For each, ask the two questions: does it affect the outcome? and does it correlate with my predictor?

If both are yes, you already know which way you will be wrong.

Write that prediction down in advance and in writing. An analyst who predicted upward bias and then observed a large positive coefficient has learned much less than they think. One who predicted it correctly has a real argument to make.

Chapter 10 called this a backdoor path. This is the same thing written as algebra instead of as a graph.

1 minute. Transition to Section 3: "we have said 'holding tenure fixed' about nine times now. Let us find out what that phrase actually means, because it turns out to be a literal three-step operation you can run."

What “Controlling For” Actually Does

Section 3  •  Frisch–Waugh–Lovell  •  8 min

A Phrase Everyone Uses and Few Can Define

Here is what “controlling for tenure” is, as a procedure you can actually run:

1

Take the part of wages that tenure cannot explain — the residuals from wage ~ tenure.

2

Take the part of gender that tenure cannot explain — the residuals from female ~ tenure.

3

Regress leftover on leftover.

That is the multivariate coefficient. Exactly, not approximately. This is the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem (1933, 1963), and it turns a vague phrase into a three-line procedure.

2.5 min. Reveal the three steps one at a time, then the theorem. In lab you will run exactly these three lines. Say the words "strip out, strip out, relate what is left" — students remember the rhythm.

The only honest visual check of a controlled relationship. Chapter 3's rule — plot before you trust — applies with full force.

2 min. The two clumps in the scatter are the two genders after tenure has been partialled out; each is a cloud rather than a point because tenure does not fully predict gender. Identical to machine precision — not similar, the same number.

Poll 3: What About the Standard Error?

FWL reproduced the coefficient to machine precision.

What about the standard error from that two-step route?

A) Also identical — same coefficient, same data, same uncertainty
B) Too large — the two-step throws information away
C) Too small — the second stage does not know a variable was partialled out first
D) Undefined — you cannot get a standard error from a residual regression
60 seconds. A is the natural inference from "the coefficient matched exactly" and that is exactly the trap.

Answer: C — Too Small

SE from FWL 258.33  •  SE from the full regression 258.38

The second-stage regression does not know a variable was already used up, so it never adjusts its degrees of freedom for it — and reports slightly more confidence than the data supports. With one control, trivial. With fifty, not.

Multi-step estimators transfer point estimates faithfully and standard errors carelessly. Use FWL to understand your model. Use the full regression to report.

Write the principle on the board — it recurs throughout applied econometrics and it is the identical failure mode as a hand-rolled two-stage least squares in the graduate track. Assessment moment for LO 13.2.

Why FWL Matters Beyond the Algebra

It makes “controlling for” operational. You can stop treating it as a magic phrase, and you can say exactly which variation the estimate is using.

It explains the partial regression plot — the only honest way to look at a relationship you have controlled for.

It is the foundation of Double Machine Learning (Ch 24). DML is exactly this procedure with the two first-stage regressions replaced by flexible ML models. Residualise both sides, then regress the leftovers. The logic does not change at all.

“Holding constant” means using only the variation in \(x_j\) that the other variables cannot account for. If they account for nearly all of it, there is very little left to work with — which is exactly Section 6.

1.5 min. Say the Chapter 24 line clearly — it is a forward seed the causal ML lecture depends on, and 3916 is the only track that ever sees FWL developed. Then transition: "so far every control has been a good one. Now the uncomfortable part."

Good Controls and Bad Controls

Section 4  •  LO 13.4  •  the centre of the chapter  •  10 min

Chapter 10's Taxonomy, Priced in Dollars

Confounder

Causes both the treatment and the outcome.

Control it. That removes bias.

Mediator

Sits on the causal path from treatment to outcome.

Control it and you remove the part of the effect that travels through it. That is not a correction.

Controlling for a mediator is a change of question, not a fix.

The hook is exactly this case. If being a woman reduces the chance of promotion, and promotion raises pay, then job level is a mediator.

2 min. Before advancing, build the data-generating process on the board: a direct penalty of 3,000 dollars, plus a promotion penalty of 0.80 job levels at 9,000 dollars a level. Make the class compute the total themselves — 3,000 + 7,200 = 10,200 — BEFORE any code runs. That participation is what makes the next slide land.

Interactions

Section 5  •  when the effect depends on who you are  •  6 min

One Effect for Everybody?

Every model so far assumed each predictor has one effect, the same for everyone. Suppose the return to experience differs by group. A single tenure slope cannot represent that — it fits one compromise slope through both groups and reports it as though it described either.

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t_i + \beta_2 f_i + \beta_3 (t_i \times f_i) + \varepsilon_i \]

\(\beta_1\) — the return to tenure for the reference group (men, coded zero).

\(\beta_2\) — the gap at tenure zero. Not the average gap.

\(\beta_3\) — how much the return to tenure differs for women.

The return to tenure is \(\beta_1\) for men and \(\beta_1 + \beta_3\) for women. And the pay gap becomes \(\beta_2 + \beta_3 \times \text{tenure}\) — a function, not a number.

2 min. CUT POINT: if you are behind at 0:56, skip this whole section and go straight to multicollinearity. Nothing downstream depends on it and Ch 14 picks interactions up again.

Reading an Interaction Without Getting It Wrong

Misconception: “the coefficient on female is the gender pay gap.”

In a model containing tenure:female it is the gap at tenure = 0. If nobody in your data has zero tenure, that number describes nobody.

Fix: evaluate \(\beta_2 + \beta_3 \bar{t}\) at mean tenure, or centre the interacting variable before fitting — the same centring trick Chapter 12 used on the intercept, one level up.

Three cautions, one line each

Power. You are estimating a difference in slopes. A null interaction is weak evidence of homogeneity, not proof of it.

Common support. The model extrapolates freely. If almost no women have 20 years, −10,159 is arithmetic, not observation.

Functional form. A product term tests one very specific shape — that the effect changes linearly in the moderator.

2 min. The centring trick is the practical takeaway — most students will need it in the capstone. Transition: "one last failure mode, and it is the one that shows you what your data can and cannot answer."

Multicollinearity

Section 6  •  LO 13.2  •  9 min

When the Others Explain Almost All of It

FWL said the multivariate coefficient uses only the variation a predictor has that the others cannot explain. So what happens when they can explain almost all of it? You are left estimating an effect from a sliver of residual variation — and slivers are noisy.

Add a near-duplicate of tenure — yrs_at_firm, “years at this firm,” which here is just tenure plus a little measurement noise. Nothing genuinely new has entered the model.

VariableVIF
female1.33
tenure48.82
job_level2.12
yrs_at_firm47.78

\[ \mathrm{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} \]

where \(R_j^2\) comes from regressing \(x_j\) on all the other predictors

Plain language: “how much of this variable do the other variables already know?” VIF = 1 means orthogonal — nothing is shared. Conventional thresholds flag 5 or 10 as serious. Tenure and its twin sit near 48.

2.5 min. Note that female and job_level are barely affected — a high VIF is a statement about specific columns, not about the model as a whole. That distinction is the poll in four slides.

What It Wrecks, and What It Does Not

1. The standard error exploded. 28.1 → 147.2, five times wider. The classic signature: a model that is clearly working, made of coefficients that individually look like nothing.

2. The coefficients split arbitrarily. Tenure keeps 820.7, the twin takes 45.8 — and that division is essentially noise. Resample and it shifts, possibly changing signs.

3. R² is identical to five decimal places. 0.80622 both ways. The predictions did not move. Neither did the coefficient on gender — which is what we actually cared about.

3 min. Show the VIF table, then the SE explosion, then — LAST, and slowly — the identical R-squared. Give that reveal space; it is the moment of the section. Ask which model a forecaster should use and which an economist should use, and make the class notice they are the same model.

B is the tempting one — “redundant variable” sounds like it should cost you fit. But adding any column can only weakly increase in-sample R², never decrease it. That was Chapter 12.

C is the panic answer, and it is exactly why people delete variables they should keep. The right reading of a high VIF is narrow: this particular coefficient is now unanswerable with this data.

Assessment moment for LO 13.2. If C got votes, that is the deletion reflex and the next slide addresses it head on.

Fatal for One Job, Harmless for the Other

Multicollinearity is not a data-quality problem or a modelling error. It is a statement about which questions your data can answer. Correlated predictors leave little independent variation, so individual coefficients destabilise and their standard errors inflate. Aggregate fit is untouched, because the model still knows the combined relationship — it simply cannot attribute it.

Want to PREDICT wages?

The model is fine.

Want to say what a year of tenure is worth?

Now useless.

The same fitted object is simultaneously both. This is Chapter 12's explain-versus-predict distinction cashing out in one table.

“A high VIF means drop the variable.” Only if you need that variable's coefficient. Predicting? Drop nothing — regularization (Ch 16) handles redundancy far more gracefully than deletion, and dropping a correlated predictor throws away real information. A high VIF on a variable you do not care about is not your concern.

1.5 min. Forward seed to Ch 16: ridge shrinks correlated predictors TOGETHER rather than forcing the model to choose between them, which is why the VIF table is a diagnostic and not a verdict. Then transition to the last section: "all of that was mechanics. Here is the procedure."

Choosing What Goes In

Section 7  •  LO 13.4  •  the part you will actually use  •  6 min

Start From the Question, Not the Data

Write down, in one sentence, what you are trying to estimate. Then decide which of two kinds of question it is.

A prediction question

“What will this house sell for?”

Easy: include whatever improves out-of-sample error and evaluate on a test set. Confounders, mediators and colliders are all fair game — you are not claiming any coefficient means anything.

A causal question

“Does this programme raise wages?”

Now every variable needs a justification — and the justification is structural, not statistical. Nothing in the output will tell you whether you got it right.

The single most common error in applied work: running the second kind of analysis with the first kind of variable selection — picking controls by what improves fit, then interpreting a coefficient causally.

1.5 min. The misconception box is the sentence to repeat twice. Students will do exactly this in the capstone unless they hear it named.

Classify Every Candidate Variable

PatternWhat it isInclude?
Causes both treatment and outcomeConfounderYes
Caused by treatment, causes outcomeMediatorNo — unless you want the direct effect, and say so
Caused by both treatment and outcomeColliderNo — never
Causes the outcome onlyPrecision covariateOptional — tightens SEs, no bias either way
Causes the treatment onlyInstrument-likeNo — inflates variance without reducing bias

Row 4 is the one people forget. A variable that affects only the outcome is safe and helpful. Not every control is a risk — after forty minutes of being made afraid of controls, leave calibrated rather than paralysed.

The Procedure, for a Causal Question

1

Draw the graph before you open the data. Drawing it after seeing results is how motivated reasoning gets in.

2

Classify every candidate with the table you just saw.

3

Apply the timing screen. Anything measured after treatment is a mediator or a collider. Necessary, not sufficient (Ch 10's M-bias) — but it catches most real errors cheaply.

4

Decide before you look at the coefficient. An analyst who adds and removes controls while watching a coefficient move is searching — and the resulting standard errors do not account for the search.

5

Report the alternatives anyway. The BWWC standard. A table of specifications with each estimand stated in words beats one number — and removes the suspicion that you chose the flattering one.

1.5 min. Step 4 will get pushback — the honest response is that everyone is tempted, which is exactly why you write the justification down first. It costs nothing and it is the difference between an analysis and an argument for a foregone conclusion.

When You Cannot Measure the Confounder

Ability in a wage regression. Management quality in a firm regression. Three honest responses, in ascending order of effort:

Sign the bias

Use Section 2's formula. Argue the omitted variable raises both, and your estimate becomes an upper bound. A bounded claim is a real contribution.

Bound the magnitude

Chapter 10's sensitivity analysis: how strong would the unmeasured confounder have to be to overturn the result? Converts an unfalsifiable objection into a number your reader can judge.

Change the design

Find a natural experiment — a policy that changed at a point in time, an arbitrary threshold, variation unrelated to the outcome.

Better identification beats better controls, every time.

1 min. That last line is why the graduate track spends three whole chapters on design. Say it and move — the discussion is next and it needs its four minutes.

Discussion 3: Classify Four Controls

Causal question: does a job-training programme raise participants' wages one year later?

Classify each candidate control — and say what happens if you get it wrong.

1. Years of prior education (measured before)

2. Found a job through the programme (after)

3. Local unemployment rate in the participant's county

4. Whether the participant was later promoted (after)

3 min in groups of 3 → 1 min, one classification called out per group

4 min. Assesses LO 13.4. Answers: 1 = confounder, include. 2 = mediator; excluding it gives the total effect, almost certainly what you want. 3 = precision covariate (moves wages, not who enrols) — optional and helpful, and it is row 4 of the table. 4 = mediator, and arguably a collider if promotion also depends on wages — leave it out. Make groups say the CONSEQUENCE of getting each wrong, not just the label. Press 3 for the timer.

Key Takeaways

A coefficient is a partial effect. It has no meaning independent of the model it sits in. Same variable, different specification, different number — and none is correct in the abstract.

OVB = \(\beta_2\delta\), exactly. Two signs multiply, so you can predict the direction of your error before you have the data — and you should write that prediction down first.

Controlling for a mediator changes the question. Today it removed $7,386 of a $10,200 gap — and every dollar removed was real discrimination travelling through promotion.

Multicollinearity is fatal for explanation and harmless for prediction. The same fitted model, the same output, is broken for one purpose and fine for the other.

Every control you add is a claim about how the world works. The software will never ask you to defend it. Your reader should.
