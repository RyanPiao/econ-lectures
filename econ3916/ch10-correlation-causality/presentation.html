A Finding That Was Never There

A Decade of Published Research

A line of strategy and management research reported that when a company appoints a woman as CEO, the other women executives at that company appear to do worse — lower compensation, lower representation in the ranks below.

Published. Cited. Circulated as evidence of something people were quick to name.

Three Reasons Two Things Move Together

Section 10.1 • and why no test tells them apart

Your Scatterplot Slopes Upward. Why?

1. Causation

\(X\) causes \(Y\). The association is the causal effect. The one you hope for.

2. Confounding

Some third thing causes both. More police, more crime — city size causes both. Real, reproducible, and useless.

3. Selection

Nothing causes anything. The way the data came to be in front of you created the pattern. This is the hook.

They are indistinguishable in the data. There is no statistical test that separates them. A correlation of 0.7 looks identical whichever produced it.

Any claim to have distinguished them is a claim about structure — and structure is not something you compute from a spreadsheet. It is something you assert, and then defend.

4 min. Get the class to supply their own example of each of the three before you reveal the fragment; that is 90 seconds well spent and makes the "indistinguishable" claim land as a loss rather than a definition. Almost every mistake in applied economics is a failure to distinguish these three.

Chapter 3 Said "Look at Your Data." It Is Not Enough.

Anscombe's Quartet: four datasets, identical means, variances and correlations, completely different when plotted. The lesson was that summary statistics hide shape.

Here is the harder version. Plotting does not save you either — because the variable that distinguishes the three worlds (\(U\), or the selection rule) is not on the axes.

Common misconception — "with enough data, the truth will emerge."
More data makes a confounded estimate more precise, not more correct. Going from \(n = 5{,}000\) to \(n = 5{,}000{,}000\) shrinks the interval around a number that is wrong.

This is Chapter 6's data defect identity in causal clothing: sample size cures noise, never bias.

3 min. This is the moment visualization stops being sufficient and structure becomes necessary — say that sentence out loud. Anticipate "couldn't you just test whether U exists?" The honest answer: you can if you measured it, which is what the rest of the chapter is about, and you cannot if you did not. Let the discomfort sit for a minute; do not introduce graphs yet.

What Chapter 9 Gave You — and Where It Stopped

$$\underbrace{E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{what you compute}} = \underbrace{\tau}_{\text{what you want}} + \underbrace{E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{selection bias}}$$

Interpretation. The selection-bias term asks whether the treated and untreated groups would have differed even with no treatment at all. Randomization forces it to zero by construction. Without randomization it is an unknown number of unknown sign — and all of observational causal inference is the project of arguing you have made it small.

The limitation. It tells you the bias exists. It gives you no purchase on what to do about it — which variables, held fixed, would shrink that term. And it certainly does not warn you that some variables, held fixed, will grow it.

2 min. Do NOT re-derive this — Ch 9 owns it and re-deriving costs four minutes you need later. Read the interpretation line aloud, then spend your time on the limitation box, which is the actual bridge into DAGs. "For that, we need to talk about structure."

Drawing the Structure

Section 10.2 • nodes, arrows, paths, and the backdoor criterion

Two Ingredients, One Very Strong Convention

Nodes are variables. An arrow from \(X\) to \(Y\) means "\(X\) is a direct cause of \(Y\)." That is all.

Directed: causes point at effects. Acyclic: you cannot follow arrows in a loop back to where you started.

The most important thing about a DAG is what a missing arrow means.

Drawing an arrow \(X \rightarrow Y\) says "\(X\) might affect \(Y\)" — a weak claim.

Leaving it out says "\(X\) has no direct effect on \(Y\) at all" — a strong one.

A DAG's assumptions live in its absences. When you critique someone's DAG, you are almost always arguing about an arrow they left out.

3 min. Acyclicity rules out simultaneity, which is a genuine limitation and a real subject in economics — flag it in one sentence, promise the last section, move on. The absences point is the one to slow down for: it reframes every seminar question they will ever ask about someone's identification strategy.

Everything Is Built From Three Configurations

Conditioning on a variable — controlling for it, stratifying on it, filtering to one of its values — closes chains and forks. It opens colliders.

5 min. Draw all three on the board by hand BEFORE showing this slide, and make the class supply a concrete example of each. Spend disproportionate time on the collider: ask students to predict what conditioning does to it before you tell them — the wrong answer is nearly universal and the surprise is what makes it stick. Learn these three and the rest is bookkeeping.

Paths, Backdoors, and the Pipe Metaphor

A path is any sequence of edges connecting \(D\) and \(Y\), ignoring arrow direction. It is causal if every arrow points away from \(D\).

Every other path is a backdoor path — so named because its first arrow points into \(D\), through the back door.

A path is blocked by a set \(\mathbf{S}\) if either:

1. it contains a chain or a fork through \(W\), and \(W \in \mathbf{S}\); or

2. it contains a collider \(C\), and neither \(C\) nor any descendant of \(C\) is in \(\mathbf{S}\).

Interpretation — a path is a pipe. Chains and forks are open by default and conditioning plugs them. Colliders are plugged by default and conditioning unplugs them. Block every path and the two variables are d-separated: the graph implies they are independent given \(\mathbf{S}\).

The descendant clause is where careful people still get caught: controlling for a proxy of a collider is enough to do the damage.

4 min. The pipe metaphor is the sentence to repeat verbatim three times today. Do not derive d-separation formally — 3916 needs the operational rule, not the proof. The descendant clause is worth 30 seconds because it is what catches people who think they have avoided the collider by controlling for something "related to" it.

Key Concept: The Backdoor Criterion

A set \(\mathbf{S}\) suffices to identify the causal effect of \(D\) on \(Y\) if:

1. \(\mathbf{S}\) blocks every backdoor path from \(D\) to \(Y\)

the familiar one — close the confounding channels

2. \(\mathbf{S}\) contains no descendant of \(D\)

the one people forget

Condition 2 in plain English: never control for anything that happened after the treatment. Anything downstream of \(D\) is either a mediator (blocking the effect you want) or a collider (creating bias you did not have).

Common misconception — "the DAG is something you discover from the data." Structure-learning algorithms exist, but several widely used methods effectively sort variables by marginal variance rather than causal direction — so rescaling a variable can reverse an arrow. A DAG is a hypothesis you bring from theory and institutional knowledge. Data can refute a graph. It cannot hand you one.

3 min. Satisfy both conditions and adjusting for S recovers the causal effect — that is the whole payoff of the section. The misconception box is worth 45 seconds; students who have taken a CS course will have heard of causal discovery and should hear the caveat from you rather than from a referee.

Poll: Should You Control For It?

A job-training program raises participants' skills, and skills raise earnings. You want the total effect of the program on earnings.

Should you control for skills?

A) Yes — it strongly predicts earnings
B) Yes — it improves model fit
C) No — it is a mediator
D) No — it is a collider
1 min. Click to vote. Watch the C/D split — it tells you whether the room is reading arrowheads or just reading position in the diagram. That diagnostic is more useful to you than the raw score.

Answer: C — Skills Is a Mediator

Skills sits on the chain \(D \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y\). Conditioning on a chain closes it, so you would measure the effect of training holding skills fixed — close to zero by construction, and not what anyone asked for. It also violates backdoor condition 2: skills is a descendant of \(D\).

Why D is wrong — and worth naming. A collider has two arrows pointing in. Skills has one in (\(D \rightarrow M\)) and one out (\(M \rightarrow Y\)). Direction is the whole distinction.

A and B are the dangerous answers. Both are prediction criteria. This is an identification problem. Ch 12 will show you how thoroughly those two can disagree.

2 min. If D polls high, spend the extra minute drawing both structures side by side on the board with the arrowheads exaggerated. Reading arrowheads rather than positions is the single skill this section has to install.

Good Controls

Section 10.3 • making "just control for it" precise

What "Controlling For It" Actually Does

A confounder is a common cause of treatment and outcome. It sits at the top of a fork. Controlling for it blocks the backdoor path and moves your estimate toward the truth.

The mechanics, plainly. You compare units that have the same value of it. Compare a treated and an untreated person with the same 1975 earnings, the same education, the same age — and whatever bias those three introduced is gone from that comparison. Do it for every combination of values, average the results, and you have adjusted for them.

That is all a multiple regression is doing — Ch 13 gives you the algebra. What the graph adds, and what no amount of algebra supplies, is the answer to which variables belong in that list.

2 min. Keep this concrete and slow. Many students have run regressions for two years without ever hearing "controlling for" defined as a comparison operation rather than a software argument. Seed Ch 13 explicitly: identification first, estimation second.

The Adjustment Formula

$$E[Y_i(d)] = \sum_{\mathbf{s}} E[Y_i \mid D_i = d, \mathbf{S}_i = \mathbf{s}] \cdot P(\mathbf{S}_i = \mathbf{s})$$

Interpretation. Compute the average outcome separately within each stratum of the controls, then average across strata, weighted by how common each stratum is. This is what turns a graph into a number: everything on the right is estimable from observed data, the left-hand side is a counterfactual, and the backdoor criterion is precisely the condition that licenses the equality.

Overlap (positivity). Every stratum needs both treated and untreated units. If nobody with a PhD ever entered the program, no arithmetic recovers what would have happened if one had.

Dimensionality. 20 binary controls → over a million strata, and your data will not populate them. Regression imposes functional form; Ch 24 uses ML. Same problem, two answers.

3 min. Do not dwell on the summation notation — read the interpretation line and move. Practitioners check that estimated treatment probabilities stay away from 0 and 1; that is the overlap condition in the wild, and it is what the propensity-score trimming in the Netflix slide is doing.

Think Like an Economist: The Three-Question Test

Before adding a variable to a regression, ask three questions in this order:

1

Does it cause the treatment?

2

Does it cause the outcome?

3

Did it happen before the treatment?

All three yes → confounder, it belongs. Third answer no → stop. Notice what is not on the list: "does it improve model fit." Fit is a prediction criterion. This is an identification problem.

2 min. This is the single most portable thing in the chapter. Say out loud: "photograph this slide." Then stop talking for five seconds so they actually do. You will invoke it by name twice more today — in Discussion 2 and in the takeaways.

Bad Controls

Section 10.4 • adding a control can create bias that was not there

One Line of Difference

GOOD CONTROL — a fork

u = rng.normal(0,1,n)
d = 1.4*u + eps
y = 1.4*u + eps
OLS(y, [d, u])  ->  0.014

BAD CONTROL — a collider

d = rng.normal(0,1,n)
y = rng.normal(0,1,n)
c = 1.2*d + 1.2*y + eps
OLS(y, [d, c])  -> -0.593

Same estimator. Same syntax. Same number of controls. Opposite outcome. The only thing that changed is which way the arrows point — and that is not in the data.

3 min. THIS IS THE SLIDE THEY PHOTOGRAPH. Do not narrate both halves — they have seen the left one. Put your finger on the two code blocks and say "find the difference." Wait. Someone will say "u versus c" and you say: no, look at what causes what. There is no diagnostic in your regression output that flags the right-hand column. Residuals look fine. Reported fit improved. Every test you have learned will pass.

Why It Happens: The Restaurant

A restaurant is worth visiting if the food is good or the location is convenient. One or the other suffices.

In the full population of restaurants, food quality and location have nothing to do with each other.

Among restaurants people actually visit, they are negatively correlated. Went somewhere inconvenient? The food must have been excellent. Food mediocre? It must have been very close by.

Now notice what "filter to the ones people visit" means. Your dataset is the restaurants people visited.

Selection into the sample is conditioning on a collider.

3 min. Lead with this rather than the medical analogies — it works far better for undergraduates and every one of them has the lived experience. Get them to generate the negative correlation themselves before you assert it. The fragment is the retroactive payoff: Ch 6's selection bias and today's collider bias are the same phenomenon wearing different clothes.

That trade-off is the manufactured correlation. Nothing about the world changed — only which units are being compared.

Key Concept: Conditioning Opens Colliders

Because sample selection is a form of conditioning, any filter applied to your data — restricting to ICU patients, to executives who appear in the data, to customers who completed onboarding, to restaurants people visited — is a conditioning operation. If the filtering variable is a collider, it induces associations that do not exist in the population.

"Bias from a control variable" and "bias from sample selection" are the same mathematical event.

Ch 6 callback. Survivorship bias, non-response bias, and Berkson's paradox are one structure with three names.

Ch 8 callback. The collider regression returned \(p < 0.001\) on an effect of exactly zero. A \(p\)-value is silent about whether the estimand is the one you wanted.

And back to the hook. Executive presence in the observed data is caused both by the CEO's gender and by the career outcomes being measured. Conditioning on it — which is what analysing the observed executives does — opened a path and manufactured a negative relationship. The Frake replication needed no new data. It needed a better graph.

3 min. The word you asked them to carry thirty minutes ago now cashes out — make that explicit: "you have been holding the word collider since slide six." The Ch 6 callback retroactively explains a lot and is worth naming as such.

Three Ways a Control Variable Hurts You

FailureStructureWhat it doesDirection
Collider \(D \rightarrow C \leftarrow Y\) Opens a non-causal path. Bias appears where there was none. Unpredictable
Mediator \(D \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y\) Blocks part of the causal path you are trying to measure. Shrinks to zero
M-bias \(U_1 \rightarrow W \leftarrow U_2\) Opens a backdoor via a pre-treatment collider. Unpredictable

The mediator case is the formal statement of the "don't control for post-treatment variables" rule, and it is why the backdoor criterion's second condition exists. You have measured the direct effect of \(D\) while holding fixed the mechanism through which it operates — which is usually not the quantity anyone wanted.

2 min. Name them separately because they fail differently — students who lump all "bad controls" together will misdiagnose in the wild. The mediator row is the one they will actually meet most often in a co-op; the collider row is the one that ends careers.

M-Bias: The Rule You Actually Use Is Wrong

\(W\) is pre-treatment. Not caused by \(D\). Not caused by \(Y\). Measured before anything happens. And controlling for it is still wrong.

Misconception killed: "it's safe to control for anything measured before the treatment." Timing is a useful screen for mediators. It is not sufficient, and no rule based on timing alone can be.

They Did Not Stop at the Graph. They Tested It.

The testable implication. If ICU selection generated a spurious protective effect, the same artifact should appear for other conditions that lower the admission threshold.

They looked. It did not. Other diagnoses showed no such protective association, before or after adjustment. Re-estimated with propensity score overlap weighting, the association survived.

The honest summary is not "the collider explained it away." It is that a plausible collider was proposed, formalised as a graph, converted into a testable implication, and rejected on evidence.

That is what a DAG is actually for. It does not settle arguments by itself — it turns a vague worry ("isn't this just selection?") into a specific claim that data can bear on.

The Answerable Question

The immature response. "There is no unobserved confounding." Untestable, and everyone in the room knows it.

The mature response. "How strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to overturn this result?"

$$\text{E-value} = RR + \sqrt{RR \times (RR - 1)}$$

Interpretation. An E-value of 2.36 says that to explain away your result there would have to exist an unmeasured variable that raises the probability of treatment by a factor of at least 2.36 and raises the probability of the outcome by a factor of at least 2.36. Weaker confounders cannot do it.

Whether such a variable plausibly exists is a question for domain expertise — which is the point. The statistic hands the argument back to the economist rather than pretending to settle it.

3 min. Emphasise the conversion: an unfalsifiable assertion becomes a quantity, and quantities can be argued about by people with subject-matter knowledge. "A confounder twice as strong as the strongest thing we measured" is something a labour economist can evaluate. "There is no unobserved confounding" is not. \(RR\) here is the observed risk ratio; protective effects are inverted so the metric is symmetric.

Poll: Which Is Easier to Explain Away?

Two observational studies, adjusting for the same covariates. Study A reports \(RR = 1.2\). Study B reports \(RR = 3.0\).

Which is easier for an unmeasured confounder to explain away — and roughly how much easier?

A) Study A — needs strength 1.69 vs 5.45
B) Study B — larger effects have more room to be wrong
C) Equally — the E-value depends only on sample size
D) Neither — the E-value is a \(p\)-value in different units
1 min. Click to vote. B is the intuitive-sounding trap; C and D are the two ways students assimilate a new statistic to an old one.

Answer: A — 1.69 vs 5.45

A confounder that triples both the treatment probability and the outcome probability is a large, unusual thing that someone would likely have noticed. One that multiplies both by 1.7 is routine.

Why C deserves killing explicitly. The E-value is a function of the point estimate only — no \(n\), no standard error, no \(p\)-value anywhere in the formula. It answers a different question from every statistic you have met: not "could this be noise?" but "could this be structure I failed to measure?"

2 min. Make the C rebuttal the memorable part. Students have spent nine chapters learning statistics that are all about sampling variability; this is the first one that is not, and saying so out loud reorganises the whole term for them.

Three Things a DAG Will Not Do For You

1. It will not find the arrows you forgot. The graph encodes your beliefs; wrong beliefs give a confidently wrong adjustment set. Less a defect than a relocation — the assumptions were always there, and the graph makes them visible and arguable instead of buried in a specification.

2. It does not handle simultaneity. Acyclicity rules out feedback loops. Supply and demand determine price and quantity simultaneously — a cycle, and one of the oldest problems in econometrics.

3. It does not give you an estimate. The graph says what to adjust for. It says nothing about how — regression, matching, weighting — or about standard errors, functional form, or overlap. Identification and estimation are separate problems, and the graph solves only the first.

And one more misconception. "A robustness check where the estimate doesn't move proves there's no confounding." It tells you about the variables you measured. It is silent about the ones you did not — and under some structures a stable estimate is exactly what an omitted confounder produces.

3 min. This section is where the lecture earns its credibility, so resist compressing it when running late. Students who have just been shown a powerful tool need to hear its limits from you rather than discovering them in a seminar in three years. Point 2 is one reason economists were slow to adopt graphs — the potential outcomes tradition grew up around exactly these problems, and ECON 5200's Ch 12 on instrumental variables is largely about this case.

Key Takeaways

Three explanations, no test. Causation, confounding, selection — the distinguishing information is not in the data.

A DAG's strongest claims are its absences. The arrows it leaves out are the assumptions worth arguing about.

The backdoor criterion is mechanical. Block every path with an arrow into \(D\); never condition on anything downstream of it.

Conditioning opens colliders. A control can manufacture significance between independent variables — and no diagnostic flags it.

You can never prove there is no unmeasured confounder — so quantify how strong one would have to be, and let domain knowledge judge whether it plausibly exists.

The three-question test: causes the treatment? causes the outcome? happened before it? — "improves fit" is not on the list.
