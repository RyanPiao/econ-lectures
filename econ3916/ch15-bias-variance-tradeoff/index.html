The Same Model, Two Verdicts

A city housing authority regresses rent on square footage, bedrooms, neighbourhood, building age and distance to transit — then asks two questions of it.

"How much does an extra bedroom add to rent?"

Chapter 13's question. The answer is a coefficient. To believe it causally you need the adjustment set right (Ch 10) and the standard error honest (Ch 14). Whether it predicts well is almost beside the point.

"What should this vacant unit rent for?"

Now the coefficients are irrelevant. If a Random Forest — which has no coefficients — predicts better, that is strictly better. Nobody asks a Random Forest what a bedroom is worth.

Neighbourhood fixed effects sharpen the bedroom coefficient and may destroy prediction — hundreds of parameters on few observations each. You cannot optimise both. Decide which question you are answering before you fit anything.

3 min. The worked example matters more than the abstraction. Ask which of the two questions their midterm project is asking — most will not have decided, and that is the point of the slide. Mixing the two is the most common failure in applied work that touches both.

Common Misconception

"More variables make a model better."

This is the econometrician's instinct, and it is correct for the job econometrics does. For prediction it is wrong more often than right.

Each added variable buys flexibility, and flexibility is paid for in variance. Once the model can already represent the signal, further variables fit noise — improving in-sample \(R^2\), which cannot decrease, while degrading out-of-sample accuracy.

Chapter 12 showed twenty columns of pure noise raising \(R^2\). Today you see what that cost.

2 min. The sentence to land: nothing about the mathematics changed, the objective did. Same action — adding a control — virtuous in Ch 13, destructive here. The Decomposition

Section 15.2 • Three errors, only two of them negotiable

Bias and Variance Are Shapes Before They Are Terms

300 training sets drawn from the same world. Same model family. Only the sample changes.

Degree 1 — all 300 lines lie on top of each other, and all 300 are wrong the same way. Tight cluster, wrong place. Averaging them would not help: their average is also a straight line.

Degree 15 — a thicket. Each curve chases the noise of its own sample. Their average tracks the truth, but no individual curve does, and you only ever get one.

Degree 5 — tight and near the truth. That is what a minimum looks like.

3 min, and show it BEFORE the equation. This is Chapter 4's dartboard with a different label: bias is the systematic offset from the bullseye, variance is the spread of the darts. The reason to carry the analogy is that it makes the tradeoff a shape you can diagnose from a plot rather than an inequality you have to remember. The chapter describes this fan in words and never draws it — so this picture is the one thing on the slides that the reading does not give them.

Expected Prediction Error, Decomposed

Truth: \(y = f(x) + \varepsilon\), with \(E[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\). Fit \(\hat f\) on a training sample; ask about squared error at a new point \(x_0\), averaging over all the training sets you might have drawn.

$$\text{EPE}(x_0) = \underbrace{\big(E[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{E\big[(\hat f(x_0) - E[\hat f(x_0)])^2\big]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible}}$$

\(\hat f(x_0)\) — the prediction from a model fitted on one training sample

\(E[\hat f(x_0)]\) — the average prediction across all possible samples

\(f(x_0)\) — the truth

\(\sigma^2\) — the variance of the noise in \(y\)

Scope, said out loud: this is a statement about squared error, and nothing else. It is not a general law of learning. Classification — Chapters 17–18 — gets no clean analogue, which is why those chapters need different metrics.

3 min. Read the expectation out loud: we are averaging over training sets, not over test points. That is the move students miss. Say the scope restriction explicitly — every year someone writes "by the bias-variance decomposition" about a classifier's accuracy on a problem set, and it does not decompose that way.

Three Distinct Failures, in Plain Words

Bias — being systematically wrong.

Your model is too rigid to represent the truth. It would still miss with infinite data. The remedy is more flexibility.

Variance — being unstable.

Your model swings depending on which particular sample it happened to see. And you only ever get one sample. The remedy is less flexibility.

Irreducible error — the noise in \(y\) itself.

No model reduces it. Ever. It is not a modelling failure at all — it is a property of the world you are measuring.

You trade the first two against each other by choosing complexity. You cannot touch the third.

2 min. Bias and variance have opposite remedies — that is the single most useful sentence on this slide, and it is why "the model is underperforming" and "the model is overfitting" are different diagnoses. Come back to it on the U-curve slides.

"It Sums to Four Decimals" — What That Does and Does Not Prove

At every degree, bias\(^2\) + variance + \(\sigma^2\) equals the measured error to four decimal places. That is worth pausing on, for the right reason.

What it proves. The three boxes are exhaustive and non-overlapping. There is no fourth source of squared error hiding anywhere. This is an identity, not an approximation.

What it does not prove. That any particular number is accurate. Both sides are computed from the same array of predictions, so their agreement is forced by algebra — the executed residual is 7×10\(^{-18}\), and it would be that small on random junk.

The evidence that the numbers are right is the figure and the standard errors, not the sum-check. This distinction will matter again in twenty minutes, when cross-validation lands suspiciously close to the truth.

2 min. This is a small integrity lesson and undergraduates take it well if you keep it short. An identity is a guarantee about structure; a measurement is a claim about the world. The chapter says "it is an identity" outright — the deck just refuses the temptation to read it as confirmation. Flag forward to the CV coincidence so it does not arrive from nowhere.

The Gap Is the Diagnostic

Degree 1: 1.09 vs 1.48. Both bad, almost no gap. A rigid model that is honestly bad.

Degree 15: 0.25 vs 0.45. Excellent on train, poor on test. A model that has memorised.

A widening gap between training and validation error is the signature of overfitting — and it is more informative than either number alone.

2 min. The gap is what students will actually look at in their labs, so make them say it back. Connect explicitly to Ch 12's noise-columns demonstration: that showed R-squared rising on garbage, this shows what the garbage costs.

Three Consequences Students Under-Absorb

1. A model can be bad without overfitting.

Degree 1 is genuinely poor with almost no gap. Its problem is bias, and the remedy is more flexibility, not less. "Underperforming" and "overfitting" are different diagnoses with opposite treatments.

2. The best model is rarely the most flexible one you can afford.

Degree 15 fits the training data best of anything in the table and is the second-worst choice on new data. Any procedure that rewards training fit reliably selects the worst model — which disqualifies in-sample \(R^2\) from this job entirely.

3. Degrees 5 and 7 are indistinguishable — and the menu is an artifact.

0.3648 vs 0.3650 on 48 observations is not a ranking. Worse: sweep every degree and this split's minimum is degree 6, at 0.3639 — a degree the menu never offered. Cross-validation can only compare what you put on the menu.

3 min. Consequence 3 is the bridge to Section 4 and to Chapter 16: a discrete search over seven degrees is a crude instrument, and regularization replaces it with a continuous dial. When two models are this close, prefer the simpler one — not from aesthetics, but because it has less variance and the comparison lacks the resolution to justify anything else.

Common Misconception

"A model that fits the training data perfectly is a good model."

A model with enough parameters can fit any training set exactly — including one that is pure noise. Perfect training fit is evidence of flexibility, not of accuracy, and past a point it is evidence of a problem.

The only score that means anything is on data that played no part in fitting.

This is the same sentence as the retrieval question you answered at the start of class. \(R^2\) could not fall when Chapter 12 added twenty noise columns, and a degree-15 polynomial fits 72 points better than a degree-5 one, for exactly the same reason — and in both cases the number that went up told you nothing about accuracy.

1 min. Keep it short; it is a restatement they need in words rather than a new idea. If someone raises "but sometimes very flexible models do fine" — that is double descent, it is real, it is the lab's optional take-home challenge, and it is not what governs a 120-point economics dataset.

\(k\)-Fold Cross-Validation

$$CV(k) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\text{MSE}_j$$

Where: \(\text{MSE}_j\) is the error on fold \(j\), from a model fitted on the other \(k-1\) folds.

In plain words. Cut the data into \(k\) blocks. Hold each block out in turn, train on the rest, score on the held-out block, and average the \(k\) scores.

Every observation is used for validation exactly once and for training \(k-1\) times, so the estimate does not hinge on one lucky partition. Averaging \(k\) noisy draws is what makes model selection reliable rather than lucky.

Use \(k = 5\) or \(k = 10\). Both work; larger \(k\) costs compute without buying much.

2 min. Never present the formula bare — the plain-words box is the version they will remember. If a student asks why not leave-one-out: it is the graduate answer, involving the correlation between the fitted models, and the practical answer is that 5 or 10 both work fine. That correlation argument returns in Ch 19 as the reason Random Forests decorrelate their trees.

Two Things This Table Cannot Tell You

1. That CV is unbiased. Its estimate at the minimum, 0.3222, sits 0.0003 from the true expected error the decomposition measured (0.3219). Resist the obvious reading. That estimate carries a standard error of 0.0426 across its own ten folds — 142 times the size of the agreement. Landing within 0.0003 is a coincidence of this partition. CV is close to unbiased for expected prediction error — but this table is not the evidence, because a single run cannot resolve anything smaller than about 0.04.

2. That degree 5 beats degree 7. Degrees 5, 7 and 10 come in at 0.3222, 0.3328 and 0.3406 — a spread of 0.0184, less than half of one standard error. CV has not found a winner. It has found that all three are indistinguishable and picked the smallest number anyway.

The one-standard-error rule. Among models within one SE of the best, take the simplest — because the ordering inside that band is noise, and the simpler model has less variance.

3 min, and this is the most important slide in Section 4. Two habits to send them away with: always print the SE beside a CV mean, and never rank models whose SEs overlap. The one-standard-error rule is not a convention or a preference — it is what you do when your instrument has finite resolution. They will use it on their own lab output in fifteen minutes.

Why There Is a Third Split

Once you use validation scores to choose something — a degree, a hyperparameter, an algorithm — that score stops being an unbiased estimate of future performance, because you selected on it. Chapter 8's multiple-comparisons problem, arriving in a new costume.

Validation

If you check the test set and then change something, it is a validation set now, and you no longer have an honest estimate. The professional move is to say so.

How Much Is Model Selection Actually Worth?

The floor was 0.3025 and the best model reached 0.3219. The entire range between the best and worst reasonable models — degrees 3 through 10, spanning 0.3648 to 0.4942 on the test split — is small relative to the noise you can never remove.

Squeezing out the last few percent of achievable error is a much smaller prize than it looks when you report improvements as percentages of the total.

This is why experienced practitioners spend their time on data quality, feature construction and problem framing rather than algorithm selection. Those can lower \(\sigma^2\) itself — a better-measured target, a feature capturing something previously unobserved — which is the only way to move the floor.

Swapping one model for another slides you along a curve whose minimum is already fixed. Chapters 16 to 19 buy you a closer approach to the floor. They do not lower it.

2 min — and this is the marked cut if the room is running long, since the takeaways slide restates it. Otherwise it is worth the time: it is the most transferable career advice in the chapter, and it inoculates them against the "which algorithm should I use" question that dominates their final projects.

Leakage

Section 15.5 • When the held-out data was not really held out

Leakage, and the First of Three Kinds

Data leakage is any path by which information from outside the training set reaches the model during fitting. It inflates validation scores, sometimes spectacularly, and the inflation is invisible until deployment — at which point the model is much worse than advertised and nobody can say why.

Preprocessing leakage. Scale or impute before splitting, and the training data has been transformed using the test set's mean and variance. The model has seen a summary of data it should not know exists.

The effect is usually small with simple scaling on a large sample — which is precisely why it survives review. It becomes serious with feature selection, imputation or target encoding: choosing the twenty best predictors by correlation with \(y\) on the full dataset lets the test set vote on which features the model gets. In the extreme, selecting features on pure noise before splitting produces a model that validates well and predicts nothing.

2 min. The "small effects survive review" point is the one worth landing — a bug that produces a 40% inflation gets caught; a bug that produces a 2% inflation gets published. Order note: I lead with preprocessing because it is the one their code will commit today, but grouped leakage is the one their panel data will inflict on them, and it comes last for emphasis.

The Fix Is Structural, Not Disciplinary

WRONG — the scaler has already seen the test set

X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X_scaled, y)

RIGHT — the scaler is refitted inside every fold

pipe = Pipeline([('scaler', StandardScaler()), ('model', Ridge())])
scores = cross_val_score(pipe, X_train, y_train, cv=5)

Put every transformation inside a Pipeline and scikit-learn guarantees that .fit() touches only training folds. Manual scaling followed by cross-validation is a bug that produces optimistic numbers and no error message.

2 min. Say the last sentence twice. "No error message" is why this is a structural fix rather than a matter of care: careful people write this bug, and nothing tells them. Their PS4 Part B will be graded on whether the transformations are inside a Pipeline.

Answer: B — Shuffling Leaked the Future

Shuffling ordered data is temporal leakage, and the replication says the flexible model gains roughly four times as much from it. The honest comparison uses TimeSeriesSplit — and under it, in our example, OLS wins.

A is not impossible — but it is the claim that has to be earned on a time-respecting split, and usually it is not. C and D are real issues that would not produce this pattern.

Leakage does not merely inflate scores. It inflates them differentially, manufacturing an apparent advantage for exactly the models that would otherwise be penalised for their variance. Any comparison run on leaky splits is systematically biased toward complexity.

2 min. The key-result box is the mechanism and it is the thing to carry into the Kapoor & Narayanan slide: a flexible model has the capacity to exploit leaked information and a rigid one does not, so leaky evaluation is not neutral noise — it has a direction, and the direction favours complexity.

Grouped Leakage — The Subtlest, and Yours

If a dataset contains repeated observations of the same unit — several trips by one driver, several quarters for one firm, several students in one school — a random split puts the same unit on both sides. The model learns to recognise that unit rather than the general pattern.

Economist at Work — Cambridge Mobile Telematics

CMT processes sensor data from over 55 million drivers to assess driving risk; insurers price policies on the output. The obvious validation is to pool every trip and split randomly. It is wrong. One driver contributes hundreds of trips over the same commute, the same roads, the same car. Split randomly and that driver is on both sides — so the model does not have to learn the physics of risky driving. It can learn this person's commute.

In Chapter 14's language: repeated measures from one driver are a panel, and pooling them violates the independence that standard CV assumes. Clustering did not disappear because the model became non-parametric.

The fix is GroupKFold — every trip from a driver confined to one fold, so performance is measured on drivers the model has never seen. Which is the only question an insurer entering a new market is asking.

3 min. Lead the leakage block's emphasis here rather than on preprocessing — this is the one they have never considered and the one their own panel data will inflict on them. Generalise at the end: if your rows are not independent, your random split is not measuring what you think, and the fix is to split along the dimension you want to generalise across.

Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) found leakage pervasive. In area after area — civil war prediction, health forecasting — complex models had reportedly crushed traditional logistic regression. When the data was repartitioned to respect chronology, so the future could not inform the past, the advantage largely vanished: decades-old linear models performed comparably to the neural networks that had supposedly superseded them.

It is not that the sophisticated models were bad. It is that the evaluation was wrong, in a way that systematically favoured flexibility — because a more flexible model exploits leaked information more efficiently.

2 min. "329 papers across 17 fields" reframes leakage from a coding slip to a discipline-wide problem, and that reframing is the reason this slide exists. Let it sit uncomfortably for a beat before moving on.

The Simplicity Finding

On noisy, tabular, moderately sized economic data, simple models often win. Not always, and not by much — but often enough that flexibility should be argued for rather than assumed.

The decomposition explains exactly why. Economic data tends to have a large irreducible component (human behaviour is genuinely partly unpredictable), a modest number of informative features, and sample sizes in the thousands rather than the millions. That combination puts the minimum of the U-curve at low complexity: a rigid form accepts some bias and buys a large reduction in variance, and when \(\sigma^2\) is big the variance saving dominates.

The environments where flexible models decisively win look different — enormous samples, low noise, rich structure a linear form genuinely cannot represent: images, language, board positions. Those conditions are real. They are not the conditions of most economic panels.

Always fit the simple model first, and make the complex one earn its place on held-out data.

2 min. This is a discipline rather than a prejudice, and worth saying that way. A penalised linear regression is not a fallback for people who cannot run gradient boosting; on a great many economic problems it is the correct answer, and Ch 16 makes it considerably stronger.

The Outcomes You Never Observe

Section 15.5b • The failure no splitting scheme fixes

Selective Labels

Return to the hook. Zillow's model was evaluated on houses — but which houses ended up in Zillow's inventory was decided by sellers choosing whether to accept the offer. Its real-world performance was measured on a set assembled by other people's choices, made in response to the model's own output.

You observe the outcome only for the units where some decision went one way.

A bank observes repayment only for applicants it approved. The defaults of rejected applicants are unobservable, forever.

A court observes reoffending only for defendants released on bail.

A hiring team observes performance only for candidates hired.

A platform observes conversion only for users shown the offer.

In each case the training data is a non-random sample selected on the very outcome you are modelling — and the selection was performed by a decision-maker, often a previous version of your own model.

2 min. Start with the bank example — everyone sees it immediately — then Zillow, then bail on the next slide. This section is the chapter's argument for why economists belong in machine learning, so give it real time and do not let it get compressed by an overrun earlier.

Perfect Splitting Discipline Provides No Protection

Kleinberg, Lakkaraju, Leskovec, Ludwig and Mullainathan (2018) set this out in the bail setting. An algorithm predicting reoffending can only be trained on defendants judges chose to release. If judges used information the data does not contain, the released population is favourably selected on unobservables — and the algorithm's apparent accuracy tells you little about the defendants judges detained. Which is exactly the population a proposal to replace judges with algorithms would apply to.

Your split can be immaculate. Your Pipeline airtight, your GroupKFold correct, your test set untouched, your cross-validation textbook. And your validation score still answers a question about a population that was chosen for you, by a process that correlates with the outcome.

The remedies are not statistical hygiene. They are research design — and two of the three you already own.

Randomise the decision. Approve some applicants by lottery and their outcomes are unselected. Chapter 9.

Use variation in the decision-maker. Randomly assigned judges of differing strictness give outcomes a stricter judge would have denied — someone else's coin flip has done your randomising for you.

Bound what you cannot see. State what would have to be true of the unobserved outcomes to reverse your conclusion. Chapter 10's E-value and sensitivity analysis, a second time.

3 min. The moment worth engineering is the realisation that a perfect train/test discipline provides no protection whatsoever here. Students who have just absorbed five sections of splitting discipline find that genuinely destabilising, which is the right reaction. Be precise about what this track owns: remedies 1 and 3 are Ch 9 and Ch 10 and they genuinely already have them. Remedy 2 is the instrumental-variables idea, which ECON 3916 does not develop — so present the judge-strictness variation as a design they can recognise, not an estimator they can run, and say that ECON 5200 builds it out. Do not tell them it is "Chapter 12"; on this track Chapter 12 is OLS for prediction.

In Practice

Section 15.6 • The checklist is the deliverable

Six Steps, in This Order

1

Split before you look. Hold out a test set as the first operation, before any exploration, scaling or feature engineering. Everything you learn from data you later test on contaminates the test — including what you learn with your eyes.

2

Put every transformation inside a Pipeline. Not a style preference — the mechanism that makes preprocessing leakage structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged.

3

Choose complexity by cross-validation, on the training set only. Five or ten folds. Never on the test set, and never on training error. Report the SE, and apply the one-standard-error rule.

4

Ask whether your rows are independent. Same unit more than once → GroupKFold. Ordered in time → do not shuffle at all. Random \(k\)-fold is the correct default only for genuinely independent observations, which economic data frequently is not.

5

Touch the test set once. Report that number. If you then change the model, the honest move is to say you no longer have a test set.

6

Ask what the prediction will be used for. Everything today measures squared error, which is right only if errors are symmetric, all observations matter equally, and nobody responds to your predictions. Zillow failed on the third with every other box ticked.

3 min. Present these as a checklist and say the checklist IS the deliverable — it is what PS4 Part B is graded against. Step 1 gets resistance because students want to explore first, so be concrete: looking at a scatter plot of the full data and choosing a functional form from it is model selection on the test set, performed by eye. Step 4 is the one their own project data will punish them for. Close on step 6 and the Zillow callback.

Common Misconception

"Cross-validation prevents overfitting."

It detects overfitting; it does not prevent it. Cross-validation is a measurement device — it tells you which complexity generalises best among those you tried. If every candidate is too flexible, CV faithfully reports the least-bad one.

And CV itself can be overfit: try enough model configurations against the same folds and you will eventually find one that suits those particular partitions. Chapter 8's multiple-comparisons problem again — and the reason the final test set exists.

1 min. Tie it back to slide 22's menu point: CV can only compare what you put on the menu, and it cannot tell you the menu was wrong. That is the setup for Ch 16, where the menu becomes continuous.

In Lab 15, 5-fold CV picks degree 4 (CV MSE 0.1401) and a single held-out set of 2,000 points picks degree 5 (CV MSE 0.1510). The fold-to-fold SE at degree 4 is 0.0112.

Which do you report?

A) Degree 4 — CV used all the data
B) Degree 5 — 2,000 held-out points beat 5 folds
C) Either — the difference is 0.0109 against an SE of 0.0112, so the data cannot rank them
D) Re-run with a different seed until they agree
1 min. Both A and B are defensible-sounding and both miss the point, which is why they are here. Let the room vote before saying anything.

Answer: C — The Data Cannot Rank Them

The gap is smaller than one standard error of the estimate itself. This is the one-standard-error rule doing its job on your own lab output: both sit inside the band, so take the simpler — degree 4 — and say why, which is that the comparison lacks resolution, not that degree 4 won.

D is the honest name for a common practice, and it is p-hacking. B confuses the size of the held-out set with the reliability of the selection made from it — which is exactly what the 200-splits histogram was about.

1 min. If a student objects that 0.0109 versus 0.0112 is "basically one SE, so it is borderline" — agree, and say that borderline is exactly the case the rule is for. The rule is not a hypothesis test; it is a tie-breaking convention that resolves ties toward simplicity.

Key Takeaways

1. Expected prediction error decomposes exactly into bias\(^2\) + variance + irreducible noise — for squared error — and only the first two are yours to trade.

2. The irreducible term is a floor. With \(\sigma = 0.55\) the best achievable error was 0.3219 against a floor of 0.3025, and no model of any sophistication crosses it.

3. Training error falls monotonically and therefore cannot select a model. The widening gap between training and validation error is the signature of overfitting.

4. One split is a single noisy draw — 200 splits of identical data gave a 2.4× spread and chose the wrong model 28% of the time. Cross-validate, print the SE, and when models sit within one SE of each other, take the simplest.

5. Leakage makes every number above meaningless, and it inflates scores most for the most flexible models — 44.5% versus 11.6% in our replication; and across 329 papers in 17 fields ML's reported advantage over logistic regression largely vanished once time was respected.

6. And none of it protects you when someone else's decision determined which outcomes you observe. That is Zillow — and it is an economics problem before it is a modelling one.

2 min. Land on takeaway 6 and make the return to the hook explicit: everything between slide 5 and here was the machinery, and Zillow had the machinery. What it did not have was an economist asking who gets to decline the offer.
