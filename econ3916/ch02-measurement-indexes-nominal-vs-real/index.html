Part One · 22 minutes

What Is an Index?

From a three-item basket to 80,000 price quotes

Part 1. We build the CPI from scratch, then look at what the BLS really does, then show that a different weighting rule gives a completely different index.

An Index Is a Compression Algorithm

A grocery store

30,000 → 1

The CPI compresses every shelf price into one number.

A stock market

30 → 1

The Dow compresses thirty share prices into one headline.

A product team

millions → 1

DAU/MAU compresses every user action into a dashboard tile.

Every compression discards information. The index is not wrong for discarding — that is its job. It is wrong when what it discarded is the thing you needed.

Lean on compression algorithm; this is a data science course and the metaphor is exact. A JPEG throws away high-frequency detail; a CPI throws away who bought what. Both are lossy, both are useful, and both fail in a characteristic way.

The Laspeyres Price Index

$$CPI_t = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_{t,i} \times Q_{0,i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_{0,i} \times Q_{0,i}} \times 100$$

\( P_{t,i} \) — price of good \( i \) today

\( P_{0,i} \) — price of good \( i \) in the base period

\( Q_{0,i} \) — base-period quantity. Frozen. This is the whole story.

\( k \) — how many goods are in the basket

The base period index equals 100 by construction — it is a definition, not a finding.

Point at the Q-nought term and say it twice. Everything interesting about the CPI, its comparability and its bias, comes from the fact that the quantities carry a subscript zero and not a subscript t.

What That Formula Actually Says

“How much would it cost today to buy exactly what a typical consumer bought in the base year?”

The strength

Freezing quantities makes the index simple to compute and comparable across time. Only prices move, so any change you see is a price change.

The weakness

Freezing quantities ignores substitution. Real people switch away from things that get expensive. The index assumes they do not.

Same design choice. Both consequences. That is what a measurement decision looks like.

House rule: never present math bare. This slide is the interpretation of the previous slide, and it earns its own screen because the strength and the weakness are the same feature seen from two sides.

Answer: B — Weight Beats Percentage

Rent is $14,400 of a $16,500 basket — about 87% of the weight. It contributes $4,320 of the total $5,220 increase. Gasoline, despite the biggest percentage move, contributes only $660.

Common mistake (A): a percentage change feels like the important quantity because it is the number printed next to the price. The Cost of Freezing the Basket

What real people do

Beef prices spike. You buy chicken. Your actual cost of living rises — but less than the beef price did.

What the index assumes

You kept buying the same quantity of beef, at the new price, all year.

Substitution bias. A Laspeyres index systematically overstates the true cost-of-living increase, because it holds quantities fixed while consumers move. It is the most studied limitation of the CPI — and it is not a bug in the data, it is a consequence of the formula.

Hold that thought. We will put a number on it in Part 2 — the Boskin Commission's.

Stress "not a bug in the data." Students want measurement problems to be sloppiness. This one is structural: you get comparability by freezing quantities, and the price of comparability is bias. Trade-offs, not mistakes.

The Hard Part: Quality Adjustment

A laptop costs the same as last year but has twice the processing power. Did the price stay flat — or effectively fall?

Where it works

The BLS uses hedonic regression — decomposing a price into the value of its measurable characteristics: screen size, clock speed, RAM, storage. Technology products have exactly those.

Where it fights back

Apparel, medical care and housing. What is the “characteristic vector” of a hospital stay? Quality adjustment there is contested, and the choices move the published number.

We are naming hedonics as a BLS practice, not estimating one. You already have the tools to read the idea: it is a regression of price on features — which is Chapter 12.

3916 scope: hedonics is named, not derived. The forward pointer to Ch 12 (OLS Prediction) is the right one for this track — a hedonic regression is just a linear model with product characteristics on the right-hand side.

What Is Actually in the Basket

Housing is more than a third of the index — bigger than food and medical care combined.

Weights come from the Consumer Expenditure Survey — about 24,000 households, 200+ spending categories.

They are updated every two years. That lag is a measurement problem all by itself — we come back to it as Goodhart's third mechanism.

Poll 1's lesson, at national scale: the biggest weight wins, not the biggest percentage move.

Tie back to the poll explicitly. Students who voted A now see the same structure at national scale — housing carries the index because housing carries the budget.

Housing: The BLS Does Not Track House Prices

A house is an investment asset. The CPI measures consumption. So instead of house prices, the BLS uses owners' equivalent rent (OER) — an estimate of what homeowners would pay to rent their own homes.

That was a deliberate, defensible choice. It also has a consequence:

2021–22: rents spiked

OER lags market rents by 12–18 months, so the CPI understated the immediate hit to households.

Late 2023: rents cooled

The same lag ran the other way, so the CPI overstated housing costs just as consumers were feeling relief.

This is the vibecession's second cause — independent of the rate-versus-level problem, and it hits the heaviest third of the basket.

Two independent mechanisms now explain the same gap: rate-vs-level, and the OER lag. Point out that neither involves anyone lying or making an error. Good measurement decisions can still produce a number that mismatches lived experience.

A Different Index, A Different Rule

CPI

Cost-weighted. Goods count in proportion to household spending.

Dow Jones

Price-weighted. Stocks count in proportion to their share price.

S&P 500

Cap-weighted. Companies count in proportion to market value.

The Dow's rule has a strange consequence. A $200 stock carries four times the weight of a $50 stock — regardless of how big the two companies are. And when a stock splits, the sum of prices falls even though nothing economic has happened.

Same 30 companies. Change the weighting rule and you get a different index — and a different story about the market.

Set up the split problem but do not solve it yet — the figure does that, and then the poll checks whether they actually followed. The deeper point to say out loud: an index does not discover a truth about the world, it implements rules someone chose.

The Divisor Exists to Absorb Arithmetic

Two stocks: A at $200, B at $100. Sum $300, divisor 0.152, index \( 300/0.152 = 1{,}974 \).

A splits 2-for-1. Market value is identical. But the price sum drops to $200, and the old divisor prints 1,316 — a 33% crash caused by nothing.

So the committee resets the divisor: \( d_{new} = d_{old} \times \frac{200}{300} = 0.1013 \), and the index prints 1,974 again.

The real divisor is about 0.152, so a $1 move in any Dow stock moves the index roughly 6.6 points. It started at 30 in 1928 and has been reset hundreds of times since.

Correction to the printed chapter: it rounds the reset divisor to 0.101, but 200/0.101 = 1,980, not 1,974. The exact rule is d_new = d_old x new sum / old sum = 0.1013. Use the rule, not the rounded number — a student will check the arithmetic.

Poll: The Morning After a Split

A Dow component trading at $200 announces a 2-for-1 split. Each share becomes two $100 shares. Nothing else about the company changes.

What happens to the Dow Jones Industrial Average the next morning?

A) It falls about 33% — the price sum dropped
B) Unchanged — the divisor is reset to absorb the split
C) It rises — there are now twice as many shares
D) Unchanged — the Dow weights by market cap, which did not move
D is the interesting distractor: right conclusion, wrong mechanism. Watch for it in the bar chart and address it directly, because a student who picks D thinks the Dow is the S&P.

Answer: B — The Divisor Does the Work

The divisor is reset from 0.1520 to 0.1013 precisely so the index prints the same value across the split. Maintenance, not markets.

A is the trap — it is exactly what would happen without the reset, which is why the divisor exists. D is the subtler trap: right answer, wrong reason. The Dow is price-weighted, not cap-weighted. The S&P 500 is the cap-weighted one. If you got the right answer from the wrong model, you will get the next question wrong.

Spend the time on D. "Right answer, wrong model" is a failure mode worth naming explicitly — it is undetectable on a multiple-choice exam and fatal in applied work.

Your Index BS Detector

Before you trust any index — in this course or in a job — ask four questions.

1

What is in the basket?  CPI: 80,000 items. Dow: 30 stocks.

2

How are the components weighted?  Spending share, price, or market cap — three different answers.

3

When was the basket last updated?  CPI: every two years. Dow: whenever a committee decides.

4

Who benefits from the number being high or low?  Social Security recipients want it high. Employers citing raises want it low.

These four recur in every chapter from here — through R² in Chapter 12 and feature importance in Chapter 19.

Write these on the board and leave them up for the rest of the class. Question 4 is the one students never think to ask and the one that matters most in industry — it converts a measurement question into a political-economy question.

The single most common operation in applied economics

Who Is Richer?

Your grandfather, 1970

$5,000

You, today

$55,000

a year — and you worry about rent

The naive comparison says you earn eleven times more. Is that right?

The honest comparison has to adjust for the fact that a 1970 dollar bought far more than a 2026 dollar. Do the adjustment before you draw the conclusion.

His $5,000 had the purchasing power of about $41,400 in 2025 dollars. Against your $55,000, that is a real gain of roughly 33% — not 1,000%.

The Error Everyone Makes Once

Both numbers must be in the same dollars before you divide. Deflate his 1970 income into 2020 dollars and then compare it to your 2025 nominal salary, and you are dividing two different currencies.

Mixed units

$55,000 (2025 nominal) ÷ $33,322 (2020 dollars) = +65%

Inflated by five years of price level that was never removed from the numerator.

Matched units

$44,220 (2020 dollars) ÷ $33,322 (2020 dollars) = +33%

Same answer as deflating both to 2025 dollars. The base year cancels.

Heads up for Lab 2: Step 4's closing line makes exactly this mistake and prints 65%. Read the units before you trust the print statement — and note the correction in your write-up.

Announce the lab defect out loud before they run it. Step 4 of lab-ch02-guided compares a 2025 nominal 55,000 against a 1970 income expressed in 2020 dollars. The correct real gain is about 33 percent. Turning it into a spot-the-bug exercise is better than a silent fix.

The Deflation Formula

$$\text{Real Value}_t = \frac{\text{Nominal Value}_t}{CPI_t} \times 100$$

Plain reading: a real value of $25.00 in 1982–84 dollars means the wage, after stripping out price-level change, buys what $25.00 bought in 1982–84.

Multiply by 100 and you land in base-period dollars (1982–84 for CPIAUCSL).

Multiply by the base-year CPI instead and you land in that year's dollars. This is what deflate_series() does.

The CPI is a weighted mean. Compare it to the simplest index of all, the sample mean \( \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \), which gives every observation equal weight. Choosing between equal and unequal weighting is itself a measurement decision.

The sample mean appears here as the degenerate index, which sets up Chapter 4 where we ask when a mean lies at all. Say the base year cancels in any ratio; it changes the units on the axis, never a real comparison.

Your First Reusable Function

def deflate_series(nominal, cpi, base_year=2020):
    base_cpi = cpi.loc[cpi.index.year == base_year].mean()
    aligned  = pd.DataFrame({"nominal": nominal, "cpi": cpi}).dropna()
    real     = (aligned["nominal"] / aligned["cpi"]) * base_cpi
    real.name = f"real_{base_year}"
    return real

Line 1. Take the base year's average CPI, not one month. A single month carries seasonal noise into every value.

Line 2. Align on shared dates and drop the mismatch. Wages start in 1964; CPI starts in 1947.

Line 3. Divide, then rescale. Whole columns divide element by element — no loop.

You will import this again in Chapters 4, 12, 16 and 20. Write it once, write it right.

Live-code this in the lab if there is time. The three lines each fix a real bug students hit: seasonal base, misaligned index, and looping over rows. Chapter 12 is OLS Prediction on this track, and deflation becomes a preprocessing step there.

Two Structural Breaks

A structural break is a point where the underlying process shifts abruptly. This chart has two — and both are invisible in the nominal series.

1973 — the peak, then two decades down

Oil shocks in 1973 and 1979. The end of Bretton Woods in 1971. A productivity slowdown that decoupled wages from output growth. Entire literatures exist on “the great decoupling.”

2020 — the jump

Pandemic stimulus and a tight labour market — plus a composition effect: lower-wage workers left the labour force, which raises the average mechanically without anyone getting a raise.

Money illusion hides structural change. The nominal line is smooth through both events. Deflating is not cosmetic — it is what makes the breaks visible at all.

Chapter 20 tests breaks like these formally with changepoint detection. Today you only have to see them.

The composition effect is worth a full sentence: an average can rise because the population changed, not because any individual did. That distinction returns in Chapter 9 when we design experiments.

Poll: The Raise That Was Not

Over ten years, your nominal hourly wage doubles. Over the same ten years, the CPI also doubles.

What happened to your purchasing power?

A) It doubled — your paycheck is twice as large
B) Unchanged — the raise exactly offset the price level
C) It halved — prices ate the raise and then some
D) Cannot tell without knowing the base year
Watch for D. It sounds like careful thinking and it is the sign that a student has not internalised that the base year is a units choice, not information.

Answer: B — The Ratio Is Unchanged

Real wage is nominal divided by CPI. Both terms doubled, so the ratio is identical: \( \frac{2w}{2p} = \frac{w}{p} \). You buy exactly what you bought before.

A is money illusion in its purest form — and it is the mechanism behind the vibecession: workers who got 3–4% nominal raises after 2021 felt cheated because they were, in real terms. D is tempting and wrong: the base year rescales every value by the same constant. It changes the units on the axis; it can never change a ratio of two real values.

If A polls high, that is good news for the lecture, not bad. Say so. It means the concept was worth twenty minutes and they now have a name for the mistake.

Misconception: “The CPI Measures the Cost of Living”

It measures a fixed basket, which is not the same thing as a true cost-of-living index. A genuine COLI would let consumers substitute.

Put a number on it

The Boskin Commission (1996) concluded the CPI overstated true cost-of-living increases by 0.8–1.6 percentage points per year.

The partial fix

The Chained CPI lets the basket update, correcting for substitution. It still cannot capture quality change, brand-new goods, or outlet substitution.

So when you hear “inflation was 3%,” the question is not just 3% of what? but 3% for whom, buying what, compared to what alternative?

Note that 0.8 to 1.6 points a year is not an academic curiosity: it is an argument about the size of Social Security payments. That is index question 4 - who benefits - arriving with a dollar figure attached. It sets up Goodhart's first mechanism.

Misconception: “Wages Have Been Stagnant Since the 1970s”

True — in real terms

Real AHE peaked in the early 1970s at $24.43 and had not durably regained that level by 2019 ($23.71, both in 2020 dollars).

Invisible — in nominal terms

Nominal AHE went from $2.50 to $31.83 over the same period. Quote that series and “stagnation” looks like nonsense.

Which claim you can defend depends entirely on which series and which deflator you chose. So state both. An unlabelled dollar figure is not an argument.

This is the professional habit today is really teaching: never present a monetary series without saying real or nominal, and in which year's dollars.

Both sides of the popular argument are quoting real data. Neither is lying. They are quoting different series, and the deflator is doing all the work. That is a more useful lesson than picking a side.

Real (2020 dollars): $3.39 → $4.94. That is +46%.

Roughly three-quarters of the sticker increase was inflation, not a more expensive hamburger.

Look at the right-hand end: since 2016 the real price is roughly flat while the sticker keeps climbing. A price that rises slower than the price level is a real price cut.

Executed 2026-09-05 from The Economist's big-mac-full-index plus FRED CPIAUCSL, matching the lab exactly. The chapter's table claims the sticker was frozen at 5.71 to 5.69 across 2020-2024; the dataset shows 4.82 to 5.69 over that window, so do not make the four-year-freeze claim from the podium. The chart behind you contradicts it.

But the Real Price Did Rise

46% real growth is not nothing. Why would a burger get genuinely more expensive?

Tradeable inputs

Beef, wheat, packaging. Priced in global markets. If they get expensive here, you can source them elsewhere.

Non-tradeable inputs

Restaurant rent, local labour, utilities. Priced locally. You cannot import a Boston storefront from a cheaper region.

Non-tradeables have inflated faster than the overall CPI, because they cannot be sourced away. That is the Balassa–Samuelson effect showing up in a hamburger: in high-productivity economies, non-tradeables become relatively more expensive over time.

This is the payoff for bothering to deflate. Without it you would attribute the whole 178 percent to inflation or the whole thing to beef. Deflation isolates the 46 percent that needs an economic explanation, and then the explanation is interesting.

Say this out loud in your first job: when someone reports “revenue grew 15%,” the first question is real or nominal? With 5% inflation, 15% nominal is 10% real. The other 5% was the dollar shrinking.

The revenue line is the single most portable thing in this lecture. Students will use it in an internship within a year. Say it slowly and tell them it is the question that makes an economist sound like an economist.

Part Four · 10 minutes

When Indexes Fail

Goodhart's Law, and the counter-metric that was Goodharted too

Part 4. The most transferable section of the chapter. Everything so far has been about building numbers correctly; this is about what happens once someone starts caring about them.

Goodhart, 1975

Working at the Bank of England, Charles Goodhart noticed that whenever the central bank targeted a monetary aggregate — M3, say — that aggregate immediately stopped being a reliable indicator. Banks found ways to move money in and out of the measured category without changing any underlying activity.

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

This is not about dishonesty. It is about optimization. When people know they are measured on a specific metric, they optimize that metric — not necessarily the behaviour it was designed to capture.

Ask for examples before moving on: GPA, standardized test scores, follower counts, hours logged, story points. Take three or four fast. Students always have better examples than you do, and the ones they volunteer are what they will remember.

The Signature of a Goodharted Metric

Before the target. The metric and the thing it measures move together. That is why it was chosen — it was a good proxy.

After the target. The reported number keeps climbing. The underlying reality does not. Nobody had to lie for this to happen.

The diagnostic problem: from inside the organization you only see the blue line. The orange line is not measured — that is the whole reason you were using a proxy.

Schematic, not data. It names the shape so you can recognise it in a real dashboard.

Label the figure as schematic explicitly - do not let anyone think it is estimated. The point it earns is the third box: you cannot see the orange line, which is why detecting Goodhart requires a second measurement rather than better attention.

Three Mechanisms, Three Different Fixes

MechanismWhat happensExampleFix
1. Optimization

Diagnosing which mechanism is running is what determines the fix. A counter-metric does nothing about obsolescence. Faster reweighting does nothing about gaming.

This table is the practical skill of the section. Students reach for counter-metrics as a universal answer; make them notice that the fix is mechanism-specific. Mechanism 1 is not solvable by measurement at all - it is political economy.

Tech KPIs Are Indexes Too

DAU/MAU — daily active users over monthly active users, the “stickiness ratio.” A value of 0.50 means half of the month's users show up on a given day. Facebook has historically run near 0.65, among the highest in the industry.

But what counts as “active”? Opening the app for one second to dismiss a notification scores identically to a thirty-minute session. The index compresses wildly different behaviours into one fraction.

Misconception: “a higher DAU/MAU always means better engagement.” It measures visit frequency, not visit quality. A notification-spam strategy inflates DAU while creating zero user value. Always pair a frequency metric with a quality metric.

Run the four index questions on it: what is in the basket, how is it weighted, when was it updated, who benefits?

Explicitly run the four questions from Part 1 against DAU/MAU. The basket is user-sessions; the weighting is one-vote-per-user-per-day regardless of depth; it is never reweighted; and the product team's promotion case benefits from it being high. All four detect the problem before any scandal.

Lifetime Value, and Its Two Lies

$$LTV = \frac{ARPU}{\text{monthly churn rate}}$$

Plain reading: with average revenue per user of $12.99 a month and 5% of subscribers cancelling each month, \( LTV = 12.99 / 0.05 = 259.80 \) — the revenue you expect from one subscriber before they leave.

Lie 1: churn is constant

It is not. Churn is front-loaded — new subscribers leave far faster than loyal ones.

Lie 2: ARPU is constant

It moves with pricing changes, upsells and discounts, none of which the formula sees.

A fine first approximation — and a Goodhart trap the moment it becomes a target. Teams “improve LTV” by redefining churn (counting a paused subscription as active) rather than by retaining anyone.

Never present math bare: the interpretation line does the work here. The redefinition attack is the important one - it requires no fraud, only a definitional memo, and it is undetectable from the number itself.

Meta, 2021: The Metric That Measured Notifications

Internal Meta documents reported by the Wall Street Journal showed that product teams had optimized DAU/MAU to the point where it no longer reflected genuine engagement. Features were prioritized by their ability to drive daily opens, regardless of whether those opens meant anything.

What went up

Notification frequency, tuned to maximize re-engagement — and with it, the reported DAU/MAU.

What did not

Anything a user would recognise as value. Many found the notifications irritating.

Mechanism 2, exactly as the table describes it. So what is the fix? The theory says: build a counter-metric.

Set up the next slide as if the answer is obvious. It is - and that is what makes the MSI story land. Do not preview it.

Meta Had Already Built the Fix — in 2018

January 2018 — three years before the Facebook Files. Meta re-ranked News Feed around “Meaningful Social Interactions” (MSI), explicitly to shift the objective from passive consumption toward genuine interaction between people. This is exactly the repair the theory prescribes.

And it became the second Goodhart failure. Because MSI scored reshares and comment threads heavily, the ranking system learned that the reliable way to manufacture “meaningful interaction” was content that provoked argument. The 2021 internal research found the metric was amplifying divisive material — and that Meta's own data scientists had raised it internally.

The hard lesson. A counter-metric is still a metric. It compresses, it becomes a target, and it can be optimized into a failure of its own. Index repair is not a state you reach — it is maintenance you keep doing. An immune response is not immunity.

This is the intellectual peak of the lecture. Slow down. Sources: Meta News Feed announcement January 2018; Wall Street Journal Facebook Files September 2021. The takeaway students should leave with is that Chapter 2's question - what did this number discard - has to be asked of the guardrail too.

Wells Fargo: When the Accounts Are Real

Meta inflated a digital number. Wells Fargo fabricated actual bank accounts.

From 2002 to 2016 the retail bank set targets for cross-selling — products per household. The internal target was eight, sloganized as “Going for Gr-eight.” Branch managers were evaluated, promoted and fired on that single number.

The instructive part: cross-sell was a reasonable metric. Customers holding multiple products really are more loyal and more profitable. The failure was not the metric's logic — it was its promotion from diagnostic indicator to compensation target.

Sources: CFPB consent order September 2016; DOJ and SEC settlement February 2020; Fed enforcement action February 2018. Emphasise the last box - students want to conclude the metric was stupid. It was not. The promotion to a compensation target is what broke it, and that can happen to any good metric.

Poll: Which Counter-Metric Catches It?

Wells Fargo's cross-sell ratio kept rising for over a decade while the behaviour it was meant to capture was being destroyed.

Which counter-metric would have caught it earliest?

A) Total accounts opened per branch per quarter
B) Share of new accounts opened at the customer's own initiative
C) Average revenue per household
D) Employee satisfaction survey scores
The design principle being tested: a counter-metric must be hard to move by the SAME gaming behaviour as the primary metric. Ask them to apply that test to each option before voting.

Answer: B — It Moves the Opposite Way

The fraudulent accounts were bank-initiated by construction. So as the headline ratio climbed, the customer-initiated share would have collapsed — a visible divergence, exactly the shape on the Goodhart chart.

A fails because it is the primary metric with a different denominator — the same gaming moves it the same direction. C is contaminated: fee revenue rose on the fake accounts too. D is lagging and noisy, and would have surfaced years late. The rule: a counter-metric must be hard to move by the same behaviour that games the primary metric.

Give them the rule as a portable sentence. It is the one testable design principle in the whole Goodhart section and it generalises directly to Chapter 9's experiment design and Chapter 18's evaluation metrics.

Key Takeaways

An index is a compression. What is in the basket, how it is weighted, and when it was updated shape every conclusion drawn from it.

Laspeyres freezes the basket. That buys comparability across time and costs you a known upward substitution bias.

Never compare dollars across time without deflating — and make sure both sides are in the same dollars before you divide.

Tech KPIs are indexes with the same anatomy and the same failure modes as the CPI.

Goodhart's Law. When a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure — and the counter-metric you build to fix it becomes a target too.
