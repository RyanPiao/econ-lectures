1  •  The Language of Uncertainty

Three interpretations, three axioms, and one trick worth more than either.

11 minutes for this section. Ends at minute 15.

Three Ways to Mean “Probability”

Classical — count equally likely outcomes. \(P(\text{six}) = 1/6\). Works where symmetry was designed in: dice, cards, roulette. Almost never defensible in economics.

Frequentist — the long-run frequency across repeatable trials. The chance a scratch ticket pays is the fraction of winners across millions sold. Awkward for one-off events like “a recession in 2027.”

Bayesian — a degree of belief, updated with evidence. Handles one-off events naturally, which is why it dominates election forecasting and A/B testing (Ch 9).

All three obey the same three rules.

3 min. Ask which one GenCast is doing. Answer: Bayesian — it is a belief about one specific tomorrow, and there is no long run of tomorrows to take a frequency over.

The Three Axioms

$$P(A) \geq 0 \qquad P(\Omega) = 1 \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

the third holds when \(A\) and \(B\) cannot both happen

In plain language: probabilities are never negative; something must happen; and if two things cannot happen together, the chance of either is the sum of their chances.

Before you compute anything, ask what \(\Omega\) is. For “the probability of a 2027 recession”: all US quarters since 1947 (frequentist), or all trajectories consistent with today (Bayesian)? In economics the sample space is usually harder than the arithmetic that follows.

3 min. Everything today — Bayes, the Law of Large Numbers, Value at Risk — is these three lines plus bookkeeping. Say that explicitly; it lowers the perceived difficulty of the rest of the hour.

Two Rules That Follow Immediately

Complement rule

$$P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$$

The chance something does not happen is one minus the chance it does.

General addition rule

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

Subtract the overlap, or you count it twice.

The complement rule looks trivial. It is the single most useful line in risk analysis.

2 min. Foreshadow: "probability of at least one default in a portfolio" is computed as one minus "probability of zero defaults", every time, in every bank.

At Least One Six in Four Rolls

Counting “at least one” directly means enumerating one six, two sixes, three, four. The complement is one line.

$$P(\text{no six, one roll}) = \tfrac{5}{6} \qquad P(\text{no six, four rolls}) = \left(\tfrac{5}{6}\right)^{4} = 0.482$$
$$P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - 0.482 = 0.518$$

In plain language: compute what you do not want, then subtract from one. Just above a coin flip — which surprises most people.

2 min. Work it on the board rather than clicking through. Then set up the poll without giving anything away.

Poll 1 — Vote on Your Device

A fair six-sided die, rolled four times. What is \(P(\text{at least one six})\)?

No arithmetic — go with your instinct.

A) \(4 \times \tfrac{1}{6} \approx 0.667\)
B) about 0.52
C) \(\tfrac{1}{6} \approx 0.167\)
D) \(1 - (\tfrac{1}{6})^{4} \approx 0.999\)
1 min. Expect a heavy A. Do not let anyone compute — the value of this poll is entirely in how many people add one-sixth four times.

Answer: B — 0.518

\(1 - (5/6)^4 = 0.518\). Barely better than a coin flip.

Why A is wrong, and why it matters. Adding \(1/6\) four times treats the four rolls as mutually exclusive. They are not. Push the same logic to seven rolls and it returns a probability above 1 — which is how you know it broke an axiom, not just a calculation.

D subtracts the wrong complement: “no six ever” is \((5/6)^4\), not \((1/6)^4\).

1 min. The seven-rolls line is the one to deliver slowly — it converts "you made an arithmetic slip" into "you violated an axiom," which is much more memorable.

Conditional Probability

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0$$

In plain language: conditioning shrinks the sample space. You stop asking one question and start asking a completely different one.

What share of all transactions are fraud?

0.1%

What share of flagged transactions are fraud?

the real question

2 min. The two boxes are the whole idea. Students who confuse P(A|B) with P(B|A) are confusing these two boxes, every time.

Bayes’ Theorem Inverts the Conditioning

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\, P(H)}{P(E)}$$

\(P(H)\) — the prior. What you believed before.

\(P(E \mid H)\) — the likelihood. How well the evidence fits.

\(P(E)\) — the marginal. How often that evidence turns up at all.

\(P(H \mid E)\) — the posterior. What you believe after.

In plain language: Bayes is a learning rule. Start with a belief, look at evidence, end with a revised belief. The denominator only rescales, so the answer is still a proper probability.

3 min. Name all four out loud in the fraud example before moving on: prior = 0.001, likelihood = 0.99, marginal = the number we are about to compute, posterior = what the poll got wrong.

Two Steps, on the Board

Step 1 — how often does the model fire at all?

$$P(\text{Flag}) = \underbrace{0.99 \times 0.001}_{0.00099 \ \text{real}} + \underbrace{0.02 \times 0.999}_{0.01998 \ \text{false}} = 0.02097$$

Look where the mass is. The false alarms outnumber the catches twenty to one before you compute anything else.

Step 2 — invert.

$$P(\text{Fraud} \mid \text{Flag}) = \frac{0.00099}{0.02097} = 0.0472$$
3 min. Do this on the board, not by clicking. The under-braces in Step 1 are the whole explanation — once students see 0.00099 next to 0.01998 they do not need Step 2 to know the answer is small.

Misconception: “A 99%-Accurate Test Is 99% Reliable”

Accuracy is a property of the test. What you actually need is a property of the result: given a positive, how likely is the condition?

When the base rate is low, the answer is low no matter how good the test is. There is no accuracy figure that rescues you from a rare event.

Always ask for the base rate. It is the number nobody puts in the press release.

That limiting curve is the probability distribution. Every observation you have ever plotted is one draw from it. The histogram was your estimate; the distribution is what you were estimating.

2 min. This is the Chapter 3 bridge and it lands well — several students visibly reinterpret three weeks of work here. Do not rush it.

Two Discrete Workhorses

Bernoulli — one trial, two outcomes

$$P(X = 1) = p, \quad P(X = 0) = 1-p$$

Every binary outcome in economics: employed or not, default or repay, click or no click. \(n\) independent ones make a Binomial.

Poisson — counts of rare events

$$P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^{k}}{k!}$$

How many times does a rare thing happen this month? Claims, earthquakes, arrivals at a help desk.

Banks’ own operational-risk models pair a Poisson count of loss events with a heavy-tailed severity distribution — how many, times how bad.

2 min. Brisk. They need to know these exist and to recognise the shape of the question each one answers. No derivations.

The Normal, and Its Two Parameters

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)$$

In plain language: \(\mu\) says where the bell sits, \(\sigma\) says how wide it is. Nothing else. Two numbers commit you to a claim about how likely every possible value is — including the ones you have never observed.

$$E[X] = \sum_x x\,P(X=x)$$

\(\bar{x}\) from Ch 2 estimates this.

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = E\big[(X - E[X])^{2}\big]$$

\(s^{2}\) from Ch 4 estimates this.

The statistic is what you have. The parameter is what you want. Chapters 6 and 7 are about that gap.

3 min. The sample-versus-population distinction is the single most important thing on this slide and it is worth saying twice. It is the whole reason Chapter 6 exists.

Same Bell. Very Different Tails.

Beyond −3 s.d. the fat-tailed curve is about 21 times more likely to land. Same centre. Same general shape.

1 min. Do not get drawn into what degrees of freedom means. If asked: "a dial for how fat the tails are; three is very fat." That is enough for this course.

4  •  Monte Carlo — and How Wrong It Is

When the maths is intractable, stop solving and start counting.

19 minutes, the longest section. Ends at minute 60. If you are behind, cut the Ulam and von Neumann history, not the standard error block.

What Monte Carlo Is

Draw thousands of random scenarios from your distributions, then count what happened. You trade mathematical elegance for compute, and it works.

Named after the casino. In the 1940s Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann, working at Los Alamos, realised they could estimate hard integrals by random sampling instead of solving them.

The Fed — 250,000 draws for stress tests

Swiss Re — hundreds of thousands of simulated hurricane seasons

Climate economists — sampling damage-function uncertainty

2 min. The framing that lands: "you already know how to do this. You have been calling it a for-loop."

Estimating \(\pi\) by Throwing Darts

Inscribe a circle of radius 1 in a 2×2 square. The circle’s area is \(\pi\); the square’s is 4. Drop points uniformly — the fraction inside converges to \(\pi/4\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, 1_000_000)
y = rng.uniform(-1, 1, 1_000_000)
pi_hat = 4 * ((x**2 + y**2) <= 1.0).mean()   # 3.14178

No geometry. No integral. Just counting. That is the whole method — and it scales to problems where no formula exists at all.

2 min. Mention default_rng rather than np.random.seed and say why in one sentence: default_rng gives you an isolated generator, so nothing else in your program can quietly consume your draws. They will use it all term.

What “the Simulation Is Working” Looks Like

3.14178 against a true 3.14159. The estimate stays inside its own ±2 SE envelope essentially throughout.

1 min. Point at the shaded funnel. That shape is the picture of this entire lecture — it is the same shape as the cover slide, and it is what an error bar looks like when you watch it shrink.

The Law of Large Numbers

$$\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \ \longrightarrow \ E[X] \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

In plain language: average enough independent draws and you get arbitrarily close to the true expected value. The LLN is the guarantee that simulation works at all.

It is also why the Massachusetts Lottery’s $1.067 billion is not a gamble. Across millions of independent tickets, the payout must converge to 74.1 cents on the dollar.

2 min. Callback to Lab 5 Part 1 Q3: the sample proportion IS a sample mean, and the mean is the non-robust statistic they distrusted in Chapter 4. Why does LLN still work? Because non-robust does not mean biased — it means sensitive to individual points, and the LLN is a statement about what happens when no individual point matters any more.

Every Monte Carlo Number Has a Standard Error

$$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \qquad \text{for a proportion out of } n \text{ draws}$$

In plain language: run the same simulation again with a different seed and you get a different answer. The standard error says how much different — it is the typical distance between one run’s answer and the truth.

About two-thirds of runs land within 1 SE of the truth.

About 95% land within 2 SE.

The Same Result, with Its Error Bars On

Both bars’ error bars straddle the theoretical line. The simulation confirms the theory — it does not correct it.

1 min. "Confirms, does not correct" is the sentence. Students routinely report simulated results as though they have improved on the algebra.

The Rule to Take Away

Report the standard error beside every simulated number. And refuse to rank two simulated numbers that sit within about two standard errors of each other — the simulation has not told you which is larger.

This is not pedantry. “Method A beat method B by 0.3 points” is a claim about the world. If 0.3 is half a standard error, you have not reported a finding — you have reported your seed.

2 min. This is the slide to photograph. Tell them so. It is also the grading criterion for Lab 5 Part 4 — be explicit about that now rather than in the rubric.

The Price of Precision

Monte Carlo error falls at \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\)

In plain language: you need four times the draws to halve the error. Going from 10,000 to 20,000 buys a 29% reduction, not 50%.

The redeeming feature. That rate does not depend on how many dimensions the problem has. Grid methods die in high dimensions; Monte Carlo does not notice. That is why a slow method survives.

2 min. The dimension-independence line is what makes Monte Carlo worth teaching rather than a curiosity. It also explains why the Fed can simulate a bank's whole balance sheet.

Answer: B — 40,000

This is the chapter’s own warning: “more simulations always means better results” is true but wildly oversold. At some point compute costs more than the precision is worth. 10,000–100,000 covers most economic applications; the Fed uses 250,000 because billion-dollar capital decisions turn on it.

5  •  Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall

Where a Monte Carlo standard error becomes a sentence in a memo.

12 minutes. Ends at minute 72.

Value at Risk

$$\mathrm{VaR}_q = -F^{-1}(1-q)$$

\(F^{-1}\) is the quantile function of portfolio returns

In plain language: at 95% confidence, VaR is the loss you expect to exceed on about one day in twenty.

It is a cut-off, not a worst case. A book with a 95% VaR of $19,494 could have a worst day of $25,000 or of $250,000. VaR reports both identically.

2 min. The cut-off-versus-worst-case confusion is the single most common misreading of a risk report, and it appears verbatim in Lab 5 Part 4 Question 4.

By Hand First, Assuming Normality

Mean daily return \(\mu = 0.04\%\), standard deviation \(\sigma = 1.2\%\):

$$\mathrm{VaR}_{0.95} = -(\mu - 1.645\,\sigma) = -(0.0004 - 1.645 \times 0.012) = 0.01934$$

$19,338

on a $1,000,000 portfolio

Clean, instant — and it is entirely a consequence of the words “assuming normality.” Monte Carlo removes that “if.”

2 min. Hold onto $19,338. It is the reference point for the whole rest of the section.

Expected Shortfall

$$\mathrm{ES}_q = -E\big[R \mid R \leq -\mathrm{VaR}_q\big]$$

In plain language: if the day goes badly, how badly on average? ES averages every loss past the cut-off, so it sees the shape of the tail instead of one point on it.

ES is always larger than VaR — it is an average of numbers that are all worse than the cut-off. On our book: $24,230 against $19,494.

2 min. If a student's ES ever comes out below their VaR, they have a sign error or they have selected the wrong tail. Say that now; it is the single most common Lab 5 Part 4 bug.

Why the Regulators Switched

1. VaR ignores severity

A book with a 95% VaR of $19,494 could have a worst day of $25,000 or of $250,000. VaR reports them identically. ES does not.

2. VaR is not sub-additive

Combine two portfolios and measured VaR can come out higher than the sum of the separate VaRs — so the measure can penalise diversification. ES cannot do that.

Basel III’s Fundamental Review of the Trading Book made ES the standard for bank trading books in the EU and UK from 1 January 2025. The US is phasing it in over 2025–26. Banks are implementing this right now.

2 min. Expect "why not use ES from the start?" Answer: VaR was simpler to compute and to explain, and it took the 2008 crisis plus fifteen years of regulatory revision to move. Do not state the coherence axioms — sub-additivity in one sentence is the right depth for this course.

Key Takeaways

1. A probability distribution is an assumption made explicit. Two numbers commit you to a claim about every value you have never seen.

2. Bayes is a learning rule, and the base rate does most of the work. 99% accuracy on a 0.1% base rate gives you 4.7%.

3. Monte Carlo replaces intractable maths with counting. The LLN says it works; \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) is the price.

4. Every simulated number carries a standard error. Report it, and do not rank inside the noise.

5. Expected Shortfall replaced VaR because VaR names a cut-off and ignores how bad things get past it.
