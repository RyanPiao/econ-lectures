Adventures in Microeconomics · Practice MC #1

Q1. A firm's profit function is \(\pi(q) = 20q - q^2 - 25\). The profit-maximizing quantity satisfies which first-order condition?

A) \(20 - 2q = 0\)

B) \(20q - q^2 = 25\)

C) \(-2q = 0\)

D) \(20q + 25 = q^2\)

Answer

A

Differentiate \(\pi(q)\) and set equal to zero: \(\pi'(q) = 20 - 2q = 0\), giving \(q^* = 10\). The FOC always sets the derivative of the objective equal to zero at an interior optimum.

Adventures in Microeconomics · Practice MC #2

Q2. Which statement best describes equilibrium in a competitive market?

A) The price the government wants to set

B) A price-quantity pair at which neither buyers nor sellers want to change their behavior

C) The point where supply is at its maximum

D) The price at which firms make zero accounting profit

Answer

B

Equilibrium is the state where no agent has an incentive to deviate. In a competitive market, that's where \(Q^d(P^*) = Q^s(P^*)\) — buyers' and sellers' plans align.

01
Adventures in Microeconomics · Practice MC #3

Q3. A bike courier has completed 40 deliveries today, earning \(\$520\) in total revenue against \(\$360\) in total cost. A 41st delivery is offered: it pays \(\$9\) and would cost \(\$12\) to complete. Should she take it?

A) Yes — she averages \(\$4\) of profit per delivery, so one more adds about \(\$4\).

B) Yes — the \(\$9\) fare covers her \(\$9\) average cost per delivery.

C) It makes no difference to her total profit.

D) No — it earns \(\$9\) but costs \(\$12\), cutting total profit by \(\$3\).

Answer

D

Averages describe history; the margin decides the next unit. Her averages look healthy — \(AR = 520/40 = \$13\), \(AC = 360/40 = \$9\), so \(\$4\) of profit per delivery — but none of that bears on the 41st job. That one turns on \(MR = \$9\) against \(MC = \$12\), so it destroys \(\$3\): total profit falls from \(\$160\) to \(\$157\). A applies the average to a marginal decision. B compares marginal revenue to average cost — and the \(\$9\) fare matching the \(\$9\) average cost exactly is the bait. C ignores the \(\$3\) gap altogether.

Adventures in Microeconomics · Practice MC #5

Q5. For \(f(x)\) to have an interior maximum at \(x^*\), we need:

A) \(f'(x^*) = 0\) only

B) \(f'(x^*) = 0\) AND \(f''(x^*) \leq 0\)

C) \(f''(x^*) \geq 0\) only

Answer

B

  • \(Q^d(P), Q^s(P)\) as functions
  • Inverse: \(p^d(Q), p^s(Q)\)
  • Linear: \(Q = a - bP\)
  • Slope = \(dQ/dP\)
  • Solve \(Q^d(P^*) = Q^s(P^*)\)
  • Find \(P^*, Q^*\)
  • Algebraic, not graphical
  • Single equation in \(P\)
  • \(CS = \int_0^{Q^*}\!(p^d - p^*)\,dq\)
  • \(PS = \int_0^{Q^*}\!(p^* - p^s)\,dq\)
  • Linear → triangles
  • Nonlinear → integrate
  • Total-differentiate eq. condition
  • \(\dfrac{dP^*}{d\alpha}, \dfrac{dQ^*}{d\alpha}\)
  • Sign + magnitude
  • Predicts shift outcomes
  • Movement: \(P\) changes → \(Q\) adjusts
  • Shift: parameter \(\alpha\) changes → curve moves
  • Math: \(\partial/\partial P\) vs \(\partial/\partial \alpha\)
  • Don’t confuse
Key takeaway: CS and PS are integrals: \(CS = \int_0^{Q^*}\!(p^d(q) - p^*)\, dq\). In the linear case the integral reduces to \(\tfrac{1}{2} Q^*(p^d(0) - p^*)\) — the familiar triangle.

Supply, Demand, and Market Equilibrium · Practice MC #2

Q2. Inverse demand is \(p^d(Q) = 50 - 0.5Q\). At equilibrium \(Q^* = 60\) and \(P^* = 20\), consumer surplus equals:

A) \(60\)

B) \(600\)

C) \(900\)

D) \(1{,}800\)

Answer

C

Watch which number is the height. The choke price is \(p^d(0) = 50\), but the triangle’s height is \(p^d(0) - p^* = 50 - 20 = \mathbf{30}\) — not 50. So \(CS = \tfrac12(\text{base})(\text{height}) = \tfrac12(60)(30) = 900\). By integral: \(\int_0^{60}\!\left[(50 - 0.5q) - 20\right]dq = \int_0^{60}(30 - 0.5q)\,dq = \left[30q - 0.25q^2\right]_0^{60} = 1800 - 900 = 900\) ✓. A reports \(Q^*\). B uses \(p^*\) as the height, \(\tfrac12(60)(20)\). D forgets the \(\tfrac12\) and gives the rectangle \(60 \times 30\).

Elasticity and Government Intervention
1Point Elasticity
2Cross & Income
3Tax Incidence
4DWL of a Tax
5Price Controls
  • \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{dQ}{dP} \cdot \dfrac{P}{Q}\)
  • Derivative form (NOT midpoint)
  • \(|\varepsilon| > 1\) elastic
  • Unit elastic \((\varepsilon = -1)\): \(MR = 0\)
  • Income: \(\varepsilon_I = \dfrac{dQ}{dI} \cdot \dfrac{I}{Q}\)
  • \(\varepsilon_I > 1\) luxury, \(<0\) inferior
  • Cross-price: \(>0\) substitutes
  • \(<0\) complements
  • Consumer share: \(\dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}\)
  • Legal payer \(\ne\) economic burden
  • More inelastic side pays more
  • Symmetric for subsidy
  • \(DWL \approx \tfrac{1}{2} t \Delta Q\) (triangle)
  • Scales with \(t^2\)
  • Bigger if either curve more elastic
  • Welfare loss from wedge
  • Ceiling below \(P^*\) → shortage + DWL
  • Floor above \(P^*\) → surplus + DWL
  • Non-binding = no effect
  • Subsidy = negative tax
Key takeaway: Point elasticity uses the derivative: \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{dQ}{dP} \cdot \dfrac{P}{Q}\). Tax incidence depends on the ratio of elasticities, not which side legally pays.

Q2. A market has \(\varepsilon_D = -1\) and \(\varepsilon_S = 3\). A \$2/unit tax on producers is borne by consumers in the share:

A) \(25\%\)

B) \(50\%\)

Elasticity and Government Intervention · Handout: Soda Tax in a Calibrated Market

Set-up A city considers a \$0.50/oz tax on sugary drinks. Pre-tax: \(Q^d = 1000 - 200P\), \(Q^s = -200 + 200P\) (Q in millions of ounces/month, P in dollars/oz).

Show your work for each part:

a

Solve for the pre-tax equilibrium \(P^*, Q^*\) and compute \(\varepsilon_D\) at the equilibrium.

b

The tax is statutorily on producers — they receive \(P^c - 0.50\) per unit, so new supply is \(Q^s = -200 + 200(P^c - 0.50)\). Solve for the new consumer price \(P^c\), producer price \(P^p = P^c - 0.50\), and quantity \(Q^t\).

c

What share of the tax is borne by consumers? Verify against \(\varepsilon_S / (\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)\).

d

Compute the DWL triangle \(\tfrac{1}{2} t \cdot \Delta Q\) and tax revenue \(R = t Q^t\).

Answer Sketch

(a) \(1000 - 200P = -200 + 200P \Rightarrow 1200 = 400P\), so \(P^* = 3,\ Q^* = 400\). \(\varepsilon_D = (dQ/dP)(P/Q) = -200(3/400) = -1.5\) (elastic). (b) Expand new supply: \(Q^s = -200 + 200(P^c - 0.50) = -200 + 200P^c - 100 = -300 + 200P^c\). Set \(Q^d = Q^s\): \(1000 - 200P^c = -300 + 200P^c \Rightarrow 1300 = 400P^c \Rightarrow P^c = \$3.25\). Producer price \(P^p = 3.25 - 0.50 = \$2.75\). Quantity \(Q^t = 1000 - 200(3.25) = 350\). (c) Consumer pays \(\Delta P^c = 3.25 - 3.00 = \$0.25\) of the \$0.50 tax = 50%. Formula: \(\varepsilon_S = 200(3/400) = 1.5\); share \(= \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D) = 1.5/(1.5 - (-1.5)) = 0.5\) ✓. (d) \(DWL = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.50)(400 - 350) = \$12.5\)M/mo; \(R = 0.50 \cdot 350 = \$175\)M/mo.

Q1. For \(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{0.4} x_2^{0.6}\), the MRS at \((x_1, x_2) = (10, 30)\) is:

B

Q2. Alex prefers apples to bananas, bananas to cherries, but cherries to apples. Which axiom is violated?

A) Completeness

B) Transitivity

C) Monotonicity

D) Convexity

B

Q3. Let \(V(x_1, x_2) = U(x_1, x_2) + 100\). Then \(V\):

A) Has higher MRS at every point

B) Has the same MRS as \(U\) at every point

C) Has zero MRS

B

Adding a constant is a monotonic transformation: it preserves preferences. \(MU_i\) for \(V\) equals \(MU_i\) for \(U\) (derivative of a constant is zero), so the ratio \(MU_1/MU_2 = \text{MRS}\) is unchanged. Utility is ordinal, not cardinal.

  • \(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = I\)
  • Slope \(-p_1/p_2\)
  • Intercepts \(I/p_i\)
  • Price change → rotation
  • Income change → parallel
  • \(\mathcal{L} = U + \lambda(I - p\!\cdot\!x)\)
  • where \(p\!\cdot\!x = p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2\)
  • \(\lambda\) is the multiplier
  • One \(\mathcal{L}\) per constraint
  • \(MU_i = \lambda p_i\) for each \(i\)
  • Tangency: \(\text{MRS} = p_1/p_2\)
  • Equimarginal: \(\dfrac{MU_i}{p_i} = \lambda\)
  • \(\lambda\) = MU of income
  • \(x_i^* = \alpha_i I / p_i\)
  • Spending share = exponent
  • No cross-price effect
  • Own-price elasticity = \(-1\)
  • Income elasticity = \(+1\)
  • Perfect subs: corner on higher \(MU/p\)
  • Perfect comp: \(ax_1 = bx_2\) + budget
  • Kinked budgets (SNAP, ACA)
  • Tangency may fail at corners
Key takeaway: Write \(\mathcal{L} = U(x_1, x_2) + \lambda(I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2)\). FOCs give \(\dfrac{MU_1}{p_1} = \dfrac{MU_2}{p_2} = \lambda\). Tangency: \(\text{MRS} = p_1/p_2\). For Cobb-Douglas, just use \(x_i^* = \alpha_i I / p_i\).

Set-up Sam has \(U = x_1^{0.5} x_2^{0.5}\), income \(I = 200\), prices \(p_1 = 2,\ p_2 = 4\). Now \(p_1\) rises to \(4\).

Show your work for each part:

a

Compute the original Marshallian bundle \((x_1^{(a)}, x_2^{(a)})\) and original utility \(\bar u\).

b

Compute the new Marshallian bundle \((x_1^{(b)}, x_2^{(b)})\) at the new price. Total effect on \(x_1\) = (b) - (a).

c

Find Hicksian demand \(h_1\) at new \(p_1\) holding \(\bar u\) fixed. Substitution effect = \(h_1 - x_1^{(a)}\).

d

Compute income effect = \(x_1^{(b)} - h_1\). Verify SE + IE = total effect. Sign each.

Answer Sketch

(a) \(x_1 = 50,\ x_2 = 25,\ \bar u = \sqrt{1250} \approx 35.36\). (b) At \(p_1 = 4\): \(x_1^{(b)} = 25,\ x_2^{(b)} = 25\). Total effect \(= 25 - 50 = -25\). (c) Hicksian solves a different problem — minimise spending subject to \(U = \bar u\). Step 1, tangency at the NEW price: \(\dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2} = \dfrac{x_2}{x_1} = \dfrac{p_1'}{p_2} = \dfrac44 = 1\), so \(x_2 = x_1\). Step 2, substitute into the UTILITY CONSTRAINT (not the budget): \(\sqrt{x_1 \cdot x_1} = x_1 = \bar u\), giving \(h_1 = h_2 = 25\sqrt2 \approx 35.36\). Show your work for each part:

a

Write the cost-min Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}\) and derive the tangency \(\text{MRTS} = w/r\) from the FOCs.

c

Compute the minimum cost \(c(4, 9, 60)\) and recover \(\mu\) from FOC 1.

d

Answer Sketch

(a) \(\mathcal{L} = 4L + 9K + \mu(60 - K^{0.5} L^{0.5})\). FOCs give \(\mu \cdot MP_L = 4,\ \mu \cdot MP_K = 9\), so \(MP_L/MP_K = K/L = 4/9 \Rightarrow K = 4L/9\). Top 11 Mistakes to Avoid

1

MRS \(\ne\) MU. Always the ratio: \(\text{MRS} = MU_1/MU_2\). Don’t write \(MU_1\) and call it MRS.

2

Midpoint elasticity is 1116. In 2316 use the derivative: \(\varepsilon = (dQ/dP)(P/Q)\). Different formula, same concept.

3

Wrong Lagrangian sign. \(\mathcal{L} = U + \lambda(I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2)\) — minus inside, plus on \(\lambda\). Be consistent across all FOCs.

4

Tangency fails for perfect substitutes. MRS is constant, so \(\text{MRS} = p_1/p_2\) either holds everywhere or nowhere. Use \(MU/p\) comparison — spend all on the higher one.

5

Sign of the IE term in Slutsky. The Slutsky term is \(-x \cdot \partial x/\partial I\). For a normal good (\(\partial x/\partial I > 0\)), this is negative — IE reinforces SE.

6

SE is always non-positive. Hicksian (compensated) demand slopes down for own price — no exceptions. Giffen arises from the IE flipping, not the SE.

7

Cobb-Douglas own-price elasticity = \(-1\). Because \(x_i^* = \alpha_i I / p_i\). Income elasticity = \(+1\). These are useful to memorize.

8

Isoquants \(\ne\) indifference curves. Isoquants live in \((K, L)\) space; the slope is \(-\text{MRTS}\), not \(-\text{MRS}\).

9

Returns to scale \(\ne\) diminishing MP. Test RTS by computing \(f(\alpha K, \alpha L)\), NOT by signing the second derivative.

10

\(\lambda\) vs \(\mu\) have different units. \(\lambda\) (utility max) is utils per dollar; \(\mu\) (cost min) is dollars per unit of output. Don’t reuse one in the other’s problem.

11

MC = ATC only at the minimum. Otherwise MC \(\ne\) ATC. MC is the derivative of TC; ATC is the average.

Day-of-Test Game Plan

1. Scan your formula sheet first.

30 seconds to remind yourself what you have written down — so you don’t re-derive things you brought.

2. Identify the problem type before solving.

Lagrangian? Slutsky? Cost-min? Comparative statics? Naming the technique is half the work — the algebra follows.

3. Write the FOCs cleanly.

For Lagrangians, write all three FOCs in a row, then divide the first two to eliminate \(\lambda\). Partial credit lives in clean FOCs even when arithmetic goes wrong.

4. Cobb-Douglas shortcut: use it.

If you see \(U = x_1^\alpha x_2^{1-\alpha}\) or \(f = K^\alpha L^\beta\), jump straight to \(x_i^* = \alpha_i I/p_i\) or use the MRTS shortcut. No need to redo the Lagrangian every time.

5. Check units & signs.

SE \(\leq 0\). \(\lambda > 0\) for binding constraints. Elasticities of demand are negative. MRS positive. Easy points lost on missed signs.

Mindset

2316 rewards structural reasoning: write the Lagrangian, take the FOCs, interpret \(\lambda\) (utility max) or \(\mu\) (cost min), and read off the demand or cost function. The mechanics are mechanical once the structure is right.
