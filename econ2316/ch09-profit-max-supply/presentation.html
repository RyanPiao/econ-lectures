From the Cost Function to the Output Choice

Ch 8 — What's cheapest?

  • Cost-min given output: \(\min wL + rK\) s.t. \(f \geq q\)
  • Cost function \(c(w, r, q)\)
  • Marginal cost \(MC = \partial c/\partial q\)
  • Output \(q\) was taken as given

Ch 9 — How much to make?

  • Add an output price \(p\); the firm now chooses \(q\)
  • Profit-max: \(\max_q\; pq - c(w, r, q)\)
  • FOC \(\Rightarrow p = MC\); supply \(=\) inverse-MC
  • When to operate vs shut down

The cost function from Ch 8 is the input to today's problem. Cost minimization solved "cheapest way to make \(q\)" at every \(q\); profit maximization picks the best \(q\). The headline result \(p = MC\) is the envelope-theorem identity from Ch 8 (\(MC = \mu\)), now viewed from the output-choice side.

The Five Industry Characteristics

Commit these to memory — you will apply them four times across the market-structures tour.

1. Number of firms. A few giants? Hundreds of small shops? One sole provider?

2. Nature of the product. Homogeneous (perfect substitutes) or differentiated (each its own thing)?

3. Barriers to entry. Enter tomorrow with modest capital — or need patents, billions, licenses, network effects?

4. Control over price. Accept whatever the market sets (price-taker) or post your own (price-maker)?

5. Long-run economic profit. Does free entry compete profit to zero, or do barriers sustain it?

Based on these five differences, every market falls into one of four general structures — a spectrum of market power running from pure price-takers to pure price-makers. We meet all four next →

These five characteristics are the analytical engine of the market-structures tour. Number 4 (price control) is really a consequence of 1–3, but it’s the behavior we observe. Number 5 is the long-run prediction. Everything downstream is an application of this list.

Conditions 3 & 4 — Free Entry, Price-Takers

Free entry & exit: a firm that spots profit can enter; a firm losing money can leave. This is what drives the long-run zero-profit result later in the chapter.

Price taker: a firm that must accept the market price as given. It chooses its quantity; it cannot influence the price.

A price-taker is not weak — she is rational. Charge above market → lose every customer. Charge below → light money on fire.

You met “price taker” loosely in Principles; here we make it precise. Condition 4 follows from 1–3: with many sellers of an identical good, no one can deviate from the going price. Free entry/exit is the engine of Ch 10.

The Going Price — About $10–20 a Head

Check any shop on Maps — they all cluster at $10–20 per person. Nobody’s an outlier. That’s the price the market hands Alex.

The market price is an OUTCOME, not a choice — Google listings show every nearby shop landing in the same $10–20/person band. Sets up the 3 deviation scenarios that follow: what happens if one shop tries to break from the pack.

Charge Too High ($15) → Lose Everyone

Price your slice at $15 while the block sits at $10, and customers just walk to the next shop. You sell almost nothing.

Deviation 1: above the market. With an identical product and many rivals, demand for the high-priced shop collapses to ~0 — the flat (perfectly elastic) firm demand curve in action. This is WHY a competitive firm can't price above market.

Charge Too Low ($5) → Bleed Money

Drop to $5 and you’ll sell — but you torch margin you never needed to lose. The market would have paid $10.

Deviation 2: below the market. Underpricing is pointless — she can already sell all she wants at $10 (she’s a tiny share of the market), so cutting price just throws away revenue per slice. There’s no strategic reason to undercut when you face flat demand.

Match the Market ($10) → You Sell

Price at $10, like everyone else, and you sell all you can make at the going rate. The only rational move.

The equilibrium behavior: match the going price and sell freely. Not weakness — it’s the optimal response to flat demand. Every shop reasons the same way, which is exactly why the single price emerges and holds.

Therefore: a Price-Taker

Above the market ($15) → lose every customer to the 49 rivals.

Below the market ($5) → sell, but bleed margin for no reason.

At the market ($10) → sell all you can make. The only move.

Alex can’t set the price — she can only take it. That’s a price-taker (condition 4), and it’s why her firm demand curve is flat.

Synthesis of the three scenarios → the definition of a price-taker, and the bridge to the firm’s flat (perfectly elastic) demand curve from §10.2. The only viable price is the market price; deviating up loses all customers, deviating down loses money. This is condition 4 made airtight, and it motivates P = MR for the MR=MC section ahead.

The Firm’s Demand Curve Is Flat

Market demand slopes down. But the demand facing one firm is a horizontal line at the market price. How can both be true?

Because the firm is a tiny share. If Alex raises her price, she loses all 49 rivals’ worth of customers. If she lowers it, she can sell everything she makes without moving the market.

Marginal revenue (MR) = revenue from one more unit. For a price-taker, every unit sells at the same price, so…

P = MR

The single most important equation in the chapter. Everything builds on it.

§9.2

The Profit-Maximization Problem

\(\max_q\; \pi(q) = pq - c(w, r, q)\)

The Firm Chooses Output to Maximize Economic Profit

\[ \pi(q) = \underbrace{p \cdot q}_{\text{revenue}} - \underbrace{c(w, r, q)}_{\text{cost (Ch 8)}} \]

It picks output, not price

A price-taking firm is small relative to its market: its own production doesn't move \(p\). It takes \(p\), \(w\), and \(r\) all as given.

It reuses Ch 8's cost function

\(c(w,r,q)\) already solved the cheapest input mix at every \(q\). Profit-max just picks the best \(q\) given that.

Two equivalent statements — single-variable vs the joint problem over inputs:

\(\displaystyle \max_q\; pq - c(w,r,q)\)

\(\displaystyle \max_{q,K,L}\; pq - wL - rK \;\text{ s.t. }\; q \leq f(K,L)\)

The chapter's central optimization. Two features to stress: (1) the firm picks output, not price — that's the price-taking assumption; (2) the cost function from Ch 8 has already solved the interior cost-min at every output, so the full problem factors into "cost-min then output-choice." The two formulations are identical because at any \(q\) the firm chooses \((K,L)\) to minimize \(wL+rK\) — exactly Ch 8's problem — so the joint problem collapses to the single-variable form. We work with the single-variable form. Economic profit (not accounting profit) nets out the opportunity cost of owner-supplied inputs — the Ch 8 "Costs That Matter" point.

A perfectly competitive firm faces a horizontal demand curve at the market price: it can sell any quantity at \(p\), but charge a penny more and it loses every customer to identical rivals. That flat demand curve is the geometric content of price taking. Today we make it formal: \(p = MC\), the supply curve, and the shutdown threshold.

A caution on language: we don't claim Alex writes down a Lagrangian. Competitive pressure — entry, exit, owners demanding returns — produces an outcome that looks like he computed the maximum. The theory describes the equilibrium of a process, not the cognition of a manager.

Two teaching moves. (1) The "recover everything" logic: profit-max is a single-variable problem in \(q\); once \(q^*\) is pinned down, conditional input demands, cost, revenue, and profit all follow mechanically. (2) The 1116 bridge: students saw the horizontal-demand-curve picture in Principles §10.2 — verified against source. Today we convert that picture into the FOC. The closing caution heads off the standard objection ("real firms don't do calculus") — the claim is about equilibrium behavior under competitive selection, not literal optimization by managers.

§9.3

The First-Order Condition: \(p = MC\)

Produce up to where the next unit's revenue equals its cost

Set the Derivative of Profit to Zero

Maximize \(\pi(q) = pq - c(w,r,q)\). Differentiate in \(q\):

\[ \frac{d\pi}{dq} = p - \frac{dc}{dq} = p - MC(q) = 0 \]

\(p = MC(q^*)\)

The intuition is "bake one more?"

  • If \(p > MC\): the next unit earns \(p\), costs less than \(p\). Expand.
  • If \(p < MC\): the next unit costs more than it earns. Cut back.
  • Only at \(p = MC(q^*)\) is there no marginal incentive to move. Optimum.

For a price taker, \(p\) is the marginal revenue of one more unit (every unit sells at \(p\)). So \(p = MC\) equates marginal revenue and marginal cost — the producer-side twin of the consumer's equimarginal rule \(MU_i/p_i = \lambda\) from Ch 5.

The headline equation of producer theory. Derivation is one line of single-variable calculus. The "bake one more pizza?" framing is the whole content: produce the next unit iff its marginal cost is at or below the price you'll get for it. Emphasize the parallel to Ch 5's consumer equimarginal condition — same logic of equating marginal benefit and marginal cost at the optimum. For a price taker, marginal revenue is constant and equal to \(p\); Ch 11 breaks this (\(MR < p\) for a monopolist) and that single change drives everything about market power.

Second-Order Condition: \(MC\) Must Slope Up

Check it's a maximum, not a minimum:

\[ \frac{d^2\pi}{dq^2} = -MC'(q^*) < 0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; MC'(q^*) > 0 \]

The firm operates on the upward-sloping part of the \(MC\) curve. Short-run cost functions with diminishing returns to the variable input give rising \(MC\) everywhere, so the SOC holds automatically.

Heads Up — a classic trap

The SOC is "\(MC\) slopes up at \(q^*\)" — the slope \(MC'(q^*) > 0\). It is not "\(MC > 0\)," the level. Almost every cost function has \(MC > 0\) everywhere; the rising-\(MC\) condition is what rules out the falling-\(MC\) side where profit is minimized.

Calculus Corner 9.1. \(\pi(q)=pq-c(w,r,q)\Rightarrow \pi'(q)=p-MC(q)\). FOC: \(p=MC(q^*)\). \(\pi''(q)=-MC'(q^*)\), so the SOC for a max is \(MC'(q^*)>0\). \(p\) is marginal revenue (price-taking); \(MC\) is marginal cost; the FOC equates the two.

The SOC and its most-flagged student error. The condition is on the slope of \(MC\), not its level — a point worth dwelling on because students reflexively write "\(MC>0\)." Geometrically, if \(MC\) slopes down at the FOC point, profit is locally minimized: the firm wants to run away from \(q^*\) in both directions. Realistic short-run cost functions (diminishing returns to labor) give upward-sloping \(MC\), so the SOC is satisfied in every Walkthrough today. CalculusCorner 9.1 in the box keeps the calculus in the sidebar per the book's voice-protection rule.

§9.4

Supply = the Inverse of Marginal Cost

\(q^*(p) = MC^{-1}(p)\) · above the shutdown price

The Supply Curve Is the Rising \(MC\) Curve

The FOC \(MC(q^*) = p\) is one equation in \(p\) and \(q^*\). Solve it for every \(p\) and you trace a function:

\[ q^*(p) = \text{the } q \text{ solving } MC(q) = p \]

Heads Up

Supply isn't a separate mysterious object. It's literally the rising part of the \(MC\) curve, with axes flipped (price up, quantity across). Below min AVC the firm produces zero — supply "lives on the price axis."

The chapter's key structural result: the supply curve is the inverse-MC curve above the shutdown price. There's nothing to "derive from scratch" — read the marginal-cost curve right-to-left, put price on the vertical axis, and you have supply. The figure makes the shutdown convention visible: the curve starts at min AVC = \$55.60/kWh (= materials cost \(m\) for this cost function) and is vertical (zero output) for any price below that. The locked point \(p=\$84 \to q^*=50.9\) sits on the curve. This object aggregates (horizontal sum) into market supply in Ch 10.

Closed-Form Cobb-Douglas Supply & Its Elasticity

Calculus Corner 9.2. Differentiating the FOC: \(dq^*/dp = 1/MC'(q^*) > 0\) — supply slopes up because \(MC\) slopes up. For the gigafactory SR cost function \(SMC(q) = 0.011967\,(q/13.929)^{2/3} + m\):

\[ q_s^*(p) = 13.929\left(\frac{p - m}{0.011967}\right)^{1.5}\ \text{for } p > m,\qquad q_s^*(p)=0 \text{ otherwise} \]

Supply elasticity

\(\dfrac{d\ln q_s^*}{d\ln p} = 1.5 \cdot \dfrac{p}{p-m}\)

Leading \(1.5 = \beta/(1-\beta)\) is set by the production exponent; \(p/(p-m)\) amplifies through the thin value-added margin. At baseline: 4.43.

Three consequences

  • Market supply (Ch 10) = horizontal sum of firms' \(MC\) curves.
  • Anything shifting \(MC\) (wage, capital, technology) shifts supply.
  • Supply elasticity is set by the technology: \(\beta/(1-\beta)\) under Cobb-Douglas with no materials.
CalculusCorner 9.2 — the closed-form supply curve and its elasticity. Two structural facts worth emphasizing: (1) the supply elasticity has a clean decomposition — \(\beta/(1-\beta)=1.5\) is pure technology, and the \(p/(p-m)\) factor blows it up because output responds to the value-added margin \(p-m\), not to \(p\) directly; at \((p,m)=(0.084,0.0556)\) the margin is thin, so elasticity is a large 4.43. Hotelling's Lemma — Recover Supply by Differentiating Profit

Define the profit function \(\pi(p, w, r) = \max_q\,[pq - c(w, r, q)]\). Then:

\[ \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial p} = q^*(p,w,r), \qquad \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial w} = -L^*(p,w,r), \qquad \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial r} = -K^*(p,w,r) \]

(consumer, Ch 6)

(cost, Ch 8)

Heads Up — mind the signs

\(\partial\pi/\partial p = +q^*\) (a higher price raises profit by \(q^*\)). But \(\partial\pi/\partial w\) and \(\partial\pi/\partial r\) are negative — input prices are costs, so the minus signs are in the lemma.

Hotelling's lemma — the producer-side companion to Roy's identity (Ch 6) and Shephard's lemma (Ch 8). All three are the envelope theorem: differentiate a value function with respect to a parameter and the choice-variable response terms cancel by the FOC. Differentiate maximized profit w.r.t. the output price → recover optimal output; w.r.t. an input price → recover MINUS optimal input use. The sign HeadsUp is the most common error: students memorize "derivative of value function = the thing" and drop the minus signs on input prices. Named for Harold Hotelling (1932 JPE); Samuelson's Foundations made the parallels explicit.

Poll #1 — The Firm Is Losing Money. Operate?

At its profit-max output \(q^*\), a firm finds \(\pi^* < 0\) — it's making a loss. Capital \(\bar K\) is installed and the fixed cost \(r\bar K\) is already sunk. Should it keep operating in the short run?

A) No — any loss means shut down immediately
B) Operate iff \(p \geq \min AVC\)
C) Operate iff \(p \geq \min ATC\)
D) Operate iff revenue \(\geq\) fixed cost

Press 1 for a 60-sec timer. Down arrow reveals the answer. The trap is C (the LR exit rule) and A (ignoring sunk cost). The deep point: in the SR the fixed cost is sunk and cancels from the comparison, so the operate condition is \(p \geq \min AVC\), not \(\min ATC\). A firm can rationally operate at a loss. Answer: B — operate iff \(p \geq \min AVC\)

Compare operating profit to the shutdown payoff. The sunk \(r\bar K\) appears on both sides and cancels:

\( pq^* - VC(q^*) - r\bar K \;\geq\; -r\bar K \;\Longleftrightarrow\; pq^* \geq VC(q^*) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; p \geq AVC \)

Traps: A ignores that the fixed cost is sunk — a loss can still beat shutting down. C is the long-run exit rule (every cost avoidable). D compares revenue to fixed cost; the right comparison is revenue vs variable cost.

§9.5

Shutdown and Exit

The asymmetry of sunk vs avoidable fixed costs

Short-Run Shutdown vs Long-Run Exit

Short run — \(\bar K\) sunk

Fixed cost \(r\bar K\) is paid whether or not you produce, so it cancels. Compare revenue to variable cost:

\(pq^* - VC \geq -r\bar K\)

Operate iff \(p \geq \min AVC\)

Long run — \(K\) adjustable

Nothing is sunk: sell equipment, walk away from leases, leave the industry. Every cost is avoidable, so compare to total cost:

\(pq^* \geq c_l(q^*)\)

Operate iff \(p \geq \min ATC\)

The asymmetry. SR uses min AVC (the fixed cost is sunk, so operating at a loss can beat not operating); LR uses min ATC (exit avoids every cost). The wedge between the two thresholds is exactly the avoidable fixed cost.

The shutdown/exit derivation, condensed. The single algebraic move that does all the work: in the SR comparison the sunk \(r\bar K\) appears on both sides and cancels, collapsing the operate condition to \(p \geq AVC\). Since SMC crosses AVC at AVC's minimum (same algebra as MC crossing AC from Ch 8 §8.5), the threshold is min AVC. In the LR nothing is sunk, so the threshold is min ATC. The wedge between them is the avoidable fixed cost — that's the entire SR-vs-LR distinction made operational. This is CalculusCorner 9.4 in the manuscript.

Quasi-Rents: the Contribution to Recovering Sunk Cost

Quasi-rents \(= pq - VC\). Positive quasi-rents mean operating beats shutting down — even when profit is negative.

Heads Up — quasi-rents \(\neq\) profit

Quasi-rents \(= R - VC\). Profit \(= R - VC - FC\). They differ by the fixed cost. A firm can have positive quasi-rents and negative profit — operating, losing money, and rationally choosing to. Shutdown uses quasi-rents; exit uses profit.

Recall from Principles — 1116 §10.3

Alex's three zones as price fell \(\$8 \to \$7 \to \$6.50\): positive profit, operate-at-a-loss, shut down. We now write them as \(p \geq \min AVC\) (SR) and \(p < \min ATC\) (LR exit).

Quasi-rents are the chapter's most-confused concept, so the HeadsUp earns its place. The stacked-bar figure makes it concrete at the gigafactory optimum: revenue \$4278M; subtract variable cost (\$868M K+L plus \$2832M materials = \$3700M) to get quasi-rents \$579M; that \$579M then covers the sunk fixed cost \$520M and leaves \$59M profit. Operating-vs-shutdown turns on quasi-rents \(\geq 0\) (here hugely positive); exit turns on profit. The 1116 §10.3 callback (verified against source) reminds students they've seen the three zones graphically with Alex.

(2) At \(q=0\): pay the sunk fixed cost, produce nothing. Loss \(= -r\bar K = -0.52\) B\$/yr \(= -\$520\)M/yr.

(3) Check \(q = 10\)

\(VC = 0.058 + 0.556 = 0.614\); revenue \(= 0.334\). Profit \(= 0.334 - 0.614 - 0.52 = -0.800\) B\$/yr.

\(-\$800\)M is worse than the \(-\$520\)M shutdown loss. Any positive \(q\) widens the loss. Shutdown is optimal.

Five step-reveals. The decisive comparison: \(p_{\text{shock}}=0.0334 < m=0.0556\), so each unit is underwater on materials before any labor cost — there's no positive output that helps. Three Real 2024 Decisions — SR Shutdown vs LR Exit

Walgreens (Oct 2024): closes ~1,200 stores, keeps ~7,500 open despite a \$3B loss. Survivors sit in \(AVC < P < ATC\) — revenue covers variable cost (operate today), not full ATC (wouldn't reopen). The 1,200 had fallen below AVC.

Spirit Airlines (Nov 2024): Chapter 11 at the firm level, but SR shutdown played out route by route — with jet fuel \(+95\%\) to \$4.88/gal, ticket revenue fell below per-flight AVC on 14 cancelled airports.

Northvolt (Nov 2024): textbook LR exit. Production cost \(\sim\$130\)/kWh against a \$84/kWh China-frontier price: \(p < ATC\) sustained even at full capacity. Operations weren't value-creating at any output.

The chapter's real-world payoff — three 2024 bankruptcies, each illustrating a different cell of the framework. Walgreens: the surviving stores ARE the \$75 regime from the previous slide (operate at a loss, positive quasi-rents, negative profit); the closures had crossed below AVC. Spirit: SR shutdown lives at the route level (variable: fuel, crew, landing fees; sunk: multi-year aircraft leases) while the firm-level Chapter 11 was a debt/refinancing story — keep them separate. Northvolt: the cleanest LR exit — \$130 cost vs \$84 price means losing money at every output, full capacity or not. The contrast Spirit-vs-Northvolt is sharp: Spirit's operations were value-creating but its balance sheet wasn't; Northvolt's operations themselves weren't.

§9.7 The Long-Run Knife-Edge under CRS

Under our locked Cobb-Douglas with \(\alpha + \beta = 1\) (constant returns), Ch 8 showed LR cost is linear in \(q\). So LR marginal cost equals LR average cost — a constant:

\(LRMC + m = 0.0272 + 0.0556 = \mathbf{0.0828}\)
\(= \$82.81\)/kWh, constant in \(q\)

The LR profit-max FOC \(p = LRMC + m\) is one equation in zero unknowns:

\(p > \$82.81\): every unit earns a margin \(\Rightarrow q \to \infty\).

\(p < \$82.81\): every unit loses \(\Rightarrow\) exit, \(q = 0\).

\(p = \$82.81\) exactly: any \(q \geq 0\) gives zero profit — output indeterminate.

This degeneracy is the bridge to Ch 10. With CRS, individual LR firm supply isn't pinned down. Free entry drives the equilibrium price down to \(LRMC + m\), where every firm earns zero economic profit and the number of firms becomes the equilibrating variable. The locked \(p = \$84\) sits just \$1.20 above the knife-edge — no coincidence.

The chapter's most subtle point and its cliffhanger into Ch 10. Under CRS Cobb-Douglas, LR cost is linear, so LRMC = LRAC = a constant (\$82.81/kWh including materials). The LR profit-max problem is therefore degenerate: above the knife-edge the firm wants infinite output, below it wants zero, and exactly at it any output gives zero profit. Individual LR firm supply is undefined — which is precisely why we need industry-level analysis. Free entry in Ch 10 resolves the indeterminacy by pinning the equilibrium price at the knife-edge and making the number of firms the adjusting variable. The locked \$84 sitting \$1.20 above \$82.81 is deliberate: the 2025 China-frontier battery market is near-competitive, exactly the free-entry world Ch 10 models.

§9.8 When Does \(p = MC\) Hold? — The Empirical Caveat

Theory and Data

With market power, \(p > MC\). The markup \(\mu = p/MC\); De Loecker-Eeckhout-Unger (2020) find US average markups rose from 1.21 (1980) to 1.61 (2014). For the average US firm, \(p = MC\) is increasingly a benchmark, not a description.

  • \(p = MC\) is the baseline for commodity-like industries (wheat, generic drugs, LFP cells).
  • Empirical supply elasticities run far below Cobb-Douglas: tight oil \(\approx 0.076\) (Newell-Prest), vs our 4.43.
  • Frictions, capital lumpiness, and thresholds eat the textbook number.
§9.8 — the honest caveat that keeps the chapter from overclaiming. Two empirical pressures on \(p=MC\): (1) markups. DLU 2020's production-approach finds average US markups rose 1.21→1.61 over 1980-2014 — though Bond et al. 2021 argue the method overstates when revenue proxies for output; both readings are actively contested. The pedagogical line: \(p=MC\) is the price-taking limiting case (good for wheat, generic pharma, 2025 LFP cells), and Chs 11-13 add the markup wedge for smartphones, platforms, patented drugs. (2) Elasticities: real supply elasticities (tight oil 0.076) run a factor of ~60 below the Cobb-Douglas prediction because of adjustment frictions, capital lumpiness, and threshold effects. The BNEF figure puts the locked \$84 in its real-world context: the decade-long collapse toward the China-frontier price.
