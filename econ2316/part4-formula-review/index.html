Part IV in Formulas

The calculus behind Chapters 10–12 — market structure and the markup, in plain English

Dr. Richeng Piao • Northeastern University

First: one wedge organizes all three chapters

Chapters 10–12 are a single story about the gap between price and marginal cost. Four ideas carry it.

\( p = MC \)

The competitive benchmark. When the firm is one price-taker among many, the markup is zero — price sits right on marginal cost.

\( MR = P + q\,P'(q) \)

Marginal revenue with market power. Selling one more unit drops the price on every unit, so \(MR \lt P\). This wedge is where the markup comes from.

\( L = \dfrac{P - MC}{P} \)

The Lerner index. The markup as a fraction of price — the single number that measures market power. It equals \(-1/\varepsilon_D\) at the optimum.

\( \varepsilon_D = \dfrac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\dfrac{P}{Q} \)

Demand elasticity. The elasticity from Chapter 3 returns as the control dial on the markup: elastic demand \(\Rightarrow\) small markup.

Perfect competition sets the markup to zero (Ch 10); monopoly derives it (Ch 11); price discrimination fires it once per segment (Ch 12).

Market structure 1 of 3

Perfect Competition = The Zero-Markup Benchmark

Price-takers, market supply as a sum, and free entry that competes profit to zero.

Price-taking, and market supply as a sum

Perfect competition

Ch 10 • §10.2–10.3
\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial q_i} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; p = MC \]

Each firm is a price-taker

\[ Q^{s}(p) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i^{*}(p) \]

Market supply — horizontal sum

In plain English

A competitive firm is too small to move the price, so its own output doesn’t change \(p\): it just applies Chapter 9’s \(p = MC\). Add up every firm’s supply curve horizontally and you get market supply — with identical firms, simply \(N\cdot q^{*}(p)\).

The market elasticity of supply equals the firm’s: scaling by \(N\) cancels in the ratio.

Free entry pins the long-run price

Long-run equilibrium

Ch 10 • §10.5
\[ \pi^{LR} = 0 \iff p^{LR} = \min LRATC, \qquad N^{*} = \frac{Q^{d}(p^{LR})}{q^{*}} \]

In plain English

Positive profit pulls new firms in; losses push firms out. Entry stops only when the marginal entrant earns exactly zero economic profit, which forces price to the bottom of the long-run average cost curve. Under constant returns the firm’s own output is indeterminate, so the number of firms \(N^{*}\) becomes the equilibrating variable — demand at the entry price, divided by plant size.

This is the gigafactory punchline: 50 plants at \$82.81/kWh comes straight out of \(N^{*} = Q^{d}(p^{LR})/q^{*}\).

Why the benchmark matters: surplus is maximized

First Welfare Theorem

Ch 10 • §10.7
\[ TS(Q) = \int_0^{Q}\!\bigl[\,P^{d}(q) - P^{s}(q)\,\bigr]\,dq \quad\text{is maximized at}\quad P^{d} = P^{s} = MC \]

In plain English

Total surplus is the integrated gap between what buyers will pay and what sellers must receive. It is largest exactly where the competitive market clears — every trade whose benefit exceeds its cost happens, and no others. That is the efficiency yardstick the next two chapters will fail.

Hold onto this line \(P = MC\). Every deadweight-loss triangle ahead is measured as a departure from it.

Market structure 2 of 3

Monopoly = Marginal Revenue Below Price

One seller, a downward-sloping demand curve, and a markup with a formula.

Marginal revenue — and the inverse-elasticity form

Marginal revenue

Ch 11 • §11.3
\[ MR(q) = P(q) + q\,P'(q) = P\left(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_D}\right) \]

linear demand \(P = a - bq \;\Rightarrow\; MR = a - 2bq\)

In plain English

Because a monopolist faces the whole market, selling one more unit means cutting the price on every prior unit — the \(q\,P'(q)\) term. So marginal revenue sits strictly below price. Factor the price out and demand elasticity appears: as \(|\varepsilon_D|\to\infty\), \(MR\to P\) and we’re back to the price-taker.

For linear demand the MR line has twice the slope of demand — they meet only at the intercept, never at the optimum. Don’t set \(MR = P\).

The monopolist’s rule: \(MR = MC\)

Profit maximization

Ch 11 • §11.4
\[ \frac{d\pi}{dq} = MR(q) - MC(q) = 0 \iff MR(q^{*}) = MC(q^{*}) \]

In plain English

Same move as always — set profit’s slope to zero. The only change from the competitive firm is that marginal revenue is no longer the price. Where the price-taker wrote \(p = MC\), the monopolist writes \(MR = MC\), and then reads the price up off the demand curve.

For linear demand and constant MC, the monopolist produces exactly half the competitive quantity — the source of the deadweight-loss triangle.

The Lerner index — markup as inverse elasticity

Lerner index & DWL

Ch 11 • §11.5–11.6
\[ \frac{P - MC}{P} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_D} \]

Markup = inverse elasticity

\[ DWL = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(P_M - MC)(Q_C - Q_M) \]

The welfare cost

In plain English

Substitute the FOC into the markup and it collapses to a single elegant fact: the profit-maximizing markup is exactly the negative inverse of demand elasticity. Elastic buyers \((\varepsilon_D = -10)\) get a 10% markup; inelastic buyers \((\varepsilon_D = -2)\) get 50%. The output the monopolist withholds is the deadweight-loss triangle between demand and MC.

\(\varepsilon_D = -3.33\) predicts Spotify’s \(L = 0.30\) almost exactly — the abstract rule reading a real price tag.

Market structure 3 of 3

Price Discrimination = The Markup, Segment by Segment

Charge different buyers different prices — and convert their surplus into profit.

First-degree PD — the upper bound

Perfect price discrimination

Ch 12 • §12.2
\[ \text{charge each buyer } P = \text{their reservation} \;\Rightarrow\; Q^{*} = Q_C, \quad DWL = 0, \quad CS = 0 \]

In plain English

If the firm knew every buyer’s exact willingness to pay and could charge it, it would sell to everyone whose value exceeds MC — so output reaches the competitive level and the deadweight loss vanishes. But every dollar of surplus goes to the firm: consumer surplus is zero. Efficient, and maximally unequal.

This is the ceiling. Real firms can’t reach it because they don’t know each buyer’s reservation — the reason second- and third-degree PD exist.

Third-degree PD — the Lerner rule, per segment

Group pricing

Ch 12 • §12.4
\[ L_i = \frac{P_i - MC}{P_i} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{D,i}} \qquad i = 1,\ldots,K \]

In plain English

Split buyers into observable groups you can keep apart (student vs. adult, weekday vs. weekend). Each group’s FOC is independent, so the Chapter 11 inverse-elasticity rule simply fires once per segment. The headline is unchanged: the more elastic group gets the smaller markup. Students pay less because they walk away more easily.

Ch 11 had one segment; Ch 12 has \(K\). Same rule, run in parallel — Spotify’s five prices are five copies of \(-1/\varepsilon_{D,i}\).

The two-part tariff — a fee to grab the surplus

Two-part tariff

Ch 12 • §12.5
\[ T(q) = A + p\,q, \qquad \text{set } p = MC \ \text{and} \ A = CS(p) \]

In plain English

Charge a fixed entry fee \(A\) plus a per-unit price \(p\) — a membership plus a per-use charge. With identical buyers the firm’s best move is to price the units at marginal cost (so the buyer consumes the efficient quantity) and set the fee to scoop up the entire consumer surplus. Output is efficient; the surplus is the firm’s.

Same goal as first-degree PD — capture consumer surplus — reached through a menu instead of buyer-by-buyer mind-reading.

One wedge, three chapters.

Perfect competition

Price-takers set \(p = MC\); free entry drives \(p^{LR} = \min LRATC\); surplus is maximized. Markup \(= 0\).

Marginal revenue

Market power puts \(MR = P(1 + 1/\varepsilon_D) \lt P\). The FOC becomes \(MR = MC\).

The Lerner index

The markup has a formula: \((P - MC)/P = -1/\varepsilon_D\). Deadweight loss is the withheld-output triangle.

Price discrimination

The same markup, per segment: \(L_i = -1/\varepsilon_{D,i}\). Converts consumer surplus into producer surplus.

Every result here is a statement about \(P - MC\) — zero under competition, \(-P/\varepsilon_D\) under monopoly, one per segment under discrimination. Chapter 13 asks what happens to that wedge when a few firms interact strategically.
