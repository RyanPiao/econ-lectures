Part III in Formulas

The calculus behind Chapters 7–9 — the theory of the firm, one equation at a time

Dr. Richeng Piao • Northeastern University

First: the producer side is the consumer side, mirrored

Every tool in Chapters 7–9 is one you already met in consumer theory — with the labels swapped. Here is the Rosetta Stone.

\( f(K, L) \)

The production function. The producer’s \(U(x_1,x_2)\). It maps inputs to output; its level sets are isoquants — the isoquant is the indifference curve of the firm.

\( MP_L = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial L} \)

A marginal product. Just a partial derivative — the producer’s marginal utility. Change one input, freeze the rest, read the extra output.

\( \text{MRTS} = \dfrac{MP_L}{MP_K} \)

The slope of an isoquant. The producer’s MRS — how much capital one worker replaces at constant output.

\( \mu \ \ (\text{the multiplier}) \)

A shadow price. The producer’s \(\lambda\). In the cost problem it turns out to be marginal cost — the dollars the next unit of output adds.

Three central optimizations: pick the recipe (Ch 7), minimize its cost (Ch 8), pick how much to make (Ch 9). Same Lagrangian, three times.

Firm tool 1 of 4

The Production Function & Its Slopes

Two partial derivatives, and the ratio between them, do almost all the work.

Marginal products — the two slopes of the hill

Production function

Ch 7 • §7.3
\[ MP_K = \frac{\partial f(K,L)}{\partial K} \]

Marginal product of capital

\[ MP_L = \frac{\partial f(K,L)}{\partial L} \]

Marginal product of labor

In plain English

Think of \(f\) as a hill whose height is output. A marginal product is a slope of that hill in one direction — the extra output from one more worker, holding capital still. Both are positive but eventually falling: \(\partial^2 f/\partial L^2 \lt 0\) is the calculus of “too many baristas, one espresso machine.”

For Cobb–Douglas \(Q = AK^\alpha L^\beta\), the products collapse to a share of the average: \(MP_L = \beta\,Q/L\). Handy — we reuse it constantly.

MRTS — the slope of the isoquant

Production function

Ch 7 • §7.3
\[ \text{MRTS}_{LK} = -\frac{dK}{dL}\bigg|_{f = \bar Q} = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K} \]

In plain English

Stand on an isoquant — a single output level — and ask: give me one more worker; how much capital can I give up and still make the same output? That trade rate is the MRTS, and it is just the ratio of the two marginal products. It is the producer twin of the consumer’s \(\text{MRS} = MU_1/MU_2\).

The minus sign only keeps the number positive as the isoquant slopes down — the same cosmetic move as MRS.

Firm tool 2 of 4

Cost Minimization = The Lagrangian, Again

Cheapest way to hit a target output — the same tangency you solved in Chapter 5.

The problem, and the tangency it produces

Cost minimization

Ch 8 • §8.2
\[ \min_{K,L \,\ge\, 0} \; wL + rK \quad \text{subject to} \quad f(K,L) \ge q \]

tangency \(\;\Rightarrow\;\) \( \text{MRTS}_{LK} = \dfrac{w}{r} \)

In plain English

Slide the cheapest possible isocost line (slope \(-w/r\)) down until it just touches the isoquant. At that kiss the slopes match: the rate at which the firm can swap inputs (MRTS) equals the rate at which the market prices the swap \((w/r)\). Exactly the consumer’s budget-tangent-to-indifference picture, with a level of output instead of a level of utility.

Equimarginal per dollar — and what \(\mu\) really is

Cost minimization

Ch 8 • §8.3
\[ \frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r} \]

Equimarginal principle

\[ \mu = \frac{\partial c}{\partial q} = MC \]

The multiplier is marginal cost

In plain English

Rearrange the Lagrangian’s first-order conditions and the price ratio drops out, leaving marginal product per dollar equalized across inputs — the last dollar spent on labor buys as much output as the last dollar on capital. And the multiplier \(\mu\), like the consumer’s \(\lambda\), carries an economic meaning: here it is the shadow price of the output constraint — the marginal cost of one more unit.

Consumer: \(\lambda = MU_i/p_i\), the marginal utility of income. Producer: \(1/\mu = MP_i/\text{price}\), the marginal output per dollar. Same machinery, dual reading.

Firm tool 3 of 4

The Cost Function and Its Derivatives

Solve the tangency for every \(q\) and you get \(c(w,r,q)\) — then differentiate.

Marginal and average cost — two derivatives of \(c\)

Cost function

Ch 8 • §8.5
\[ MC = \frac{\partial c(w,r,q)}{\partial q} \]

Marginal cost — the next unit

\[ AC = \frac{c(w,r,q)}{q} \]

Average cost — per unit so far

In plain English

Once cost minimization hands you the cost function, its \(q\)-derivative is marginal cost (what the next unit costs) and \(c/q\) is average cost (what a unit costs on average). By the envelope theorem, \(MC\) equals the multiplier \(\mu\) — the indirect effects through \(K^{*}, L^{*}\) cancel.

Why MC always cuts AC at its minimum

Cost function

Ch 8 • §8.5
\[ \frac{d}{dq}\!\left(\frac{c}{q}\right) = \frac{MC - AC}{q} \]

In plain English

AC is your cumulative GPA; MC is the grade on the next class. A next grade above your GPA pulls it up \((MC \gt AC \Rightarrow AC\) rising\()\); one below pulls it down. They can only be equal at the turning point — so MC crosses AC exactly at the bottom of the U.

This identity is algebra, not a picture — it holds for every cost function. Under constant returns \(c\) is linear, \(MC = AC\) constant, and the U flattens away.

Shephard’s lemma — a free derivative

Cost function • envelope

Ch 8 • §8.6
\[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial w} = L^{*}(w,r,q) \]

Conditional labor demand

\[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial r} = K^{*}(w,r,q) \]

Conditional capital demand

In plain English

Differentiate the cost function with respect to an input price and you recover exactly how much of that input the firm buys. It is the producer’s Shephard’s lemma — the same envelope trick that turned the expenditure function into Hicksian demand in Chapter 6.

Firm tool 4 of 4

Profit Maximization = One Rule, \(p = MC\)

With a cost function in hand, the last choice — how much to make — is a single derivative.

The rule, and the fine print

Profit maximization

Ch 9 • §9.3

First-order condition

\[ p = MC(q^{*}) \]

Second-order condition

\[ MC'(q^{*}) \gt 0 \]

In plain English

Profit is \(\pi = pq - c(q)\). Set its slope to zero: since a price-taker’s marginal revenue is the price, the rule is \(p = MC\) — bake the next pizza exactly when it earns at least what it costs. The SOC just says the MC curve must be rising at \(q^{*}\); a maximum sits on the upward-sloping side, not merely where \(MC \gt 0\).

This is Chapter 5’s optimization template, output edition: \(\max f\) becomes \(\max \pi\), and the FOC is a slope set to zero.

The supply curve is the marginal-cost curve, flipped

Firm supply

Ch 9 • §9.4
\[ q^{*}(p) = MC^{-1}(p) \quad \text{for } p \ge AVC, \qquad q^{*}(p) = 0 \ \text{otherwise} \]

In plain English

Supply isn’t a separate object to derive from scratch — it is literally the rising part of the MC curve read with price on the vertical axis. Solve \(MC(q) = p\) for \(q\) and you have the firm’s supply. This is the hinge into Chapter 10: market supply is the horizontal sum of these curves.

Under Cobb–Douglas the short-run supply elasticity comes out to \(\beta/(1-\beta)\) — set entirely by the production exponents.

Shutdown — the asymmetry of fixed cost

Shutdown & exit

Ch 9 • §9.5
\[ \text{operate} \iff p\,q^{*} \ge VC(q^{*}) \iff p \ge AVC(q^{*}) \]

In plain English

A running firm already paid its fixed cost — that money is gone whether it produces or not. So the only question for this period is whether revenue covers variable cost: keep the lights on iff price clears average variable cost. Below that line, producing loses more than shutting does.

Short run vs long run: Spirit Airlines kept flying below ATC (a shutdown call); Northvolt exited entirely (a long-run \(p \lt LRATC\) call). Same inequality, different horizon.

Four moves. That is the whole theory of the firm.

Production & its slopes

Partial derivatives of \(f\) — marginal products, and their ratio the MRTS. \(MP_L = \partial f/\partial L\).

Cost minimization

The Lagrangian tangency: swap inputs until \(\text{MRTS} = w/r\), i.e. \(MP_L/w = MP_K/r\).

The cost function

Differentiate \(c(w,r,q)\): \(MC = \partial c/\partial q\), and MC cuts AC at its minimum.

Profit maximization

Set profit’s slope to zero: \(p = MC\), rising MC, supply \(= MC^{-1}\) above \(AVC\).

Every producer result ahead — free-entry equilibrium, the monopolist’s markup, oligopoly — starts from this cost function and this \(p = MC\) logic. The market structure changes; the firm’s calculus does not.
