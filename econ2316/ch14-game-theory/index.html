Prisoner’s Dilemma: Attack or Peace?

With both superpowers now armed, some US leaders pushed for a preemptive strike. Attack first — or keep the peace?

The chilling real-world payoff matrix. Navy Secretary Francis Matthews publicly floated the US becoming “aggressors for peace” (a first strike), and von Neumann — co-founder of game theory — argued the cold logic of preemption. Each side: attack or stay peaceful. Mutual attack = catastrophe; mutual peace = best; but each fears the other strikes first. That's the prisoner's dilemma with nuclear stakes — and exactly what RAND was hired to model.

Game Theory in 1950

1944 — Theory of Games and Economic Behavior: von Neumann & Morgenstern launch game theory (2-player zero-sum games, the minimax method).

1950 — Nash Equilibrium: John Nash proves every finite game has a set of strategies where no player can gain by changing alone.

1950 — Prisoner’s Dilemma: Merrill Flood & Melvin Dresher at RAND formulate it — two rational players, each in self-interest, end up worse off than if they’d cooperated.

The academic timeline. 1944: von Neumann & Morgenstern's book founds the field but solves only zero-sum games. 1950: two leaps — Nash's equilibrium (works for ANY game, the heart of §14.2) and Flood & Dresher's prisoner's dilemma at RAND (the think tank modeling nuclear strategy). The Cold War literally funded the math we're about to learn.

A Normal-Form Game: Three Ingredients

PLAYERS

\(\{1, \ldots, n\}\)

kicker & keeper

STRATEGIES

\(S_i\)

left or right

PAYOFFS

\(u_i(s_1,\ldots,s_n)\)

goal or save

Choices are simultaneous in the formal sense — no player observes another's before choosing. The payoff matrix records every profile: row = player 1, column = player 2, cell = \((u_1, u_2)\).

A strategy strictly dominates when it pays more for every rival choice: \(u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i', s_{-i})\) for all \(s_{-i}\). Never play a strictly dominated strategy — the sharpest prediction game theory offers.

Game Theory Assumptions

Mutual interdependence: each player recognizes that outcomes depend on others’ actions.

Rationality: players choose strategies to maximize expected payoffs, given beliefs about others.

Clearly defined preferences: players have specific preferences to maximize (or minimize) payoffs.

Three assumptions that make game theory work. Interdependence (the whole point — my best move depends on yours), rationality (maximize expected payoff given beliefs), well-defined preferences (you can rank outcomes). When these hold, we can solve for equilibrium.

§14.2

Pure-Strategy Nash Equilibrium

Mutual best responses — the equilibrium concept that works even beyond dominance

Nash Equilibrium: Mutual Best Responses

Best response. Hold the others’ choices \(s_{-i}\) fixed; player \(i\) maximises their own payoff against it.

\(BR_i(s_{-i}) \;=\; \arg\max_{s_i \in S_i} u_i(s_i, s_{-i})\)

A correspondence — ties allow several best responses.

Nash equilibrium: everyone plays a best response at once. Nobody gains by deviating unilaterally.

\(s_i^{\ast} \in BR_i(s_{-i}^{\ast}) \iff u_i(s_i^{\ast}, s_{-i}^{\ast}) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^{\ast})\ \ \forall\, s_i,\ \forall\, i\)

You already found one: against Alpha your best response is Alpha, and theirs is the same — two best responses pointing at each other. Dominance is the special case, best against every \(s_{-i}\); Nash needs it only against \(s_{-i}^{\ast}\) — which is why Nash finds equilibria where dominance finds none.

And here is the sting. (Alpha, Alpha) = (B−, B−) is perfectly stable — yet (Beta, Beta) = (B+, B+) beats it for both of you. A Nash equilibrium is where nobody can improve alone, not where everybody does best. That gap is the Prisoner’s Dilemma — and we solve the canonical version next.

Teach the two boxes in order — best response FIRST, then Nash as mutual best responses. The term has been used loosely since the “three ways to model behavior” slide and this is where it gets its definition, so do not rush it. Three things to stress. (1) The whole content of BR is the phrase "holding the others fixed" — you optimise against a conjecture, not against the world. (2) It is a CORRESPONDENCE: with ties, several strategies can be best replies, which is exactly why mixed-strategy equilibria will work later (§14.3 — at the equilibrium mix the player is indifferent, so every mix is a best response). (3) Nash equilibrium is then a fixed point: each player is in their own best-response set given the others. Also flag the relationship the key-result states: a dominant strategy is a best response to EVERY rival choice, so dominance implies Nash but not conversely. Nash \(\neq\) Efficient, and Nash \(\neq\) Unique

Heads Up 14.1 — two traps

Trap 1: “Nash is rational play, so it must be the best joint outcome.” The PD refutes this: \((D,D)\) is the unique NE and Pareto-dominated by \((C,C)\). Nash is about individual rationality, not group welfare.

Trap 2: “A game has one equilibrium.” BoS has two pure NE. The model alone doesn't pick one — selecting requires a focal point (Schelling 1960): custom, communication, or a salient feature of the payoffs.

Recall from Principles — 1116 §13.3

1116 defined Nash equilibrium informally: “each player is choosing the best response to the others — no one wants to deviate unilaterally,” after John Forbes Nash Jr. (Nobel 1994).

The 1116 example was the gas-station price war: \((\text{Low}, \text{Low})\) is Nash because neither station gains by going High while the rival stays Low. Same definition — we now write best response as a formal correspondence, and iterated elimination gives us a way to search.

Why Nash still predicts: firms need not communicate or even know game theory — by watching rivals and adjusting, markets converge on Nash through trial and error. It is self-enforcing — stable, just not efficient. (Empirical IO often takes the symmetric equilibrium as the focal point for homogeneous industries.)

The conceptual guardrails for the rest of the chapter. Trap 1 (efficiency): the PD is the standing counterexample — unique NE, Pareto-dominated. Trap 2 (uniqueness): BoS has two pure NE plus a mixed one (next section), and selecting among them needs a focal point (Schelling). The Prisoner’s Dilemma Is Everywhere

The same trap shows up across economics — rational individuals, collectively worse off:

Joint project — each member is tempted to avoid effort (free-ride).

Price competition — firms undercut each other, lowering profits.

Advertising war — firms keep spending to compete → a suboptimal Nash.

We build predictions on strict dominance because deleting is then safe — a strictly dominated strategy is never a best response to any belief, no Nash equilibrium is ever lost, and the deletion order cannot change the answer. Say why that matters — IESDS on strictly dominated strategies never deletes a Nash equilibrium and is order-independent; neither is true for weak dominance, where you can eliminate a strategy that was part of a perfectly good equilibrium and where two different deletion orders can leave two different answers. That is exactly why the chapter builds on strict dominance and treats weak dominance as a caution. Iterated Elimination — Putting the Pieces Together

A new word — the mirror of dominant. A strategy \(s_i\) is strictly dominated if some other strategy beats it against every rival choice: \(u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i, s_{-i})\) for all \(s_{-i}\). It is never a best response — so a rational player never plays it.

1. Delete every strictly dominated strategy.

2. You now face a smaller game — and everyone knows it.

3. A strategy that survived may be dominated now. Look again. Repeat until nothing more can go.

That loop is iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS). It is a sound search: it never deletes a Nash equilibrium, so every NE survives — and if exactly one profile survives, it must be the unique NE. With strict dominance the order you delete in never changes the answer.

Escaping the Dilemma — What Actually Works

Nick talked his way to Split — but that was a bluff that happened to land. Can cooperation be made rational rather than lucky? Promises alone don’t move a Nash equilibrium; only a change to payoffs, information, or the number of rounds does. Four mechanisms, each with a real case.

1. Commitment, not conversation

Talk is cheap; a commitment that is costly to reverse is not. Firms post price-match guarantees and most-favored-nation clauses — publicly binding themselves to match any cut. That makes undercutting pointless, so nobody starts. A tool sold as pro-consumer that quietly stabilises high prices.

Nick’s “I will Steal” gambit was the same move on live TV.

2. Change the payoffs — reward the first defector

The DOJ Corporate Leniency Policy (and the EU equivalent) gives full immunity to the first cartel member who comes forward — and nothing to the second. Antitrust enforcers deliberately build a prisoner’s dilemma inside the cartel: now defecting on your co-conspirators is dominant.

Same logic as the original police interrogation — aimed at price-fixers.

3. Play it again — and be able to see cheating

Repetition only disciplines players who can detect defection. That is why cartels invest in monitoring, and why the same transparency can cut both ways: the price app in the gas-station opener made undercutting instant and killed the truce.

OPEC+ (seen earlier); formalised as grim trigger in §14.5.

4. An outside rule that binds everyone at once

A regulator can impose the cooperative cell no player can reach alone — the 1971 cigarette ad ban did exactly that — two slides on we work the numbers. But the rule has to bind: the Paris Agreement sets emissions targets each country writes for itself, with no enforcement, so the free-riding dilemma survives the treaty.

A rule without teeth leaves the payoffs — and the equilibrium — untouched.

§14.5

Repeated Games

When the game never ends, cooperation can survive — firms compete again and again, and engineer ways to escape the trap.

One-Shot vs. Repeated

A one-shot game is played once: two gas stations on the same corner set price a single time, and the dominant-strategy logic forces (Low, Low).

Repeated game: the same players interact over and over — so strategies can depend on past behavior, and cooperation impossible in one shot becomes sustainable.

Real firms compete quarter after quarter. Today’s price affects tomorrow’s relationship — and that hands each player two instruments a one-shot game never gives them:

  Carrot — reward

Hold your price up today and I hold mine up tomorrow, and every quarter after. Cooperation buys future cooperation.

  Stick — punish

Undercut me and I retaliate — a price war, an ad war, or silence forever after. One good quarter buys a long bad stretch.

A one-shot game gives you neither. There is no “later” in which to hand out a reward or withhold one, so the only thing disciplining you is the payoff in front of you — and in a prisoner’s dilemma that always says defect. Repetition changes no payoff in the matrix; it hands each player a carrot and a stick. Whether they are strong enough to hold the truce depends on how much you value tomorrow — the discount factor \(\delta\), next.

The pivot of the section, framed as carrot and stick. The question to put to the room before advancing: "what exactly does repetition give you that one shot does not?" The answer is not a better payoff — the matrix is untouched — it is two INSTRUMENTS. A carrot you can extend or withdraw (my future cooperation) and a stick you can swing (retaliation). In a one-shot game neither exists, because there is no later. Make the asymmetry concrete with the gas stations: cutting price today wins you one day of traffic and costs you the truce, and the truce was worth more. Two things to flag for what follows. (1) The stick has to be CREDIBLE — an empty threat disciplines nobody, which is what grim trigger formalises. (2) The stick also has to be usable, meaning you must be able to SEE the defection; that is the monitoring requirement the OPEC+ and Coke/Pepsi cases turn on. Then hand off to the discount factor: carrot and stick are only worth something if tomorrow is worth something, which is exactly what delta measures.

Repeated Games in the Wild

TSMC & Apple — mutual lock-in

Apple designs chips it can’t build; TSMC builds them in $15–20B fabs it can’t repurpose. TSMC’s no-compete pledge (it never sells products competing with customers) is a credible commitment. Each product cycle is another round.

Uber & Lyft — tacit duopoly

Two platforms, near-identical prices and surge patterns. Neither needs to call the other: each knows a cut will be matched within hours, making a price war pointless. Repeated interaction sustains it.

Coke & Pepsi — taking turns

Two brands chasing the same switchers. Deep-discount in the same week and they merely split them; alternate weeks and each gets the category to itself. Lal (1990) shows alternating promotions is a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the infinitely repeated game — implicit collusion that needs no phone call.

Gasmi, Laffont & Vuong (1992), on 1968–86 Coke/Pepsi data: tacit collusion in advertising is supported; on price it is not.

Reputation as a commitment device. A firm known for keeping its word has more to lose from defecting — so partners trust it, enabling more cooperation. Investment banks, law firms, and builders invest heavily in reputational signals for exactly this reason. (Online platforms scale reputation to millions of users.)

Four real mechanisms: credible commitment (TSMC), tacit coordination via repetition (Uber/Lyft), alternating promotions (Coke/Pepsi), and reputation. On the cola card — this is the one students can verify at a supermarket: the two colas are rarely on deep discount in the same week. Rajiv Lal, "Price Promotions: Limiting Competitive Encroachment," Marketing Science 9(3), 1990, 247-262, shows that with enough brand-switchers and a high enough discount factor, ALTERNATING promotions is a perfect Nash equilibrium of the infinite-horizon game, and reads as implicit collusion between the national brands (he motivates it with the beverage industry, and with defending shelf space against store brands). The empirical companion is Gasmi, Laffont and Vuong, "Econometric Analysis of Collusive Behavior in a Soft-Drink Market," Journal of Economics and Management Strategy 1(2), 1992, 277-311: on 1968-86 data they cannot reject collusion, and the sharpest finding is that it shows up in ADVERTISING rather than price. Worth saying out loud, because it is the opposite of what students expect: the colas compete hardest on the thing customers see (price) and cooperate on the thing they do not (ad budgets). Careful with the claim — the alternating pattern is a documented regularity and the mechanism is well modelled, but do not assert a specific overlap statistic you have not checked. All three are the folk theorem at work in the actual economy.

Two Kinds of Repetition — Finite vs. Infinite

In a repeated game the same players play the same stage game again and again, and each of them knows the whole history of what the other has done. That history is what makes a carrot or a stick possible at all — but how the game ends decides whether they work.

  Finitely repeated

A known final round. Both players can see the end coming.

A supplier contract with 12 months left to run; a store announcing a closing-down sale; a partner’s last quarter before retirement.

The stick loses its bite in the last round — and then, by backward induction, in every round before it.

  Infinitely repeated

No final round — or nobody knows which one it is.

Two gas stations on the same corner; Coke and Pepsi; OPEC+ members expecting to still be pumping next decade.

There is no last round to reason back from, so the threat of punishment stays alive.

What matters is not literally “forever” — it is whether there is a known last round. A known ending gives you somewhere to start reasoning backwards from; an unknown ending does not. That single difference decides whether cooperation can survive, and we prove it both ways in the next few slides.

New taxonomy slide sitting between "one-shot vs repeated" and the folk theorem, so students have the vocabulary before the theorem uses it. Three things to land. (1) Knowing the HISTORY is what makes carrot and stick available — without observable history there is nothing to reward or punish, which is the monitoring point again. (2) The finite/infinite split is about a KNOWN final round, not about literal immortality; say this explicitly, because students hear "infinitely repeated" and object that no firm lasts forever. The objection dissolves once they see interpretation 2. (3) The equivalence is the good bit: playing forever with discount factor delta is mathematically identical to playing on while a coin keeps coming up heads with probability p, and with an interest rate the two combine as delta = p/(1+r). That means delta is doing double duty — it measures patience AND survival probability. Two payoffs from this: a firm near bankruptcy behaves exactly like an impatient firm (which is the recession story from the folk theorem slide), and a "temporary" relationship with no announced end date is strategically infinite. Do not derive anything here; the unravelling proof and the grim-trigger threshold both come shortly.

Last slide the argument started at the end and walked backwards. Take the end away and that argument has nowhere to stand — there is no final round whose outcome is obvious, so nothing to reason back from.

Discounting the Future

Future payoffs are worth less today — impatience plus positive interest rates. Discount each period ahead by a factor \(\delta\), with \(0 < \delta < 1\):

\(\$1\text{ next period} \;=\; \$\delta\text{ today}\)

\(\delta\) is doing two jobs at once. If the game also survives each round with probability \(p\), and money earns interest \(r\), the two fold into a single number: \(\delta = \dfrac{p}{1+r}\). So \(\delta\) measures patience and survival together — a firm that expects to fold soon behaves exactly like an impatient one.

\(\delta\) high (e.g. 0.8) — patient

A future dollar is worth ~80¢ now. The players value tomorrow almost as much as today → a permanent punishment stings → cooperation easier.

\(\delta\) low (e.g. 0.2) — impatient

A future dollar is worth ~20¢ now (high interest, near bankruptcy). The future barely counts → grab the defection payoff now.

Patience is the whole story: the higher \(\delta\), the more a forever-punishment outweighs a one-time gain. Keep \(\delta = 0.8\) and \(\delta = 0.2\) in mind — in two slides we find the exact cutoff between them.

Formalizes the discount factor introduced informally on the Folk Theorem slide. delta is the per-period weight on next period's payoff; equivalently delta = 1/(1+r) for interest rate r, so low delta <-> high r <-> impatience. The two cases pre-load the intuition for the threshold: the whole derivation is just asking how patient players must be for the discounted punishment to deter a one-shot defection.

The Threshold: How Patient Is Patient Enough?

  Refresher: the geometric series

Adding infinitely many terms does not give infinity, as long as each one is a fixed fraction of the last. For \(0 \le x < 1\):

\(x + x^2 + x^3 + \cdots \;=\; \dfrac{x}{1-x}\)

Why: call the sum \(S\). Multiply by \(x\): \(xS = x^2 + x^3 + \cdots\), which is \(S\) missing its first term. So \(S - xS = x\), giving \(S = \dfrac{x}{1-x}\).

Try \(x = \tfrac12\): \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots = \dfrac{0.5}{0.5} = 1\).
Halving forever adds up to exactly 1 — remember that number.

\(3 + 3\delta + 3\delta^2 + \cdots \;>\; 5 + \delta + \delta^2 + \cdots\)

\(2\,(\delta + \delta^2 + \delta^3 + \cdots) \;>\; 2\)

Take a 3 and a \(\delta\)-tail off both sides: you gain 2 today, you lose 2 in every later period.

\(\dfrac{\delta}{1-\delta} \;>\; 1\)

The Folk Theorem & the Discount Factor

Folk theorem: in an infinitely repeated game, players patient enough can sustain cooperation as an equilibrium. “I won’t torch a 20-year relationship for one quarter’s gain.”

Why truces break in recessions. A downturn hits \(\delta = p/(1+r)\) from both sides: survival \(p\) falls and the cost of capital \(r\) rises. \(\delta\) slips under the threshold and the cooperative equilibrium stops existing — without anyone’s preferences having changed.

Read the picture: the polygon is every payoff pair the two players could feasibly split. The shaded part is what beats each player’s punishment payoff — individually rational. As \(\delta \to 1\), every point in the shaded region is sustainable as a subgame-perfect equilibrium.

Read it as a warning, not just a result. You proved one point — \(\delta > \tfrac{1}{2}\) sustains cooperation in that matrix. The folk theorem says that as \(\delta \to 1\) almost every individually-rational payoff becomes sustainable too. So the model predicts a region, not a point: repetition tells you cooperation can hold, never that it will. What actually picks one outcome — monitoring, focal points, communication, or an algorithm’s learning rule — is §14.6.

The arithmetic is the whole intuition: a one-shot gain against a permanent loss. Patience (high delta) tips the calculation toward cooperation; recessions make firms impatient, so price wars cluster in downturns. Use Figure 14.7 to make "the folk theorem" concrete rather than a slogan. Walk it in three steps. (1) The polygon is the FEASIBLE set — every payoff pair the players could achieve between them. (2) The shaded subset is the INDIVIDUALLY RATIONAL region: pairs giving each player at least what they could guarantee themselves by taking their punishment, so nobody would rather walk away. (3) The theorem: as delta approaches 1, ANY point in that shaded region is a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the repeated game. That is why it is called "folk" — it says almost anything can be an equilibrium, which is a warning as much as a result: the repeated-game model predicts cooperation is POSSIBLE, not that it will happen. The figure returns in §14.6 on algorithmic collusion, where Q-learning agents find their way into that region with no communication at all. Tacit collusion: price coordination with no explicit agreement — through signaling, parallel behavior, or the shared expectation that rivals will match.

Price-match guarantees — that wall of logos from the start of class — sound pro-consumer, but they remove the temptation to defect. If Best Buy will instantly match any cut, Target gains nothing by undercutting. The “Low” row disappears; both hold High.

Sustaining Collusion: The Turbine Triopoly

1950s turbine market — a triopoly selling to electric utilities: GE 60% · Westinghouse 30% · Allis-Chalmers 10%.

• Days 1–17: Westinghouse & A-C bid high → GE wins

• Days 18–25: GE & A-C bid high → Westinghouse wins

A real (and almost comic) case of sustaining collusion. GE, Westinghouse, and Allis-Chalmers rigged turbine bids in the 1950s with a “phases of the moon” formula — the date set whose turn it was to submit the low bid, rotating wins to preserve market shares. It was self-policing (everyone saw the bids) and durable, until investigators spotted the lunar pattern. This is the bid-rigging behind the 1961 convictions on the next slide.

The High Cost of Price Fixing

In 1961, twenty-nine electrical-equipment makers were convicted of criminal bid-rigging and price-fixing — the “Great Electrical Conspiracy.” For the first time, executives were jailed.

Treble damages let victims recover 3× the overcharge, so the civil suits dwarfed the fines — nearly 2,000 followed. By 1965, GE had paid ~$225M, Allis-Chalmers $45M, and Westinghouse had set aside $110M.

Price-fixing is a per se criminal violation of the Sherman Act — the surest way a cartel’s “attempt to sustain” ends.

The payoff of the turbine scheme: the 1961 convictions were the largest criminal antitrust case of the era and the first to jail executives. Treble damages meant ~$225M from GE alone plus nearly 2,000 civil suits. Detection is catastrophic - exactly the risk the DOJ leniency program exploits by rewarding the first firm to defect.

Factors Enhancing Cartel Survival

• Few members with similar goals — easier to agree and to police.

• High barriers to entry — no outsiders to undercut the cartel price.

• Similar cost structures — everyone wants the same price, so the deal holds.

• Legal protection — government cover (a sovereign cartel like OPEC) removes antitrust risk.

• Undifferentiated products — little to compete on but price, so a truce tempts.

• The catch — instability: each member still has an incentive to cheat by exceeding its quota.

The more of these boxes a market checks, the longer collusion lasts — which is why these conditions explain the price stability seen across many oligopolies. But the cheating incentive never disappears, so even disciplined cartels (ADM, OPEC) eventually crack.

The textbook list of what makes collusion sustainable: few firms, similar costs and products, entry barriers, and legal cover. The flip side is the ever-present incentive to cheat by exceeding quota - the instability at the heart of every cartel. The lysine and turbine cases just shown put these in action: few firms, homogeneous product, and (for the turbine triopoly) easy mutual monitoring.

Algorithmic Collusion: When the Code Coordinates

Algorithmic collusion: pricing algorithms independently learn to sustain above-competitive prices — cartel outcomes with no human agreement.

Hub & spoke. One vendor (the hub) sells the same pricing tool to rival firms (the spokes). RealPage faced a DOJ suit for allegedly coordinating rent increases across thousands of landlords.

The Sherman Act needs a “meeting of the minds.” But if algorithms reach cartel prices with no email, no meeting — should the test be whether firms communicated, or whether the outcome looks like coordination?

The frontier of antitrust. Project Nessie was a preview; Calvano shows independent algorithms can collude without being told to. The legal standard (agreement vs. outcome) is unsettled.

• Used the prisoner’s dilemma to describe human societies and the evolutionary biology of animal behavior.

• Hosted famous tournaments: experts submit a strategy to play an infinitely* repeated prisoner’s dilemma.

“The contestants ranged from a 10-year-old computer hobbyist to professors of computer science, economics, psychology, mathematics, sociology, political science, and evolutionary biology.”

• Book: The Evolution of Cooperation (1984) — among the most-cited works in all of political science.

* Each round had a 0.00346 probability of ending the game — ensuring, on average, 200 rounds of play.

Axelrod's tournament is the canonical answer to "can cooperation emerge among selfish players?" He invited experts to submit strategies (as programs) for a repeated PD, then played them all against each other. The infinite-horizon trick: a small constant probability of ending each round keeps the future always relevant, so there's no fixed last round to unravel cooperation by backward induction.

The Famous Tournament — The Rules

• Players: floppy disks — all random pairs (💾 vs. 💾).

• Strategies: a program with preprogrammed instructions for each move.

• Payoffs: total points from all rounds.

• Repeated game: ending probability 0.00346 → about 200 rounds.

Goal: win as many points as possible.

Axelrod, R. (1980). Effective Choice in the Prisoner’s Dilemma. The Journal of Conflict Resolution, 24(1), 3–25.

Each entry is a deterministic (or randomized) rule mapping the history of play to the next move — Cooperate or Defect. Standard PD payoffs (Table 1): mutual cooperation 3 each, mutual defection 1 each, and the sucker/temptation 0 and 5. Every strategy meets every other (and a RANDOM player) in a ~200-round match; total points decide the winner.

Result: Tit for Tat Wins

Tit for Tat (Anatol Rapoport’s 4-line program) won with the highest average score (504). The striking pattern: the top 8 strategies are all “nice” — none is ever the first to defect.

The headline result. Tit for Tat — cooperate first, then copy whatever the opponent did last round — beat far more elaborate strategies. Axelrod's four properties of winners: be NICE (never defect first), be RETALIATORY (punish defection immediately), be FORGIVING (return to cooperation once they do), and be CLEAR (simple enough to be read and trusted). Every strategy in the top tier shares the "nice" property — a stunning, hopeful result about how cooperation can survive among self-interested agents.

Trigger Strategies

Tit-for-tat: cooperate first, then do whatever your rival did last period. Cooperate if they did; retaliate if they cut — then forgive when they return.

Grim trigger: cooperate until the first defection — then defect forever. No forgiveness. A harsher but powerful deterrent.

Axelrod’s tournaments (1980s): game-theory strategies competed at repeated prisoner’s dilemma. Tit-for-tat won — because it was cooperative, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear. It never beats any single rival, yet outperforms everything over the long run.

Tit-for-tat (Rapoport) is the canonical result: nice, provocable, forgiving, transparent. The threat of immediate retaliation is what sustains present cooperation.

The Evolution of Cooperation: 4 Keys to Winning

Axelrod found that the top strategies all shared four properties:

Be nice. Start cooperative — never defect first.

Don’t be a pushover. Retaliate — answer a defection with a defection.

Be forgiving. Return to cooperation once they stop defecting.

Don’t be too clever. Stay clear and predictable so others can trust you.

The four properties of the winners — Tit for Tat embodies all four. Nice (never first to defect), retaliatory (Joss/Graaskamp's probes get punished), forgiving (unlike Friedman's grim trigger), and clear (5 lines anyone can read and trust). The surprise: niceness and forgiveness — not cleverness or aggression — win the long game.

Noise in the Real World

On 26 Sept 1983, a Soviet early-warning system falsely reported five US missiles inbound — pure noise. Officer Stanislav Petrov waited for corroboration instead of retaliating. Had he “played Tit-for-Tat,” the accident could have triggered all-out nuclear war.

The noisy-PD made terrifyingly real. The system malfunctioned (noise); a strict retaliation rule would have answered a phantom defection with a real one. Petrov's refusal to immediately retaliate — forgiveness/skepticism in the face of possible noise — is exactly the correction the noisy tournaments reward. He likely prevented nuclear war.

Cooperation in Nature: Stickleback Fish

• When a predator appears, one or more sticklebacks approach to inspect it — dangerous, but it reveals if the predator is hungry (flee) or not (stay).

• Milinski (1987) showed they play Tit-for-Tat: two fish advance together,

– Cooperate = move forward

– Defect = hang back

• A fish advances only as far as its partner does — reciprocity, fin by fin.

Milinski, M. (1987). “Tit for Tat in sticklebacks and the evolution of cooperation,” Nature.

Milinski's classic field experiment: predator-inspection in sticklebacks behaves exactly like an iterated PD with a tit-for-tat rule. A fish that's "let down" (partner hangs back) hangs back too next time. Real reciprocity in animals.

Cooperation in Nature: Vampire Bats

• Vampire bats starve after ~60 hours without a blood meal — and a bad night is common.

• They feed each other by regurgitating blood: a big benefit to the recipient, a real cost to the giver.

• Wilkinson (1984): bats that received food are more likely to give it later —

– Reciprocal altruism: sharing tracks past help and familiarity.

– They recognize genuine need (near-starvation) vs. a temporary dip.

Wilkinson, G. (1984). “Reciprocal Altruism in Bats and Other Mammals,” Ethology and Sociobiology 9(2–4): 85–100.

Wilkinson's evidence: blood-sharing is reciprocal (you help those who helped you), concentrated among familiar roost-mates, and triggered by genuine need. It's a living iterated-PD: defect (refuse to share) and you won't be fed when your turn comes.

Weaponizing Credibility: Throw Away the Wheel

In Chicken your best move depends entirely on what your opponent believes you’ll do. So don’t try to out-nerve them — change what they believe is possible.

The binding mechanism

Visibly, irreversibly destroy your own ability to swerve — rip out the steering wheel and toss it out the window, where your rival can see it go.

Why it works

A rational rival who sees you can’t swerve must swerve themselves. You’ve transferred 100% of the burden of avoidance onto them.

Fewer options can make you stronger. A credible, irreversible commitment collapses Chicken’s two equilibria down to the one you want. Burning bridges, a union strike fund, a “no-negotiation” law, a doomsday device — all work the same way. (Schelling, The Strategy of Conflict, 1960.)

Schelling’s famous paradox: in Chicken, limiting your own freedom of action is a source of power. If you can credibly commit to Straight — by making swerving impossible or unthinkable — the rational opponent is forced to swerve. The catch is credibility: the commitment must be visible and irreversible, or it’s just a bluff. We just saw it in Footloose — Ren wins because his stuck foot makes swerving impossible. This bridges into repeated games, where reputation does the committing for you. Real examples: a government law forbidding ransom payments, a firm building so much capacity that flooding the market is obviously its best response, a union with a strike fund big enough to outlast management.

Mixed Strategies

As highlighted by the previous slides, sometimes there is no pure-strategy equilibrium. A player’s best option may be to choose actions randomly from a set of pure strategies.

Mixed strategy

A strategy in which the player randomizes his or her actions — a probability distribution over the available pure strategies.

Our example: a penalty kick

  • The kicker can kick the ball left or right.
  • The goalie chooses which way to dive.
  • Same side → the goal is saved; otherwise it is scored.

Neither player can commit to one side — the rival would simply learn it and best-respond. The only stable play is to randomize, keeping the other guessing.

Defines the mixed strategy after the payoff matrix has shown there is no pure NE. The penalty kick is the running example; the next slide computes the goalie's best response to different kicker mixes.

The computational version (3 worked cases). Goalie's payoff = save probability = chance of matching the kicker's side. If kicker plays Left with prob p, E[dive Left]=p and E[dive Right]=1−p. 50% → 0.50 vs 0.50, indifferent → equilibrium (kicker unexploitable). 80% → goalie dives left (0.80>0.20) → kicker should switch to right. 20% → goalie dives right → kicker should switch to left. Only the 50/50 mix leaves the goalie indifferent so the kicker has no profitable deviation — the indifference condition made concrete. Asymmetric payoffs shift the equilibrium mix.

Does Anyone Actually Mix? — Penalty Kicks

Theory and Data 14.1 — Chiappori-Levitt-Groseclose (2002, AER)

A penalty kick is stylized matching pennies: kicker picks a corner, goalie picks a corner; same corner favors the goalie. The mixed-NE prediction: scoring probability is equal across the kicker's choices (else shift weight).

French + Italian top divisions, 1995–2000: Left and Right scoring rates were 76% vs 76% — statistically indistinguishable, exactly the indifference condition. Replicated in tennis serves (Walker-Wooders 2001) and Spanish soccer (Palacios-Huerta 2003).

Heads Up 14.2 — a pure NE may not exist, but a mixed one always does

Nash's 1950 theorem: every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium (possibly mixed). Can't find a pure NE? Solve the indifference condition for the mixed one.

The counterpoint: Kovash-Levitt (2009) find MLB pitchers and NFL play-callers deviate from minimax — too many fastballs, too few passes. When the action space is large and cognitive load high, players fall into predictable patterns. Mixed-NE holds for penalty kicks and tennis, not for baseball.

The empirical test of mixed strategies. The prediction is subtle but sharp: at the mixed NE, the kicker's success rate must be EQUAL across directions (otherwise reallocate). Chiappori-Levitt-Groseclose find 76% vs 76% — striking confirmation. Heads Up 14.2: Nash existence guarantees a mixed NE in any finite game, so the indifference condition always has a solution. But Kovash-Levitt is the honest counterpoint: in complex action spaces (pitch type/location, play-calling) pros regress to predictability. The differentiator is action-space complexity, not strategic sophistication. Use mixed-NE as a benchmark, not a law.

The Maximin Strategy

Or: what if my opponent is an idiot?

A Nash equilibrium assumes rational play. What if players make systematic errors — not optimizing their own payoffs?

  • A rational player facing less-than-rational opponents may prefer a more conservative strategy.

A maximin strategy minimizes your exposure to loss — choose the strategy with the largest minimum payoff across all of your opponent’s possible choices.

B: Don’t invest B: Invest A’s row min
A: Don’t invest ✓0, 0 −10, 10✓ −10 ← MAX
A: Invest −100, 0 ✓20, 10✓ −100

The Nash equilibrium is (Invest, Invest) — but if B might err, A’s maximin is Don’t invest (worst case −10, vs −100).

Maximin = the conservative answer when you can't trust opponents to play rationally. For each of A's strategies, find the worst-case payoff across B's choices: Don't invest → min(0, −10) = −10; Invest → min(−100, 20) = −100. Maximin picks the largest of those minimums → Don't invest (−10 > −100). Contrast with Nash: the checks show (Invest, Invest) = (20, 10) is the unique Nash equilibrium, but it exposes A to a −100 disaster if B irrationally doesn't invest. A risk-averse / uncertain player hedges with maximin. Sets up that Nash isn't the only solution concept.

§14.4

Sequential Games & Backward Induction

When one player moves first — credible commitment, non-credible threats, and SPNE

The Game Tree & Backward Induction

One player moves first, the other responds. Solve by backward induction — start at the last move and work back. CloseAI can’t choose until it knows how OpenAI will react, so it puts itself in OpenAI’s shoes: “If I enter, will they really fight?”

OpenAI (B, last mover): Accommodate ($5B) > Fight ($3B) → it accommodates.

CloseAI (A, looking ahead): Enter ($3B) > Stay out ($0) → it enters.

OpenAI’s threat to “fight any entrant” is empty — once CloseAI is in, fighting only hurts OpenAI too. A non-credible threat doesn’t deter.

Making the threat credible (Dixit 1980). If the incumbent first sinks a cost — extra capacity, R&D, an exclusive contract — that lowers its post-entry cost, so fighting becomes its best response. Now the threat is real and the SPNE flips to “invest → entrant stays out” — entry deterred. The sunk cost is the commitment device; we formalize it next.

Backward induction reveals the empty/non-credible threat: OpenAI would never actually fight (accommodating $5B beats a price war $3B), so the threat can't stop CloseAI entering. The “put yourself in their shoes” move is the whole technique — reason about the last player first. Credibility is everything in sequential games, which sets up the next slide: can OpenAI make the threat real?

Strategic Investment: Making the Threat Credible

OpenAI’s Startup Fund — investing around its models to raise barriers.

Add a first move: OpenAI invests −$2B. Now winning a price war is cheap, so fighting pays ($5B > $3B) — the threat is credible.

CloseAI looks ahead, expects a fight, and stays out. OpenAI keeps the market: $8B > $5B.

An investment that’s never even used still wins — by changing what the rival believes OpenAI will do. That’s strategic investment / entry deterrence: the same credible-commitment logic as the Dixit game (§14.4), now with capacity on the line.

The commitment thread: in the one-shot tree OpenAI’s threat to fight was empty, so CloseAI entered. Add a prior investment stage and the calculus flips — sinking $2B into capacity makes fighting OpenAI’s best response, so the threat becomes credible and CloseAI is deterred. In equilibrium the capacity is never used in a war; its entire value is changing the rival’s beliefs. Schelling’s commitment idea with money on the line — the same logic as the Dixit entry-deterrence Walkthrough, and it sets up Stackelberg next.

Stackelberg: First-Mover Advantage

Stackelberg competition: a leader commits to output first; the follower observes and responds. By over-committing to a large quantity, the leader forces the follower to produce less — and claims the bigger share.

NVIDIA vs. AMD. NVIDIA holds ~80–92% of AI accelerators; its CUDA ecosystem makes its lead a credible, irreversible commitment. It sets premium prices (leader); AMD (<10%) prices underneath as the value follower — exactly what the model predicts.

First-mover advantage isn’t automatic. It needs a credible, irreversible commitment (Boeing announcing huge 787 runs to deter Airbus) and a follower who responds by pulling back. If the leader can easily reverse course, the follower ignores the bluff.

Stackelberg = the game tree applied to quantity. The commitment must be observable and irreversible — CUDA lock-in and sunk fab investment make NVIDIA's leadership credible. Empty announcements don't move followers. We formalize Stackelberg as the SPNE of the 2-stage game in W14.6.

Trees, Subgames, and Credible Threats

Many interactions are sequential: one firm moves, others observe, then respond (Stackelberg was one). The extensive form is a tree — decision nodes labeled by player, branches for actions, payoffs at the leaves.

A strategy specifies an action at every node where it's your turn — even off-path nodes, because we must evaluate counterfactuals.

A subgame starts at any node and includes everything below it.

A subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE) induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame — including off-path ones. It rules out non-credible threats: actions that wouldn't actually be optimal if that node were reached.

Backward induction finds it: start at the deepest nodes, pick each player's optimal action, fold those payoffs up into the parent, and repeat to the top.

Why SPNE, not just Nash? A plain Nash equilibrium can rest on a threat the threatener would never carry out. SPNE demands the threat be optimal at the node where it would be executed — credibility, built in.

The extensive-form vocabulary: tree, decision nodes, strategies that prescribe actions everywhere (not just on path). Subgame = any node and its descendants. SPNE = Nash in every subgame, which is exactly the requirement that eliminates non-credible threats. Backward induction is the algorithm: solve the last movers first, fold up. The credibility point is the heart of §14.4 — “I'll fight you if you enter” is only believable if fighting is actually optimal post-entry. The next slide makes this concrete with entry deterrence, where a sunk investment is what makes the threat credible.

Chapter 14 — The Equilibrium Ladder

Nash equilibrium (§14.1–14.2). A profile where no one deviates unilaterally. The PD shows it need not be efficient; BoS shows it need not be unique. IESDS is a sound search.

Mixed-strategy NE (§14.3). When no pure NE exists, randomize so the rival is indifferent. Nash (1950): every finite game has one.

SPNE (§14.4). Backward induction on the tree rules out non-credible threats. Stackelberg is the SPNE of the sequential quantity game.

Repeated games (§14.5). Finite horizons unravel; infinite ones sustain cooperation iff \(\delta \geq \frac{T-R}{T-P}\). Cournot cartel: \(\delta^{\ast} = 9/17\).

Folk theorem. For \(\delta\) near \(1\), almost any individually rational payoff is sustainable — selection (focal points, Q-learning) decides which.
