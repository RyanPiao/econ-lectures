§10.2

Four Conditions

What does it take for a market to be "perfectly competitive"?

A Market Is Perfectly Competitive If…

1. Many small firms

No firm is a non-trivial share of output, so no firm's decision moves the price.

2. Homogeneous product

Every firm's output is identical; buyers care only about price, not brand.

3. Free entry & exit

Firms enter or leave without a fixed cost beyond the capital they install or abandon.

4. Perfect information

Everyone knows every price, every cost structure, every quality.

Each is an idealization — none is literally true in any real market. Condition 1 is closest to true for Iowa corn or a single trader on the Chicago Board of Trade; Condition 2 for #2 yellow corn; Condition 3 where capital is portable; Condition 4 where prices are listed and updated.

The precise definition — worth slowing down on, because every assumption we relax in the rest of Part IV traces back to exactly one of these four. Conditions 1–2 give price-taking; condition 3 gives the zero-profit long run; condition 4 underwrites the welfare theorem. The 2025 battery industry approximates them unevenly: condition 1 fails (CATL is 39% of installations, HHI ~2,000), condition 2 partly holds (LFP cells fungible, NMC differentiated), condition 3 holds approximately, condition 4 holds well on price discovery. The point of the next slide: a benchmark can bite even when it holds only approximately.

"Free Entry" With a \$15B Price Tag?

Fair objection: a gigafactory costs billions and takes 2–4 years to build. In what sense is that free entry? Because "free" modifies barriers, not cost — it is a claim about symmetry of opportunity, not about the size of the check.

What free entry actually rules out

  • Legal exclusivity — medallions, utility franchises, spectrum licenses
  • Patents or proprietary tech an entrant can't replicate or license
  • Incumbent-only access to inputs, distribution, or customers

A barrier is something money can't buy past. The \$15B buys capacity — a cost of producing, not a toll for the right to compete. CATL, Stellantis–Samsung, LGES, and BYD each wrote a comparable check in the same 24 months. Nobody needed a battery license.

Where the objection does bite

1. It breaks condition 1, not condition 3. When minimum efficient scale is large relative to the market, only a few firms fit — exactly why CATL holds 39.2% and HHI sits near 2,000. Price-taking is the assumption under strain.

2. Sunk capital makes exit the un-free half. Northvolt's backers recovered almost none of the \$15B. In the model exit is instantaneous; in Skellefteå the plant ran at 5% of capacity for two years before the board pulled the trigger.

Free entry and exit is a claim about the direction capital flows when \(\pi \neq 0\) — not about the speed it moves, and not about the price of a ticket.

This slide exists because the objection is the single most common student pushback on the opener, and it deserves a real answer rather than a hand-wave. Lead with the distinction: free entry is about barriers, not cost — the test is whether a firm willing to pay the same costs faces the same terms. Ask the room for a genuine barrier and steer toward the medallion/patent/license family; the point lands when someone notices that money solves the gigafactory problem but cannot solve the patent problem. Then concede the two things the objection gets right, because both pay off later: the large-MES-breaks-condition-1 point is what makes the HHI ~ 2,000 fact in §10.5 coherent rather than embarrassing, and the sunk-capital point is what explains why the observed industry sits at 30–40 plants instead of the model's 50. Don't let students leave thinking the model was refuted — leave them with "free entry is a statement about direction, and the direction check is exactly what we watch Northvolt and CATL do."

Price-Taking, Made Formal

For an individual firm \(i\), its own output cannot move the price:

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial p}{\partial q_i} = 0\)

This does not mean prices are constant — they move when aggregate supply or demand shifts. It means any single firm's contribution is too small to matter. The firm treats \(p\) as exogenous — exactly what let us set \(p=MC(q^*)\) in Ch 9.

Contrast (Ch 11): a monopolist has \(\partial p/\partial q < 0\), so \(MR = p + (\partial p/\partial q)q < p\). The gap is the markup; under perfect competition the markup is zero.

Heads Up 10.1

"Perfect competition" does not mean "perfect markets." The four conditions describe a limiting case, not a claim that real markets work flawlessly. The model's value is a clean prediction (\(p=\min ATC\), surplus maximized) for any market that comes close enough.

Is the battery industry "close enough"? An empirical question — but §10.5's \(N^*=50\) lands near the 30–40 gigafactories actually operating in 2025, so industry-wide quantity dynamics still track the benchmark despite CATL's 39% share.

The formal content of price-taking: \(\partial p/\partial q_i = 0\). Stress that this is an assumption about market structure, not about the firm being naive — it's the limit of "my output is a vanishing share of the total." The monopoly contrast previews Ch 11 in one line: the entire theory of market power is the single change \(\partial p/\partial q < 0\). Heads Up 10.1 heads off the most common misreading of the phrase "perfect competition." De Loecker-Eeckhout-Unger (2020) found aggregate US markups rose from 21% to 61% (1980–2018) — perfect competition is increasingly not a good description of the whole economy, though specific commodity and cell markets still approximate it.

Electricity Generation Is a Competitive Market

Electricity has two very different sides. The wires are a natural monopoly (Ch 11) — but generation, where the power is actually made, is close to perfect competition.

Generators → one grid → homes. Only the first link is competitive.

Generation — many plants (coal, gas, nuclear, wind, solar) bid into a shared pool. Price-takers. ← today

Transmission — one high-voltage grid; enormous fixed cost → natural monopoly (Ch 11).

Distribution — the local wires to your home; also a regulated monopoly.

On the generation side, dozens of plants sell an identical product (a MWh is a MWh) into the same market, and no single plant moves the wholesale price — \(\partial p / \partial q_i = 0\). The grid operator dispatches plants in merit order (cheapest \(MC\) first) until demand is met, so the wholesale price equals the \(MC\) of the last plant switched on.

The pedagogical point: the SAME industry can be competitive in one segment and a natural monopoly in another. Generation = many price-taking plants selling a homogeneous product (a megawatt-hour) into a wholesale auction; the grid operator (the ISO) stacks them cheapest-MC-first (merit order) and the market clears at the marginal plant's MC. That is exactly the price-taking, homogeneous-product, p=MC world of Ch 10. The wires (transmission + distribution) are the natural-monopoly part — that's Ch 11, not today. Next: read one generator's supply curve straight off its plants' marginal costs.

A Generator's Marginal-Cost Curve Is Its Supply Curve

A generator runs its cheapest unit first. As the wholesale price rises, it brings costlier plants online in merit order:

  • Plant A (coal) — cheapest, ~\$18/MWh; dispatched first.
  • Plants B & C (gas) — costlier (~\$37, \$39/MWh); switched on only once the price clears their \(MC\).
  • Maximum capacity — output can't rise further, so supply turns vertical.

The rising \(MC\) curve (above shutdown) is the firm's short-run supply curve — the §10.3 building block. Sum these step-curves across all generators and you get market supply.

The payoff: a real price-taking firm whose supply curve you read off directly. Each horizontal step is one generating unit; the step height is that unit's marginal cost (fuel + O&M). The firm offers each unit into the market whenever the price covers that unit's MC — so it produces where p = MC, and the step-staircase IS the firm's supply curve above its shutdown price, going vertical at maximum capacity. This is the concrete object §10.3 sums horizontally into the market supply curve.

§10.3

Market Supply: The Horizontal Sum

\(\displaystyle Q^s(p) = \sum_{i=1}^N q_i^*(p)\)

Industry Short-Run Supply Curve

Market (industry) supply is the horizontal sum of every firm's short-run supply curve — at each price, add up the quantities.

Identical firms: one firm's curve, scaled out by \(N\).

Different firms: add the curves left-to-right at each price.

Industry output rises as \(P\) rises for two reasons: (1) each operating firm's \(MC\) slopes up, so it makes more; (2) higher-cost firms cross their shutdown price and switch on.

The §10.3 horizontal-sum, shown two ways. Left: 100 identical firms — the industry curve is the single-firm curve scaled out by 100 along the quantity axis (same shape, same elasticity, by CalculusCorner 10.1). Right: heterogeneous firms (A, B, C, D) with different cost curves — add them horizontally at each price to trace an upward-sloping industry curve. Two engines of the upward slope: the intensive margin (each firm's rising MC) and the extensive margin (higher-cost firms entering as price climbs). Visual companion to the algebra.

Stacking Them All: The Industry Supply Curve

Lay every firm's supply curve end-to-end at each price and you get the industry short-run supply curve — a rising staircase from the cheapest plant's \(MC\) to the most expensive.

It slopes up for the two §10.3 reasons: each firm's \(MC\) rises, and higher-cost plants switch on as \(P\) climbs. Where this industry supply meets demand sets the market price — and every dispatched plant produces at \(p = MC\).

The payoff of §10.3: the industry short-run supply curve = the horizontal sum (merit-order stack) of every firm's MC curve. Read left to right and you climb the dispatch order, cheapest plants first. The two sources of the upward slope are the §10.3 points: the intensive margin (each firm's rising MC) and the extensive margin (higher-cost plants entering). Where this curve crosses market demand sets the wholesale price, and every dispatched plant sits at p = MC. Sets up §10.4: clearing the short-run market.

Add Quantities Across Firms at Each Price

Every firm faces the same price \(p\) (price-taking). What differs is the quantity each supplies. So we add along the quantity axis:

\(Q^s(p) = \sum_{i=1}^N q_i^*(p) \;\xrightarrow{\text{symmetric}}\; N\cdot q^*(p)\)

With symmetric firms, market supply is just \(N\) copies of one firm's curve, scaled horizontally. For our gigafactory cohort: \(N\) times the locked Ch 9 firm supply.

Heads Up 10.2

Sum quantities, not prices. There is no meaning in adding firm \(A\)'s \$4 shutdown price to firm \(B\)'s \$6. The price is the same for everyone; quantities are what differ.

Calculus Corner 10.1 — The Market Elasticity Equals the Firm Elasticity

Symmetric firms, \(Q^s = N q^*(p)\). Compute the market-supply elasticity:

\[ \varepsilon^s_{\text{mkt}} = \frac{p}{Q^s}\frac{dQ^s}{dp} = \frac{p}{N q^*}\,N\frac{dq^*}{dp} = \frac{p}{q^*}\frac{dq^*}{dp} = \varepsilon^s_{\text{firm}} \]

The \(N\) cancels. Multiplying a curve by a constant doesn't change its percentage responsiveness.

For our gigafactory, the firm-supply elasticity is \(1.5\cdot p/(p-m)\). At \(p=\$84\), \(m=\$55.6\):

\(\varepsilon^s = 1.5 \cdot \dfrac{84}{84-55.6} = 1.5 \cdot \dfrac{84}{28.4} \approx 4.44\)

Same number at \(N=1\), \(N=30\), or \(N=50\). The \(1.5\) is pure technology \((\beta/(1-\beta))\); the \(p/(p-m)\) factor blows it up because output responds to the thin value-added margin \(p-m\), not to \(p\) directly.

Calculus Corner 10.1 — kept in the sidebar per the book's voice-protection rule. The result is small but conceptually important: scaling firm supply by \(N\) leaves the elasticity untouched, so the market inherits the firm's responsiveness exactly. The gigafactory number (4.44) reappears from Ch 9's CalculusCorner 9.2. Two factors: \(\beta/(1-\beta)=1.5\) is technology; \(p/(p-m)\) reflects the razor-thin LFP margin (only \$28.40 of value added on an \$84 pack). Thin margins → high supply elasticity. The empirical caveat (real elasticities run far below 4.44) is honest to flag but secondary.

Walkthrough 10.1 — Horizontal Sum with Heterogeneous Shutdowns

Problem. Firm \(A\): \(q_A^*(p)=2(p-4)\) for \(p\geq 4\), else 0. Firm \(B\): \(q_B^*(p)=3(p-6)\) for \(p\geq 6\), else 0. Construct market supply \(Q^s(p)\) on \([0,10]\).

Set up & solve — three regions

\(p\in[0,4)\): no firm operates. \(Q^s = 0\).

\(p\in[4,6)\): only \(A\). \(Q^s = 2(p-4) = 2p-8\).

\(p\in[6,10]\): both. \(Q^s = 2(p-4)+3(p-6) = 5p-26\).

Check the kink at \(p=6\)

From below: \(2(6)-8 = 4\). From above: \(5(6)-26 = 4\). Continuous — no jump.

But the slope jumps from 2 to 5: firm \(B\)'s output is added on once \(p\geq 6\). Market supply kinks at each firm's shutdown threshold.

Takeaway. Heterogeneous markets have piecewise supply: each kink adds the next firm. The slope is the sum of operating firms' slopes — the seed of upward-sloping long-run supply in §10.6.

Six step-reveals. Pacing: show the problem, give 60–90 sec, then reveal region by region. The economic content is the kink: market supply is continuous but its slope jumps each time a new firm crosses its shutdown threshold. Emphasize the "operating iff \(p\geq\) threshold" gating — below the lowest threshold the market produces nothing. This piecewise structure is exactly what gives heterogeneous-firm industries an upward-sloping long-run supply curve (§10.6), in contrast to the flat symmetric-firm benchmark.

§10.4

Short-Run Market Equilibrium

\(N\) fixed — the price does the adjusting: \(N\cdot q^*(P^*) = Q^d(P^*)\)

Entry, Exit, and the Zero-Profit Result

Picture the burger joints around campus. Watch how one news headline sets the whole market in motion:

 1 · The calm

In long-run equilibrium every shop earns zero economic profit — owners just cover costs plus their next-best alternative. Nobody enters, nobody leaves.

 2 · The shock

Northeastern approves a 1,370-bed dorm down the block. Overnight ~1,370 new students live next door → burger demand jumps.

 3 · The signal

More demand → price rises \$8 → \$10 a burger → shops suddenly earn positive economic profit. What happens next?

In the Short Run: Positive Economic Profit

 Burger example — the campus market from the previous slide, one shop and the whole market side by side.

The new dorm opens → demand shifts right (D₀→D₁) → the burger price rises \$8 → \$10. Each shop slides up its \(MC\) curve to \(q_1\), and since P > ATC it pockets the shaded profit rectangle.

 But with free entry, economic profit is a magnet — it lures new shops in. That’s the trigger for what happens next.

The short-run snapshot BEFORE entry. A rightward demand shock (the new dorm) lifts the market price from the long-run resting point (\$8 = min ATC) up to \$10. The price-taking shop responds by sliding up its MC curve from \(q_0\) to \(q_1\); because price now sits above ATC at that output, it earns a positive economic profit (shaded rectangle = (P - ATC) times q). Two details worth pointing at: \(q_0\) is where ATC bottoms out at \$8, and \(q_1\) sits to its RIGHT — a firm chasing a high price produces past minimum efficient scale, which is why its unit cost creeps back up to about \$8.10. That profit is exactly the entry signal — slide 28 shows entry competing it back down to \$8. Reads as an arc: \$8, demand shock, \$10 profit, entry, back to \$8.

Long-Run Free-Entry Equilibrium

\(\pi^{LR}=0 \iff p^{LR}=\min LRATC\), and \(N^*\) absorbs demand

Free Entry Drives Profit to Zero

 Back to the gigafactory — same picture as the burger market, now with the Ch 9 numbers attached.

Above \(\min LRATC\), profit is positive and firms enter; below, profit is negative and firms exit. Price settles where the marginal entrant earns exactly zero:

\(\pi^{LR}=0 \iff p^{LR}=\min LRATC\)

For the gigafactory, \(\min LRATC\) was locked in Ch 9: long-run \(K\!+\!L\) marginal cost \(\$27.21\) (constant under CRS) plus materials \(m=\$55.60\):

\(\min LRATC = \$27.21 + \$55.60 = \mathbf{\$82.81}\)/kWh

Why is \(LRATC\) flat? Under constant returns to scale, doubling every input doubles output, so long-run cost is proportional to output: \(c(q)=k\,q\). Then \(LRAC=c/q=k\) and \(LRMC=dc/dq=k\) — both equal the same constant. The LR cost curves are horizontal lines, so \(\min LRATC = LRATC = \$82.81\) at every \(q\).

Fig 10.4 — LR equilibrium: \(p=\min LRATC\), \(\pi=0\).

The free-entry zero-profit condition — the heart of the chapter. The mechanism is an arbitrage: positive profit attracts entry, which expands supply and pushes price down; negative profit forces exit, which contracts supply and pushes price up. The only resting point is \(p=\min LRATC\). For the gigafactory this is the Ch 9 lock \$82.81 (LRMC \$27.21 + materials \$55.60). Emphasize the epistemic shift: \$82.81 was something you could compute from one firm's cost function in Ch 9; now it's something the market produces through entry and exit. Four Equalities, One Resting Point

\(p = MR\)  —  every firm is a price-taker, \(\partial p/\partial q_i = 0\)

\(p = MC\)  —  every firm is profit-maximizing — the Ch 9 FOC

\(p = \min LRATC\)  —  entry and exit drove profit to zero

∴ \(LRMC = LRATC\) at the firm's output

All four hold at once — and only in long-run competitive equilibrium.

Where our calibration differs

In the U-shaped picture you drew in Principles, \(LRMC\) cuts \(LRATC\) exactly at its minimum — so the fourth line pins down a unique efficient scale \(q^*\).

Under our CRS calibration \(LRATC\) is flat, so \(LRMC = LRATC = \$82.81\) at every \(q\). The equality holds everywhere, and firm output is indeterminate — Ch 9's knife-edge.

That is precisely why the next slide makes \(N^*\), not \(q^*\), the equilibrating variable.

The consolidation slide — string the equalities together out loud, one per beat, and name where each came from: \(p=MR\) is the price-taking assumption from §10.2, \(p=MC\) is the Ch 9 profit-max FOC, \(p=\min LRATC\) is the free-entry condition we just derived. The fourth line is a geometric consequence, not a new assumption: if price equals both marginal cost and the minimum of average cost, then marginal and average cost are equal there. In the U-shaped case that is the bottom of the U — the GPA analogy from Ch 9, and exactly the picture students carry from 1116. Then deliver the twist on the right: our CRS Cobb-Douglas calibration makes LRATC horizontal, so \(LRMC = LRATC\) is true at every output rather than at one. The equality chain survives; what dies is the unique \(q^*\). Students who memorized "the firm produces at minimum efficient scale" need to hear that this is a property of U-shaped cost, not of competition. Hand off directly to \(N^*\).

Calculus Corner 10.2 — The Firm Count \(N^*\)

Because \(LRATC\) is flat under CRS, at \(p=\$82.81\) every output earns zero profit — so the firm's output is indeterminate (Ch 9's knife-edge). Anchor each firm at the Ch 7 plant size \(q^*=32\) GWh; demand then pins the firm count:

\[ N^* = \frac{Q^d(p^{LR})}{q^*} = \frac{5{,}911.43 - 52.185(82.81)}{32} \]

\(= \dfrac{1{,}590}{32} = 49.69 \approx \mathbf{50}\)

\(N^*\) is the equilibrating variable. If demand were 2,000 GWh, \(N^*=63\). The price doesn't move — the firm count does. This resolves Ch 9's CRS knife-edge.

Entry shifts SR supply \(S_0\!\to\!S_1\); the new SR eq lands on the horizontal LR supply — A (\$84) → B (\$82.81), \(\pi=0\).

The chapter's central result, and the resolution of Ch 9's cliffhanger. Under CRS, individual firm output is undefined at the knife-edge — which is exactly why we need industry-level analysis. Free entry pins \(p\); the plant-size anchor \(q^*=32\) GWh (Ch 7) pins per-firm output; demand then determines \(N^*\). The closed form \(N^* = Q^d(\min LRATC)/q^*\) is the payoff. \(1{,}590/32 = 49.69 \approx 50\). The deep idea: which variable is endogenous flips between SR (price) and LR (firm count). Drive this home — it's the most-tested concept in the chapter.

The profit got over-hyped — a flood of new shops.

The overshoot half of the long-run dance. The profit signal gets OVER-hyped: too many firms pile in, so market supply shifts S₀→S₁→S₂ and price falls past the $8 zero-profit point all the way to $6. A burger chain closes a location — a firm exits the market.

"Zero Profit" Doesn't Mean the 10-K Shows Zero

Heads Up 10.5

Zero economic profit ≠ zero accounting profit. Economic cost includes the opportunity cost of capital — for the gigafactory, \(r\bar K = 0.13\times 4 = \$0.52\)B/yr the firm could earn elsewhere.

A firm at LR equilibrium with zero economic profit still shows positive accounting profit \(\approx r\bar K\) on its 10-K. The two differ by the foregone return on equity capital.

Recall from Principles — 1116 §10.5

In Principles, "Long-Run Equilibrium — The Invisible Hand at Work" told this graphically: free entry drives price down to the minimum of \(LRATC\), where the price line is tangent to the bottom of the U-shaped cost curve and economic profit is competed to zero.

Chapter 10 writes the same picture in algebra: \(p^{LR}=\min LRATC\), with \(N^*\) the equilibrating variable. Under CRS the firm output drops out and \(N^*\) does all the work.

Two pieces students conflate. Heads Up 10.5: "zero profit" is the single most misread phrase in micro. Economic profit nets out the opportunity cost of the owner's capital; accounting profit doesn't. So competitive firms in LR equilibrium show black ink on their 10-Ks (roughly \(r\cdot K\)) while earning zero economic profit — no contradiction. The 1116 recall (verified against source) connects today's algebra to the graphical tangency picture students already know: the horizontal price line tangent to the bottom of the U-shaped LRATC. Same content, two mathematical languages.

§10.6

Long-Run Market Supply

Symmetric firms → flat; heterogeneous firms → upward-sloping + Ricardian rents

When Firms Differ, LR Supply Slopes Up

Symmetric firms → LR supply is horizontal at \(\min LRATC=\$82.81\): below it all firms exit, above it infinite entry. \(N^*\) adjusts at the flat price.

But real firms differ — management, technology vintage, input access, location. Let potential entrants have \(\min LRATC \sim F(c)\). At price \(p\), firms with cost \(\leq p\) enter:

\(Q^s_{LR}(p) = N_{\text{pot}}\,q^*\,F(p), \quad \frac{dQ^s_{LR}}{dp} = N_{\text{pot}}\,q^*\,F'(p) > 0\)

Infra-marginal firms (cost strictly below \(p\)) earn Ricardian rents \((p-\min LRATC_i)\cdot q^*\); the marginal firm earns zero. (Formalized by Melitz 2003.)

Fig 10.7 — SR supply (steep, \(N\) fixed) vs LR supply (flatter, entry/exit).

Calculus Corner 10.4 lives here. The symmetric benchmark gives a degenerate flat LR supply — useful but unrealistic. Cost heterogeneity (which the §10.5 market shares reveal directly: CATL 39%, the rest fragmented) makes LR supply slope up. The mechanism: as price rises, higher-cost firms become profitable and enter, so quantity rises with price. Infra-marginal firms earn Ricardian rents — the same idea as land rent, here applied to cost advantage. Melitz 2003 is the formal heterogeneous-firms model (graduate IO); we only sketch the intuition. Cost Differences & Economic Rent

In the long run a competitive industry earns no economic profit — but that clean result quietly assumes every firm has identical cost curves.

Real firms differ. More-efficient firms earn economic rent: a return to a specialized input above what the firm paid for it.

With different LR cost curves, price settles at the min ATC of the highest-cost (marginal) firm. That firm earns zero profit and zero producer surplus; every lower-cost firm pockets rent.

Heterogeneous firms (\(LMC_1\)–\(LMC_4\)). Each produces where \(LMC=P\); lower-cost firms earn rent (\(P\) above their min ATC), the marginal firm earns none.

Bridges §10.6's heterogeneous-firm supply to the language of rent. The symmetric model gives zero economic profit in LR; relax "identical firms" and the low-cost firms earn economic rent — a return to whatever makes them efficient (better management, a process patent, prime land). Price is pinned by the marginal (highest-cost) firm that just breaks even. The key point, set up for the next slide: that rent is NOT economic profit — it is the opportunity cost of the specialized input.

Economic Rent ≠ Economic Profit

The most common mix-up in this section. A firm can pay large rents to its scarce inputs and still earn zero economic profit — because the rent is a cost, not a profit.

Economic rent

A return to a specialized input, counted inside that input's opportunity cost. Rents accrue to the more-productive-than-average input — a star manager, a trade secret, a corner with 40,000 people walking past.

Economic profit

What's left after subtracting all opportunity costs — including the rents paid to specialized inputs. Free entry competes this to zero in the long run, even while rents persist.

Application — Rents & Avocados from Mexico  🥑

The puzzle

Mexican avocado farming is ~perfectly competitive, yet Avocados From Mexico (AFM) advertises heavily. Why advertise in a zero-profit market? → to capture economic rents.

• Price is set by the marginal (highest-cost) grower; low-cost orchards earn rent from soil, climate, and location.

• Ads shift demand out → price ↑ → higher-cost growers enter → rent to low-cost orchards grows.

• Marginal orchards still earn zero profit; rents are capitalized into orchard land values.

Advertising shifts demand \(D_0\!\to\!D_1\); price rises \(P_0\!\to\!P_1\) and the economic rent to low-cost orchards (green) expands.

Bottom line. Advertising can't manufacture economic profit in a competitive market — but it can grow the economic rents (and asset values) of the lowest-cost producers.

Application that operationalizes the rent/profit distinction. The puzzle is genuine: if avocado farming is competitive with zero economic profit, why does AFM run Super Bowl ads? Advertising shifts market demand out, raising the equilibrium price; that creates no lasting profit (entry of higher-cost growers competes it away), but it raises the RENTS earned by inframarginal low-cost orchards — and those rents are capitalized into the land/asset values of the best orchards. The beneficiaries are the owners of the specialized inputs (prime avocado land), exactly as the theory predicts.

Application 10.3 — Freelance Platforms: Competitive Sellers, One Middleman

Within a skill band, Upwork · Fiverr · Toptal approximate perfect competition among sellers. Two things to separate carefully: the cost dispersion that makes LR supply slope up, and the platform fee, which is a different animal entirely.

① Why sellers are ~price-takers

• Services within a band are reasonably homogeneous.
• Hundreds of thousands of buyers and sellers — 3.1M Fiverr active buyers in 2025.
• Prices update in real time; no single quote moves the rate.

② So where is the cost gap here? It is geographic: the same deliverable is offered by sellers facing very different living costs and outside options, so a skill band shows a spread of rates rather than one price — §10.6's \(F(c)\) in a labour market.

③ The take rate is not a cost gap

Fiverr's 27.7% take rate (FY2025) is not part of any seller's cost curve. It is a wedge between what the buyer pays and what the seller receives — the same geometry as a tax, so it carries the same deadweight loss (§10.9).

§10.7

The First Welfare Theorem

Is the competitive equilibrium good? — \(TS(Q)=\int_0^Q [P^d-P^s]\,dq\)

Two Efficiencies, Achieved Without a Planner

Panel A — surplus is maximized at \(e\). Panel B — the firm's flat demand is tangent to \(LRATC\) at its minimum. Drawn for the general U-shaped case; under CRS the tangency flattens into coincidence.

Productive efficiency — output is made at the lowest attainable unit cost, \(p = \min LRATC\). A firm carrying waste is undercut and exits.

Allocative efficiency — the price buyers pay equals the marginal cost of the last unit made, \(p = MC\). No mutually beneficial trade is left undone.

\(p = MR = MC = \min SRATC = \min LRATC\)

Zero profit is the feature, not the bug. Persistent positive profit means buyers are paying above cost; persistent losses mean producers are subsidizing buyers. Zero economic profit is the only resting point where price equals true opportunity cost.

The interpretive payoff after the surplus proof in CC 10.3 — the theorem says total surplus is maximized, and this slide says what that buys you, in the two standard names students will be tested on. Productive efficiency is the vertical question (are we making it as cheaply as possible?); allocative efficiency is the horizontal one (are we making the right amount?). Perfect competition delivers both, and delivers them with nobody in charge — no planner sets \(p\), no regulator enforces \(\min LRATC\); entry and exit do it. Panel B is drawn U-shaped because that is the general case and the picture from 1116; say out loud that under our CRS calibration the tangency degenerates — the flat \(d\) line lies along the flat LRATC rather than kissing it at one point. Close on the zero-profit reframe: students hear "zero profit" as failure, so give them the Goldilocks version — positive means overcharging, negative means unsustainable, zero means the price is exactly the resource cost. This is the sentence Chapters 11–18 will spend eight chapters breaking.

Producer Surplus for a Competitive Firm

Marginal cost slopes upward, so some output is sold at a price above its cost of production. Producer surplus is the sum of the gaps between price and marginal cost over every unit produced.

Two equivalent views, shown below: sum the per-unit \(P-MC\) markups (a), or read the same area as the \((P-AVC^*)\,Q^*\) rectangle (b).

\(PS = TR - VC\)

Producer surplus two ways — the area between \(P\) and \(MC\) equals the \((P-AVC^*)\,Q^*\) rectangle \(=TR-VC\).

Defines producer surplus at the firm level — the building block the welfare theorem (and Ch 2's surplus integrals) rest on. Two equivalent readings: (a) sum the per-unit markups \(P-MC\) over all units produced — the area between the price line and the rising MC curve; (b) the same magnitude is total revenue minus variable cost, the rectangle \((P-AVC^*)Q^*\). They are equal because variable cost is exactly the area under MC. Note PS uses VARIABLE cost only — fixed cost never enters, which is precisely why PS differs from profit (next slide).

Producer Surplus Is Not Profit — \(\pi = PS - FC\)

Producer surplus is closely related to profit — but they are not the same thing.

\(PS = TR - VC\)

\(\pi = TR - VC - FC = PS - FC\)

They differ by exactly fixed cost: PS ignores \(FC\) (sunk in the short run); profit subtracts it.

Producer surplus for a competitive industry

• Industry PS = the sum of every firm's surplus.

• In perfect competition the supply curve is the MC curve above min \(AVC\) — so industry PS is the area above supply, below price.

Watch the run. In the long run every cost is avoidable, so \(FC=0\) and \(PS=\pi\). That is why the free-entry case in W10.6 has \(PS=0\): there, "zero profit" and "zero producer surplus" are the same sentence.

Industry PS — the area above the supply curve and below price, up to \(Q\).

The distinction students most often blur. Producer surplus = TR − VC; profit = TR − VC − FC. So PS exceeds profit by exactly fixed cost, and the two coincide only when FC = 0 (the long run, where every cost is variable). This is why a firm can have positive producer surplus and still lose money (if PS < FC, profit is negative) — the short-run shutdown logic from Ch 9. For the industry, PS is the sum of firms' surpluses, which equals the area above the market supply curve (the horizontal sum of MC above min AVC) and below price — the same triangle Ch 2 drew, now derived from firm cost curves.

Competitive Equilibrium Maximizes Total Surplus

Recall the surplus integrals from Ch 2. Total surplus is the sum — and the price cancels:

\[ TS(Q) = \int_0^Q [P^d(q) - P^s(q)]\,dq \]

The integrand is positive for \(q < Q^*\) (demand price exceeds supply price — a mutually beneficial trade) and negative for \(q > Q^*\). So \(TS\) rises until \(Q^*\) and falls after. The maximum is at \(Q^*\). That's the proof.

Heads Up 10.4

The theorem is an equilibrium statement: if the market reaches the competitive equilibrium, total surplus is maximal. It says nothing about how fast, or about fairness, equality, or sustainability.

Fig 10.6 — CS + PS, maximized at \(Q^*\).

The First Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics — and the whole proof fits on one slide. The trick is that the price cancels in \(CS+PS\), leaving total surplus as a function of quantity alone: \(TS(Q)=\int_0^Q[P^d-P^s]dq\). The integrand is the gain from the marginal trade; it's positive below \(Q^*\) and negative above, so \(TS\) peaks exactly at the competitive quantity. Heads Up 10.4 is the crucial caveat: this is an "if you reach equilibrium" statement, silent on convergence speed and silent on distribution. That silence is what §10.9 and the rest of Part IV/V exploit.

Calculus Corner 10.3 — The Proof in Two Derivatives

Differentiate \(TS(Q) = \int_0^Q [P^d-P^s]\,dq\):

\[ \frac{dTS}{dQ} = P^d(Q) - P^s(Q) \]

FOC: \(\dfrac{dTS}{dQ}=0 \iff P^d(Q^*)=P^s(Q^*)\) — exactly the competitive equilibrium.

\[ \frac{d^2 TS}{dQ^2} = \underbrace{\tfrac{dP^d}{dQ}}_{<0} - \underbrace{\tfrac{dP^s}{dQ}}_{>0} < 0 \]

SOC negative → \(Q^*\) is a maximum.

The calculus says the same thing as the geometry: the competitive quantity is where inverse demand meets inverse supply, and the second-order condition confirms it's a peak, not a trough.

We've now seen these surplus integrals three times: Ch 2 introduced them, Ch 3 used them for tax DWL, and Ch 10 uses them to prove the competitive \(Q^*\) is optimal, not just consistent.

The argument generalizes: any well-behaved S&D system (demand down, supply up) has a competitive equilibrium that maximizes total surplus.

The calculus version of the welfare theorem — in the sidebar per voice protection. Two derivatives do the whole job: the first-order condition recovers the equilibrium condition \(P^d=P^s\), and the second-order condition (demand slopes down, supply slopes up, so the difference is negative) confirms it's a maximum. Emphasize the "three appearances" arc of the surplus integral — Ch 2 (definition), Ch 3 (tax DWL), Ch 10 (optimality) — it's one of the most reused tools in the book. The generalization line matters: this isn't special to the gigafactory or eggs; it's any well-behaved market.

§10.8

Long-Run Adjustment

Entry and exit carry a competitive market from one long-run equilibrium to the next

Adjustment to an Increase in Demand

Trace the move to a new long-run equilibrium after a shock. First: a viral pizza trend pushes demand out, \(D_1\!\to\!D_2\).

Short run (A): existing pizzerias earn positive economic profit because \(P > LRATC\) — price jumps to \(P_2\).

Long run (B): new pizzerias enter, supply shifts out \(S_1\!\to\!S_2\), and price falls back to min \(LRATC\) at \(P_1\).

Net: more firms, higher quantity, same price — profit competed back to zero.

The canonical comparative-statics exercise, in a pizza market. A positive demand shock raises price along the existing supply curve to point A, where incumbents earn positive economic profit (\(P > LRATC\)). That profit is the entry signal: new pizzerias enter, market supply shifts out, and price slides back down \(D_2\) to point B, where \(P=\min LRATC\) and profit is zero again. The firm faces the higher SR price \(d_2\), produces more and profits; in the LR price returns to \(d_1=\min LRATC\). Panels (c)-(d): price spikes then returns to \(P_1\), while quantity ratchets permanently up to \(Q_2\). Same engine as the §10.5 gigafactory free-entry story, told as comparative statics.

Adjustment to an Increase in Production Costs

Now costs rise — cheese, flour, wages, or rent climb — shifting supply up and in, \(S_1\!\to\!S_2\).

Short run: some pizzerias earn negative economic profit (\(P < LRATC\)).

Long run: those firms exit, supply shifts inward, and price rises until \(P\) equals the new, higher min \(LRATC\) at \(P_2\).

Net: fewer firms, lower quantity, higher price.

The mirror image: a permanent cost increase. At the old price \(P_1\) the higher cost curves mean some firms now run losses (\(P < LRATC_2\)). Losses are the exit signal: firms leave, market supply shifts inward \(S_1\!\to\!S_2\), and price rises until it reaches the new, higher \(\min LRATC\) at \(P_2\). The firm panel shows costs shifting up (\(LMC_1,LATC_1 \to LMC_2,LATC_2\)); survivors break even again at the higher price. Net: fewer firms, lower industry quantity, higher price. Capital Free Entry vs Biological Flock Rebuild

What happened

The 2022–2026 HPAI outbreak culled ~168M layers; retail eggs went from ~\$1.50–2.00 to \$4.95/dozen (Jan 2025). By March 2026 the flock was back to ~309M birds and the price had fallen to \$2.35.

Why it took that long

The binding long-run factor is biological, not financial. A pullet needs 18–20 weeks to lay, and no amount of capital shortens that. Supply returns only as fast as farmers can hatch and raise replacements.

Because the shock is temporary, price spikes then recedes and output dips then recovers — at the speed the flock can be rebuilt, not instantly.

Theory and Data 10.3  

Mitchell-Thompson-Malone (2024): HPAI raised weekly retail egg prices ~5.3% on average, and models that ignore flock recovery overstate consumer-surplus losses by 3–4× — the losses concentrate in the recovery window rather than persisting.

Both are "long-run adjustments" in the economic sense — the input that was fixed in the SR becomes variable — but the binding constraint and therefore the SPEED differ, and speed is what the two panels show. Long-Run Supply in Constant-, Increasing-, and Decreasing-Cost Industries

So far we assumed LR average cost is constant at every output level. In general, the slope of the long-run supply curve depends on what happens to firms' costs as the whole industry expands through entry.

Increasing-cost — LR average cost rises with industry output (competition over a scarce input, e.g., oil) ⇒ upward-sloped \(S_{LR}\) (Panel A).

Constant-cost — LR average cost is unchanged by industry output (the baseline so far) ⇒ horizontal \(S_{LR}\) (Panel B).

Decreasing-cost — LR average cost falls with industry output (industry-level increasing returns, or cheaper inputs) ⇒ downward-sloped \(S_{LR}\) (Panel C).

Demand rises \(D_0\!\to\!D_1\): the market jumps from \(a\) to \(b\) in the short run, then entry carries it to the new long-run equilibrium \(c\). \(S_{LR}\) joins \(a\) and \(c\).

The generalization of the long-run supply curve beyond the constant-cost benchmark. Mechanics (identical in every panel): start at the long-run equilibrium \(a=D_0\cap S_0\); demand rises to \(D_1\), giving the short-run equilibrium \(b=D_1\cap S_0\) at a higher price; positive profit induces entry, shifting short-run supply to \(S_1\) and landing the new long-run equilibrium at \(c=D_1\cap S_1\). \(S_{LR}\) connects \(a\) and \(c\). What differs is only the height of \(c\): if expansion bids up input prices (increasing cost), \(c\) sits above \(a\) and \(S_{LR}\) slopes up; if input prices are unaffected (constant cost), \(c\) is level with \(a\) and \(S_{LR}\) is flat — the horizontal case the whole chapter assumed; if expansion lowers costs (decreasing cost), \(c\) sits below \(a\) and \(S_{LR}\) slopes down. Oil/extraction is the classic increasing-cost example; constant cost is the textbook default.

§10.10 — From Metaphor to Theorem

How "the invisible hand" became something you can actually prove — three steps, 178 years.

1776

Adam Smith

The invisible hand — a metaphor. Self-interest somehow produces order. No equations, no proof, no statement of when it fails.

1874

Léon Walras

Writes it as simultaneous equations: one price vector clearing every market at once. To explain how a market finds it with no planner, he invents the tâtonnement — an imaginary auctioneer who raises prices where there is excess demand and lowers them where there is excess supply.

1954

Arrow & Debreu

Prove an equilibrium exists, and give both welfare theorems their rigorous form. Walras saw the structure; he did not prove it. This is the machinery behind the result we used in §10.7.

Where our proof sits. §10.7 was partial equilibrium — one market, everything else held fixed. Walras's frame is general equilibrium — all markets at once, which is what you need before "the market allocates efficiently" means anything economy-wide.

The Second Welfare Theorem — any Pareto-efficient allocation can be supported by some competitive equilibrium, given the right redistribution — is the other half of Arrow–Debreu. Both are graduate-level; Walras pointed at them in 1874.

 Notice what tâtonnement is: a thought experiment. Walras never ran it. Next slide: we do.

Restructured as a timeline because the old version buried three separate ideas in four prose blocks. The spine is 1776 to 1874 to 1954, and each date answers a different question. 1776: Smith asserts the result. 1874: Walras states it precisely enough to be false — a price vector clearing all markets simultaneously — and invents the tâtonnement to describe how a market might grope toward it without anyone in charge. 1954: Arrow and Debreu supply the existence proof and the rigorous welfare theorems, using separating-hyperplane arguments students do not need to see. The key-result box is the honest caveat about our own proof: §10.7 did one market with everything else fixed, which is partial equilibrium; the sentence "competitive markets allocate efficiently" only means something economy-wide once you have the general-equilibrium version. Do not belabour the Second Welfare Theorem — one line, flagged as graduate material. Economist at Work — Vernon Smith, 1962

Walras's tâtonnement was a thought experiment. Smith ran it. Index cards handed each buyer a private maximum and each seller a private minimum — nobody could see the aggregate curves — and then he opened a double oral auction.

He expected it to fail. The theory assumes many traders and good information; he had a classroom and neither. Instead prices converged to the theoretical equilibrium within about five rounds, with only a handful of traders per side.

Convergence to \(p^*\) is not something the model needs handed to it — it is an experimental finding. Launched experimental economics; Nobel Prize, 2002.

The empirical bookend to the Walras slide, and the honest answer to the question the whole chapter invites: we assumed price-taking, homogeneity, free entry, and perfect information, so does any of this survive contact with real people? Smith's 1962 JPE design is the cleanest test available — private reservation values on index cards means no trader knows the supply or demand curve, so convergence cannot be reverse-engineered from knowing the answer. Note what is small here: not hundreds of traders, a dozen or so, and they still land on \(p^*\). That matters for this chapter specifically, because condition 1 (many small firms) is the assumption we conceded is strained for gigafactories — Smith's result says the competitive prediction is more robust to small numbers than the assumption list suggests. Connect back explicitly: Walras invented a fictional auctioneer to explain how the market gropes toward the clearing vector; Smith showed a room of undergraduates does it without one. Coming Up — Chapter 11: Monopoly

Today the firm was a price taker with \(\partial p/\partial q = 0\). Chapter 11 flips the single assumption that drives everything about market power.

\(\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial q} < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; MR = p + \dfrac{\partial p}{\partial q}\,q < p \;\Rightarrow\; MR = MC \text{ at } Q_m < Q^*\)
