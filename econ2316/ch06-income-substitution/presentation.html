Two channels, one price spike

Substitution channel

Energy is now expensive relative to everything else. The household

  • turns down the thermostat,
  • weatherizes windows,
  • runs the dishwasher off-peak,
  • defers the next clothes-dryer purchase.

This response would happen even if real income were unchanged.

Income channel

The price spike makes the household effectively poorer (real income falls).

A poorer household cuts spending everywhere — including on necessary energy use that was not substitution-driven at all.

This response would happen even at unchanged prices if income fell by the same dollar amount.

Policy punchline. A price cap mutes substitution (preserves the old price, so the household conserves less). A targeted lump-sum transfer leaves the price signal sharp but neutralizes the income effect for households that need it. The two policies are not equivalent.

Conceptual setup before any algebra. Both channels operate at the same time in real-world data, which is exactly why we need a decomposition machine — the Slutsky equation — to separate them. The teaching frame: a price cap and a cash transfer might both cost the government the same dollars, but they pull on different levers. The next slide asks the students to vote on which policy is better; the answer is "depends on which channel dominates and what the externality is," but the trap is to call them equivalent.

Why C is the trap. Same government dollars can produce different welfare outcomes depending on whether substitution or income dominates the household's response. Cap is better if the household is very inelastic (substitution small); cash is better if the household is elastic and the cap would distort behavior away from conservation.

The chapter builds the Slutsky equation — the algebra that decomposes any observed Marshallian response into its substitution and income parts. Once we have it, the policy question has a quantitative answer.

§6.1.1 — Pure-income thought experiment: Sam's raise

Sam allocates \$60/month between Netflix (\$4) and Spotify (\$6). Cobb-Douglas with \(\alpha = 1/3\). His income doubles to \$120 — neither price changes.

Budget: \(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = I\) shifts outward in parallel.

  • Both axes rise. Sam spreads new income across both goods (he was buying both already).
  • Slope preserved: \(-p_1/p_2 = -2/3\) unchanged — no relative-price move.
  • Higher indifference curve: \(IC_0 \to IC_1\). Strictly higher utility (monotonicity, §4.1).

Cobb-Douglas optima

Constant-share rule: \(x_1^M = \alpha I/p_1\), \(x_2^M = (1{-}\alpha) I/p_2\).

At \(I=60\): \(A = (5,\, 6.67)\).
At \(I=120\): \(B = (10,\, 13.33)\).

Both quantities double — Cobb-Douglas income elasticity \(=1\) on each good.

§6.1.2 — Income elasticity: necessity, luxury, inferior

\[\varepsilon_I \;=\; \frac{\Delta x / x}{\Delta I / I}\;=\;\frac{\partial x}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{I}{x}.\]

Sign of \(\varepsilon_I\) sets normal vs. inferior; magnitude inside the normal range further splits necessity vs. luxury.

Necessity

\(0 < \varepsilon_I < 1\)

Quantity rises with income, slower than income. Budget share falls. Examples: toothpaste, salt, food-at-home, electricity.

Luxury

\(\varepsilon_I > 1\)

Quantity rises faster than income. Budget share rises. Examples: jewelry, first-class tickets, vacation homes, restaurant meals.

Inferior

\(\varepsilon_I < 0\)

Quantity falls as income rises. Trade up to a preferred alternative. Examples: store-brand canned soup, bus rides, used cars, ramen.

Adding-up constraint. Not every good can be inferior simultaneously. If Sam cuts bus rides when his income rises, the saved dollars go somewhere. At least one good in the bundle must be normal.

Normal vs. inferior can flip with income level. A used car is normal at low income (any car beats the bus) and inferior at high income (new cars supplant used ones).

Three-way taxonomy. Connect to 1116 §6.3 (Income Elasticity of Demand) — students saw the formula and the necessity/luxury split there; today we tie it to the Slutsky equation's income term. Trap question for poll: "Can EVERY good in a consumer's bundle be inferior?" — answer NO (the income has to flow somewhere). Mention level-dependence: the same physical good can be normal at one income range and inferior at another — this is exactly what makes the Engel curve (next slide) bend.

§6.1.3-4 — Income Expansion Path and Engel curve

Income Expansion Path (IEP)

Trace the consumer's optimum bundle from \(I = 0\) (the origin) through every increasing income level. Connect the tangencies.

  • Two normal goods: IEP slopes upward — every income increase moves the bundle northeast.
  • Normal + eventually-inferior: IEP bends backward on the inferior axis past some income threshold.

Lives in the same \((x_1, x_2)\) plane as the indifference curves.

Engel curve (Engel, 1857)

Replot: income on the vertical axis, quantity of one good on the horizontal. One point per IEP point.

  • Positive slope ⇒ normal good at that income.
  • Negative slope ⇒ inferior at that income.
  • Backward bend ⇒ normal-to-inferior transition.

Engel curve = empirical workhorse. Household surveys (BLS CEX, USDA ERS) record income + spending; plotting the data gives the Engel curve directly. The slope at any point estimates \(\varepsilon_I\) at that income range. T&D 6.1 below uses BLS CEX 2024 food-share data to verify Engel's law (food share falls with income).

Two graphical tools, one piece of information. IEP lives in (x_1, x_2) space — sit next to indifference curves. Engel curve replots with income on the vertical axis and ONE good's quantity on the horizontal — easier to estimate empirically. Backward bend in either curve = normal-to-inferior transition. Land Engel's law: as income rises, food share FALLS (food is a necessity) — this is the cleanest empirical regularity in consumer theory and the topic of T&D 6.1 later. §6.1.6 Application — Aguiar & Bils on restaurants as luxury

Aguiar & Bils (2015, AER, DOI 10.1257/aer.20120599) estimated Engel curves on CEX + PSID data for a wide range of consumer goods. Two headline findings:

PART 2 · §6.2  How price changes affect consumption choices

Substitution and income effects on an indifference map

Deriving a demand curve

Just as income shifts the bundle (§6.1), changes in relative prices — holding income constant — also reshape consumer choices. We now build Sam's individual demand curve directly from his utility model.

The recipe

  • A demand curve maps quantity demanded to price, holding income and other prices fixed.
  • To trace it, change one price on the indifference-map / budget-constraint figure and read off the new optimum.
  • Each price → one optimum → one \((Q, P)\) point on the demand curve.
  • Connect the points to get the consumer's demand curve for that good.

The observed price represents the consumer's maximum willingness to pay for the last unit consumed — the inverse-demand interpretation we use in welfare analysis (Ch 2 CS, Ch 6.7 CV).

Bridge slide between §6.1 (income channel) and §6.2's substitution/income decomposition. Concept: a demand curve isn't a primitive — it's derived from the consumer's utility-max optimization by varying ONE price at a time. Mention: the demand curve is a "summary" of how the consumer reacts to price changes, while the underlying IC map is the structure that produces it. Shifts in the demand curve

Movement along the demand curve answers “more Netflix at lower price.” A shift of the whole curve answers a different question: at the same price, has the consumer's demand changed?

What shifts the curve

  • Preferences change (taste shock; new info; ad campaign).
  • Income changes (the §6.1 channel).
  • Prices of other goods change (cross-price effects).

Sam's preference shock

Suppose Sam starts listening to more new music (podcasts, lossless audio) and values Netflix less, relatively speaking. His Cobb-Douglas weight \(\alpha\) on Netflix drops from \(1/3\) to \(1/5\).

At every Netflix price, Sam wants fewer Netflix subs — the whole demand curve shifts inward.

Mechanism: with lower \(\alpha\), Sam's indifference curves flatten in the Netflix direction — tangency on the same budget line now sits further left.

Movement ALONG vs shift OF the demand curve — the perennial confusion that students need to internalize. Movement along = same demand curve, different price → different quantity. Shift = same price, different quantity → different demand curve. Three things shift the curve: preferences, income, other prices. The Sam example uses preferences (taste shock toward Spotify/podcasts, away from Netflix). The Cobb-Douglas mechanics: drop α from 1/3 to 1/5 → x_N(p) drops from 20/p_N to 12/p_N at every price. Case study 1 — Capuchin monkeys obey the law of demand

Chen, Lakshminarayanan & Santos (2006, JPE).

  • Concept: Capuchin monkeys were taught to trade metal washers as “money” for foods (Jell-O cubes, grapes).
  • Price change: The Jell-O “price” rose — originally 3 cubes per washer, then only 1 cube per washer (3× price hike).
  • Behavior: Monkeys responded exactly as theory predicts — they substituted toward the cheaper good (grapes) and consumed less Jell-O. The law of demand isn't a human cultural artifact; it falls out of optimization.

Lesson: the SE+IE machinery isn't species-specific. Any optimizing agent with stable preferences over bundles obeys the same law.

Chen-Lakshminarayanan-Santos 2006 JPE — the canonical capuchin-monkey experiment. Yale lab taught monkeys to use metal washers as a token currency, redeemable for different foods at posted "prices" (cubes per washer). When researchers raised the relative price of Jell-O (one cube per washer instead of three), the monkeys substituted toward the now-relatively-cheaper grapes. Same qualitative behavior as human consumers facing relative-price changes. Pedagogical land: substitution and income effects aren't human-specific psychology; they're features of optimization with stable preferences. The paper is open online if students want the full read.

Case study 2 — Rats show inferior vs. luxury goods

Kagel, Battalio & colleagues, 1970s–80s (animal-economics tradition).

  • Concept: Rats in a Skinner box could press levers to receive either root beer (the preferred treat) or bitter quinine water. Daily presses available = the “budget.”
  • Income change: Researchers cut the daily press allowance — rats became “poorer.”
  • Behavior: When made poor, rats consumed less root beer and more quinine water. Root beer behaves as a luxury for rats; quinine water as an inferior good.

Lesson: the §6.1.2 taxonomy (normal/inferior, necessity/luxury) is a fact about preferences and budgets — reproducible in non-human animals.

Kagel-Battalio animal-economics tradition — most famously the 1981 AER "Demand Curves for Animal Consumers" study. Rats in Skinner boxes with daily lever-press budgets respond to relative price and income changes exactly like the consumer-theory taxonomy predicts: when income falls, root beer (the highly-preferred drink, "luxury") falls and quinine water (the bitter, less-preferred drink, "inferior") rises. Two problems, one optimum: primal vs. dual

PRIMAL (Ch 5)

Utility maximization at given prices and income.

\[\max_{x_1, x_2 \geq 0}\; U(x_1, x_2)\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\; p \cdot x \leq I.\]

Output: Marshallian demand \(x_i^M(p, I)\) + indirect utility \(v(p, I) = U(x^M)\).

DUAL (today)

Expenditure minimization at given prices and a utility floor.

\[\min_{x_1, x_2 \geq 0}\; p \cdot x \;\;\text{s.t.}\;\; U(x) \geq \bar u.\]

Output: Hicksian (compensated) demand \(h_i(p, \bar u)\) + expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u) = p \cdot h\).

Why the dual matters. The Hicksian demand isolates the substitution channel by construction — utility is held fixed, so any price-response is pure relative-price reallocation. That isolation is what makes the Slutsky decomposition possible.

The primal answers "what bundle do you actually buy?" The dual answers "what bundle would you buy if I gave you just enough income to stay on your original indifference curve?" The dual is a thought experiment, not a behavior the consumer actually exhibits — but it's the cleanest way to mathematically isolate substitution. At the same prices and the same utility, primal and dual select the same bundle: x^M(p, e(p, ū)) = h(p, ū). That's the duality identity, coming on the next slide but one.

The expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\)

\[e(p, \bar u) \;\equiv\; \min_{x \geq 0} \;\{\, p \cdot x \,:\, U(x) \geq \bar u \,\}\;=\; p_1 h_1(p,\bar u) + p_2 h_2(p, \bar u).\]

The minimum dollar cost of reaching utility \(\bar u\) at prices \(p\). The five properties that fall out of the definition:

1. Non-dec.

in each \(p_i\) — higher prices, weakly more cost.

2. HOD 1

in \(p\): \(e(\alpha p, \bar u) = \alpha\, e(p, \bar u)\). Inflate all prices, cost inflates the same.

3. Concave

in \(p\) — consumer can re-optimize when a price rises.

4. Continuous

in \((p, \bar u)\) — small changes, small cost changes.

5. Inc. in \(\bar u\)

reach a higher floor ⇒ pay more (locally non-satiated \(U\)).

Concavity intuition. If \(p_1\) rises, the consumer substitutes away from good 1 along the original indifference curve. So the cost rises by less than the “keep buying the same bundle” counterfactual — that's the definition of concavity.

Five properties, one slide. The properties matter because (i) they're the algebraic facts that get used in proofs (Slutsky derivation needs concavity, Roy needs HOD), (ii) they're poll-bait — students confuse concavity vs convexity, mix up direction of HOD, etc. (Poll 2 next exploits this.) Walras's law + non-satiation give property 5 (the floor binds at optimum).

Hicksian demand and the duality identity

Hicksian (compensated) demand

The expenditure-minimizing bundle at prices \(p\) and utility floor \(\bar u\):

\(h_i(p, \bar u) = \arg\min\,\{\, p \cdot x : U(x) \geq \bar u \,\}.\)

Read it as: “what bundle does the consumer buy when utility is held fixed and only relative prices change?” The Hicksian demand traces out a curve in \((p_1, x_1)\) space along a single indifference curve.

Duality identity

\[x_i^M(p,\, e(p, \bar u)) \;=\; h_i(p, \bar u).\]

At the same prices and the same utility floor, the Marshallian demand evaluated at the minimum cost \(e(p, \bar u)\) is exactly the Hicksian demand. Same bundle, two paths to it.

Why this is the lever. Differentiate the duality identity in \(p_j\) and you get the Slutsky equation in one line. The next part builds that derivation.

Two objects, one bundle. The duality identity is the master equation of the chapter — every Slutsky-related result derives from differentiating it. Geometric intuition: pick any indifference curve. The Hicksian curve is the locus of optima along *that* curve as relative prices change. The Marshallian curve is the locus of optima as income changes too. They coincide only at the original price-income pair.

CalculusCorner 6.1 — Cobb-Douglas dual: setup

Problem

A consumer has Cobb-Douglas utility \(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}\). Find the expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\) and the Hicksian demand \(h_1(p, \bar u),\, h_2(p, \bar u)\) for given prices \(p_1, p_2\) and utility floor \(\bar u\).

Set up the Lagrangian

\[\mathcal{L} = p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 - \mu\big(x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha} - \bar u\big).\]

Minimize cost; \(\mu \geq 0\) is the shadow value of a marginal utility unit.

Two FOCs

\(p_1 = \mu\, \alpha\, x_1^{\alpha-1} x_2^{1-\alpha}\)
\(p_2 = \mu\, (1-\alpha)\, x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{-\alpha}\)

Divide to eliminate \(\mu\): \(\;\dfrac{p_1}{p_2} = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \dfrac{x_2}{x_1}.\)

Next slide — solve for \(x_1, x_2\) on the indifference curve \(U = \bar u\), substitute into \(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2\).

Notation note: \(\mu\) for the dual Lagrange multiplier (units: dollars per util) to keep it distinct from the primal \(\lambda\) (utils per dollar). The two are reciprocals at the optimum — Math Appendix B.3 covers this. The ratio of FOCs is the tangency condition, same shape as the primal but with utility-constraint slope on the right.

CalculusCorner 6.1 — solve: closed forms drop out

From the tangency ratio:

\(x_2 = \dfrac{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}{\alpha\, p_2}\, x_1.\)

Plug into \(U(x_1, x_2) = \bar u\) and solve for \(x_1\):

\[h_1(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{\alpha\, p_2}{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}.\]

\[h_2(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}{\alpha\, p_2}\right)^{\alpha}.\]

Expenditure function

\[e(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{p_1}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha} \left(\frac{p_2}{1-\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}.\]

Sanity check. Verify properties of \(e\): non-decreasing in each \(p_i\) (powers are positive), HOD 1 in \(p\) (sum of exponents \(= 1\)), concave (product of powers of \(p\), exponents in \([0,1]\)), and linear in \(\bar u\). All five properties pass.

Returns in Slide 22: this \(e(p, \bar u)\) is the exact expression we differentiate when we run the Slutsky decomposition for Cobb-Douglas \(\alpha = 1/3\).

The two closed forms here are the backbone of every Cobb-Douglas Hicksian calculation in the rest of the chapter and on the homework. Worth pausing to point out the symmetry with the Marshallian closed forms from Ch 5 (constant expenditure shares \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\)) — same structural property, different arguments. Linearity of \(e\) in \(\bar u\) is a Cobb-Douglas accident, not a general property; CES and other forms give different curvature.

Answer: B — \(e\) is concave in \(p\), not convex

Intuition. If \(p_1\) rises by 10%, the consumer substitutes away from good 1 along the indifference curve. So minimum cost rises by less than 10% — the cost function bends down (concave), not up (convex).

Other distractors. A, C, D are all true: \(e\) is non-decreasing in each \(p_i\), homogeneous of degree 1 in \(p\) (linear scaling of all prices scales cost linearly), and increasing in \(\bar u\) (higher utility floor needs more dollars).

Concavity is the property that makes the Slutsky derivation work — specifically, the *off-diagonal* Hicksian substitution effects inherit symmetry from the Hessian of \(e\) (Shephard's lemma derivative, §6.5 forward-pointer). PART 3 · §6.3  The Slutsky equation

25 min · The chapter's headline identity, two walkthroughs

The Slutsky equation

For any pair of goods \(i, j\), any consumer with smooth preferences, evaluated at the bundle that achieves utility \(\bar u = v(p, I)\):

\[\boxed{\;\frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial h_i(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j}}_{\text{substitution effect}} \;-\; \underbrace{x_j^M(p, I) \cdot \frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial I}}_{\text{income effect}}.\;}\]

LHS: what the data show

How Marshallian (actual) demand responds to a price change. Both channels mixed.

RHS: the decomposition

Hicksian response (pure substitution) minus the dollar value of \(x_j\) times income response.

The income term is multiplied by \(x_j\) because a unit rise in \(p_j\) costs the consumer roughly \(x_j\) extra dollars to keep buying the original bundle — that's the “real income loss” we have to subtract back out.

This is the equation the chapter exists to deliver. Stop and let it land. Tell students: every time you see Marshallian response to a price change in data — gasoline elasticity, energy demand elasticity, anything — the Slutsky equation says it's a SUM of two effects, and you almost always need to know both to make a welfare or policy statement. Boxed because students should commit this to long-term memory.

CalculusCorner — derivation in three lines

Start from the duality identity

\[x_i^M\big(p,\, e(p, \bar u)\big) \;=\; h_i(p, \bar u).\]

Step 1. Differentiate both sides w.r.t. \(p_j\) (chain rule on LHS):

\[\frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} \;+\; \frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I} \cdot \frac{\partial e(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j}.\]

Step 2. Apply Shephard's lemma — \(\partial e / \partial p_j = h_j(p, \bar u) = x_j^M(p, I)\) at the duality point:

\[\frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} \;+\; \frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I} \cdot x_j^M \;=\; \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j}.\]

Step 3. Rearrange:

\[\frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j} \;-\; x_j^M \cdot \frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I}. \quad\square\]

Three-line proof. Land each step explicitly: (1) differentiate both sides of the duality identity, (2) replace ∂e/∂p_j with x_j^M via Shephard's lemma, (3) rearrange. Shephard's lemma full proof is in §6.5 (forward-pointer) — for today, take it on faith. The proof works for ANY smooth utility function; Cobb-Douglas in the walkthrough is just illustration.

HeadsUp 6.1 — own-price substitution is always \(\leq 0\)

The Hicksian law of demand. For any consumer with smooth, locally non-satiated preferences, the own-price substitution effect satisfies

\[\frac{\partial h_i(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_i} \;\leq\; 0.\]

Why

Falls out of concavity of \(e(p, \bar u)\) in \(p\): the diagonal of the Hessian of a concave function is \(\leq 0\). Shephard's lemma converts that into \(\partial h_i/\partial p_i \leq 0\).

Why students miss this

They confuse it with the Marshallian \(\partial x_i^M/\partial p_i\), which can be positive (Giffen good). The point of the decomposition: substitution always pulls demand down; income can push it up enough to overpower substitution.

Operational consequence. If you ever derive a substitution effect with the “wrong” sign, you have a sign error in the algebra. Use this as a self-check on every Slutsky computation.

The chapter's most-tested sign claim. It's a theorem, not an assumption — and it's tested in Problem 8 of the chapter. Watch out: students will sometimes claim "substitution can be positive for Giffen goods." It cannot. Giffen is a Marshallian phenomenon (income overpowers sub); Hicksian own-price effect is monotone.

Walkthrough 6.2 — Ana's boba tea: setup

Problem

Ana has \$20/week for boba tea (\(x_1\), cups) at \$1 each and “everything else” (\(x_2\), \$1 per dollar). Her preferences are quasilinear: \(U = 6\sqrt{x_1} + x_2\). The boba price doubles from \$1 to \$2 per cup. Show that the income effect on Ana's boba is exactly zero.

FOC for interior optimum

\(MU_1 = 3/\sqrt{x_1};\) tangency: \(3/\sqrt{x_1} = p_1/p_2,\) so

\(x_1^M(p_1, p_2) = \left(\dfrac{3 p_2}{p_1}\right)^2.\)

Crucial observation

\(x_1^M\) does not depend on \(I\). So \(\dfrac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I} = 0\). The Slutsky income term vanishes.

All extra income gets absorbed by \(x_2\) (the numeraire “money for everything else”). Ana's boba demand is income-inelastic at zero — the defining feature of quasilinear preferences.

Quasilinear preferences are the consumer-theory tool that nukes the income effect by assumption. Hyper-useful for welfare-measurement: when good 1 has no income effect, the area under the Marshallian curve IS the CV, no integration gymnastics needed. Marshallian change in boba:

\(\Delta x_1^M = 2.25 - 9 = -6.75\) cups.

Income effect

\(\Delta x_1^{inc} = - x_1 \cdot \dfrac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I} = 0.\)

\(\Delta x_1^{sub} = -6.75 - 0 = -6.75\) cups.

Generalization. For any quasilinear \(U = \phi(x_1) + x_2\), Marshallian and Hicksian demand for good 1 coincide. The CV equals the area to the left of that single demand curve — the cleanest welfare measure available, and the structural assumption that makes the Ch 2 consumer-surplus integral the right welfare metric.

The headline: quasilinear is the "boring" case where Slutsky decomposition is trivial — and that's precisely why it's the workhorse for welfare measurement. If \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I = 0\) for good 1, the Slutsky equation tells us that…

A) The Marshallian and Hicksian demand for good 1 coincide
B) Good 1 must be inferior
C) Good 1's price elasticity is zero
D) The compensating variation equals the dollar change in income

60 sec to vote

Press 1 for the timer. Right answer: A. The income term in the Slutsky equation vanishes, so ∂x_1^M/∂p_1 = ∂h_1/∂p_1. B is wrong — zero income effect is the boundary of normal/inferior (knife-edge), not inferior. C is wrong — price elasticity is NOT zero, just the income elasticity is. Answer: A — Marshallian and Hicksian coincide

\[\frac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_j} - x_j \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I}}_{= \,0} \;=\; \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_j}.\]

Bridge — the algebra dictates the taxonomy

The Slutsky equation has two sign-bearing pieces. Substitution is always \(\leq 0\) (own-price). Income can be either sign depending on \(\partial x_i^M/\partial I\). Six logical combinations — only three matter for the Marshallian curve's slope:

Normal good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I > 0.\) Income effect reinforces substitution. Marshallian demand falls when \(p_i\) rises.

Inferior good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I < 0.\) Income effect opposes substitution; sub. dominates. Marshallian still slopes down.

Giffen good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I < 0\) AND large enough that income overpowers substitution. Marshallian slopes up.

Walkthrough 6.3 — solve: sub. dominates the perverse income term

Step 1 — total Marshallian effect

At \((p_1', p_2, I) = (6, 8, 5{,}000)\): \(x_1' = 30 - 12 + 4 - 5 = 17\) cans/mo.

• Substitution: \(-2.021 \cdot 2 = -4.042\) cans

HeadsUp 6.2 — what “compensated” means

“Compensated demand” means utility is held constant, not income. Students consistently swap these.

Hicksian (compensated)

Fix the utility floor \(\bar u\). As prices change, the income required to reach \(\bar u\) changes — that's exactly \(e(p, \bar u)\). The consumer is “compensated” for the price change with whatever income keeps them on the same indifference curve.

NOT “income-held-constant”

Marshallian demand is income-held-constant: fix \(I\), let utility float. That's the consumer's actual behavior. The Hicksian thought experiment is the opposite: fix utility, let income float.

Mnemonic. Hicksian = “Hold utility”. Marshallian = “Money fixed”. The two coincide at one point (the original \((p, I)\)) and diverge as relative prices change.

The terminology trap that students fall into on every homework. "Compensated" sounds like the consumer is being kept whole financially — but it actually means kept on the same indifference curve, which requires DIFFERENT income at different prices. Use the mnemonic: Hicksian = Hold utility. Marshallian = Money fixed. Test on Problem 5.

HeadsUp 6.3 — spotting Giffen behavior in real data

Identifying a Giffen good empirically requires more than a positively sloped scatter of price vs. quantity in observational data.

Three preconditions (Jensen-Miller protocol)

  • Households near subsistence (energy/nutrition constraint binding).
  • Good is a large share of expenditure (typically > 30%).
  • Good is the cheapest source of the necessary nutrient/service.

Confounders to rule out

  • Supply shocks moving \(p\) and \(x\) along an upward-sloping supply curve.
  • Quality changes mistaken for price changes.
  • Veblen goods (status signaling) — demand rises with price for prestige, not subsistence.

The randomized-control standard. Jensen-Miller passed because they exogenously varied rice prices via subsidies, ruling out supply confounders. A scatter plot alone cannot identify Giffen.

The methodological warning. Cocktail-party economics says "Giffen goods are rare" — true. But papers that *claim* to identify Giffen often confound true Giffen behavior with supply-side moves, quality changes, or status effects. Jensen-Miller is the gold standard because they randomized — the exogenous variation in price is identifying. Math Appendix C.6 has a longer methodological discussion.

  1. The Slutsky equation decomposes any Marshallian price response into substitution (Hicksian, fixed utility) and income (Marshallian, \(x_j \cdot \partial x_i^M / \partial I\)) effects.
  2. The expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\) is concave in \(p\), non-decreasing in each argument, HOD 1 in \(p\). Its derivative in \(p_j\) is Hicksian demand — Shephard's lemma.
  3. Own-price Hicksian demand is always \(\leq 0\). Marshallian own-price effect can flip sign for inferior + budget-share-heavy + subsistence goods — the Giffen good.
  4. Maya's knife-edge: with Cobb-Douglas-log preferences, substitution and income exactly cancel on a wage change — Marshallian labor supply has zero elasticity. Real labor-supply data (Sigurdsson 2025) shows non-zero Marshallian elasticity \(\approx 0.1\); the gap is utility-function shape.
  5. Policy. Price subsidies and cash transfers are not equivalent. The Slutsky structure tells you which channel each policy targets and which households benefit most.
