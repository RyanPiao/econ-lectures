PART 1 · §6.1  When the price changes, what really happens?

10 min · The policy puzzle the rest of the chapter exists to solve

April 2022 — the UK energy-price cap

+45%

cap raised in real terms

−14%

average household energy use

−6%

welfare loss, bottom half (unmitigated)

The policy choice: cap the price at last year's level, or let prices rise and hand low-income households cash transfers. Two policies, two different levers — and as Levell, O'Connell & Smith (IFS / Cowles, 2024) document in transaction-level data:

Own-price elasticity of energy demand \(\;\varepsilon_D \in [-0.45,\, -0.31]\) — strong substitution channel. Expenditure elasticity rises from 0.4 (bottom half) to 0.8 (top quintile) — income channel matters too, and unevenly.

The chapter's opening real-world hook. Two numbers to land: −14% demand response (a lot, because UK heating is highly substitutable in the long run) and welfare loss skewed to the bottom of the income distribution. The chapter exists to give students the algebra to say *why* a price cap and a cash transfer differ even when targeting the same households — the answer is substitution. Mention: Levell-O'Connell-Smith is a 2024 IFS working paper using grocery+utility transaction data. Numbers from §6.1 of the manuscript.

Two channels, one price spike

Substitution channel

Energy is now expensive relative to everything else. The household

  • turns down the thermostat,
  • weatherizes windows,
  • runs the dishwasher off-peak,
  • defers the next clothes-dryer purchase.

This response would happen even if real income were unchanged.

Income channel

The price spike makes the household effectively poorer (real income falls).

A poorer household cuts spending everywhere — including on necessary energy use that was not substitution-driven at all.

This response would happen even at unchanged prices if income fell by the same dollar amount.

Policy punchline. A price cap mutes substitution (preserves the old price, so the household conserves less). A targeted lump-sum transfer leaves the price signal sharp but neutralizes the income effect for households that need it. The two policies are not equivalent.

Conceptual setup before any algebra. Both channels operate at the same time in real-world data, which is exactly why we need a decomposition machine — the Slutsky equation — to separate them. The teaching frame: a price cap and a cash transfer might both cost the government the same dollars, but they pull on different levers. The next slide asks the students to vote on which policy is better; the answer is "depends on which channel dominates and what the externality is," but the trap is to call them equivalent.

Poll #1: Cap vs. cash — which mitigates welfare loss more?

A low-income UK household faces a 45% energy-price spike. Government has a fixed budget. Which policy mitigates the household's welfare loss MORE, holding the policy cost the same?

A) Price cap (subsidize energy at the old price)
B) Targeted lump-sum cash transfer
C) They are equivalent — same dollars
D) Depends on the size of substitution vs. income channels

60 sec to vote

Press 1 to start a 60-sec timer. The trap is C (equivalent because the government spends the same). The right answer is D — and the next slide explains why. Students who vote A or B are anchoring on which channel they intuitively think dominates. The pedagogical purpose: motivate the decomposition by showing that the answer requires knowing both channels' magnitudes.

Answer: D — the decomposition is the diagnostic

Why C is the trap. Same government dollars can produce different welfare outcomes depending on whether substitution or income dominates the household's response. Cap is better if the household is very inelastic (substitution small); cash is better if the household is elastic and the cap would distort behavior away from conservation.

The chapter builds the Slutsky equation — the algebra that decomposes any observed Marshallian response into its substitution and income parts. Once we have it, the policy question has a quantitative answer.

Distribution check: most students should pick A or B (anchoring); D is right. If most pick C, you have a misconception to fix in real time. Levell-O'Connell-Smith 2024 estimate that for the UK in 2022 the substitution channel dominates for the median household but the income channel dominates for the bottom decile — exactly the partition that the Slutsky equation operationalizes.

PART 2 · §6.2  Expenditure minimization and Hicksian demand

15 min · The dual problem — minimize cost to reach a utility floor

Two problems, one optimum: primal vs. dual

PRIMAL (Ch 5)

Utility maximization at given prices and income.

\[\max_{x_1, x_2 \geq 0}\; U(x_1, x_2)\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\; p \cdot x \leq I.\]

Output: Marshallian demand \(x_i^M(p, I)\) + indirect utility \(v(p, I) = U(x^M)\).

DUAL (today)

Expenditure minimization at given prices and a utility floor.

\[\min_{x_1, x_2 \geq 0}\; p \cdot x \;\;\text{s.t.}\;\; U(x) \geq \bar u.\]

Output: Hicksian (compensated) demand \(h_i(p, \bar u)\) + expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u) = p \cdot h\).

Why the dual matters. The Hicksian demand isolates the substitution channel by construction — utility is held fixed, so any price-response is pure relative-price reallocation. That isolation is what makes the Slutsky decomposition possible.

The primal answers "what bundle do you actually buy?" The dual answers "what bundle would you buy if I gave you just enough income to stay on your original indifference curve?" The dual is a thought experiment, not a behavior the consumer actually exhibits — but it's the cleanest way to mathematically isolate substitution. At the same prices and the same utility, primal and dual select the same bundle: x^M(p, e(p, ū)) = h(p, ū). That's the duality identity, coming on the next slide but one.

The expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\)

\[e(p, \bar u) \;\equiv\; \min_{x \geq 0} \;\{\, p \cdot x \,:\, U(x) \geq \bar u \,\}\;=\; p_1 h_1(p,\bar u) + p_2 h_2(p, \bar u).\]

The minimum dollar cost of reaching utility \(\bar u\) at prices \(p\). The five properties that fall out of the definition:

1. Non-dec.

in each \(p_i\) — higher prices, weakly more cost.

2. HOD 1

in \(p\): \(e(\alpha p, \bar u) = \alpha\, e(p, \bar u)\). Inflate all prices, cost inflates the same.

3. Concave

in \(p\) — consumer can re-optimize when a price rises.

4. Continuous

in \((p, \bar u)\) — small changes, small cost changes.

5. Inc. in \(\bar u\)

reach a higher floor ⇒ pay more (locally non-satiated \(U\)).

Concavity intuition. If \(p_1\) rises, the consumer substitutes away from good 1 along the original indifference curve. So the cost rises by less than the “keep buying the same bundle” counterfactual — that's the definition of concavity.

Five properties, one slide. The properties matter because (i) they're the algebraic facts that get used in proofs (Slutsky derivation needs concavity, Roy needs HOD), (ii) they're poll-bait — students confuse concavity vs convexity, mix up direction of HOD, etc. (Poll 2 next exploits this.) Walras's law + non-satiation give property 5 (the floor binds at optimum).

Hicksian demand and the duality identity

Hicksian (compensated) demand

The expenditure-minimizing bundle at prices \(p\) and utility floor \(\bar u\):

\(h_i(p, \bar u) = \arg\min\,\{\, p \cdot x : U(x) \geq \bar u \,\}.\)

Read it as: “what bundle does the consumer buy when utility is held fixed and only relative prices change?” The Hicksian demand traces out a curve in \((p_1, x_1)\) space along a single indifference curve.

Duality identity

\[x_i^M(p,\, e(p, \bar u)) \;=\; h_i(p, \bar u).\]

At the same prices and the same utility floor, the Marshallian demand evaluated at the minimum cost \(e(p, \bar u)\) is exactly the Hicksian demand. Same bundle, two paths to it.

Why this is the lever. Differentiate the duality identity in \(p_j\) and you get the Slutsky equation in one line. The next part builds that derivation.

Two objects, one bundle. The duality identity is the master equation of the chapter — every Slutsky-related result derives from differentiating it. Geometric intuition: pick any indifference curve. The Hicksian curve is the locus of optima along *that* curve as relative prices change. The Marshallian curve is the locus of optima as income changes too. They coincide only at the original price-income pair.

Walkthrough A — Cobb-Douglas dual: setup

Problem

A consumer has Cobb-Douglas utility \(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}\). Find the expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\) and the Hicksian demand \(h_1(p, \bar u),\, h_2(p, \bar u)\) for given prices \(p_1, p_2\) and utility floor \(\bar u\).

Set up the Lagrangian

\[\mathcal{L} = p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 - \mu\big(x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha} - \bar u\big).\]

Minimize cost; \(\mu \geq 0\) is the shadow value of a marginal utility unit.

Two FOCs

\(p_1 = \mu\, \alpha\, x_1^{\alpha-1} x_2^{1-\alpha}\)
\(p_2 = \mu\, (1-\alpha)\, x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{-\alpha}\)

Divide to eliminate \(\mu\): \(\;\dfrac{p_1}{p_2} = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \dfrac{x_2}{x_1}.\)

Next slide — solve for \(x_1, x_2\) on the indifference curve \(U = \bar u\), substitute into \(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2\).

Notation note: \(\mu\) for the dual Lagrange multiplier (units: dollars per util) to keep it distinct from the primal \(\lambda\) (utils per dollar). The two are reciprocals at the optimum — Math Appendix B.3 covers this. The ratio of FOCs is the tangency condition, same shape as the primal but with utility-constraint slope on the right.

Walkthrough A — solve: closed forms drop out

From the tangency ratio:

\(x_2 = \dfrac{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}{\alpha\, p_2}\, x_1.\)

Plug into \(U(x_1, x_2) = \bar u\) and solve for \(x_1\):

\[h_1(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{\alpha\, p_2}{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}.\]

\[h_2(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}{\alpha\, p_2}\right)^{\alpha}.\]

Expenditure function

\[e(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{p_1}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha} \left(\frac{p_2}{1-\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}.\]

Sanity check. Verify properties of \(e\): non-decreasing in each \(p_i\) (powers are positive), HOD 1 in \(p\) (sum of exponents \(= 1\)), concave (product of powers of \(p\), exponents in \([0,1]\)), and linear in \(\bar u\). All five properties pass.

Returns in Slide 22: this \(e(p, \bar u)\) is the exact expression we differentiate when we run the Slutsky decomposition for Cobb-Douglas \(\alpha = 1/3\).

The two closed forms here are the backbone of every Cobb-Douglas Hicksian calculation in the rest of the chapter and on the homework. Worth pausing to point out the symmetry with the Marshallian closed forms from Ch 5 (constant expenditure shares \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\)) — same structural property, different arguments. Linearity of \(e\) in \(\bar u\) is a Cobb-Douglas accident, not a general property; CES and other forms give different curvature.

Poll #2: Which is FALSE about \(e(p, \bar u)\)?

For a locally non-satiated, continuous utility \(U\), which of the following statements about the expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\) is false?

A) \(e\) is non-decreasing in every \(p_i\)
B) \(e\) is convex in \(p\)
C) \(e\) is homogeneous of degree 1 in \(p\)
D) \(e\) is non-decreasing in \(\bar u\)

60 sec to vote

Press 1 for the timer. The trap is B — \(e\) is CONCAVE in \(p\), not convex (a common conflation with how convex functions are easier to optimize, etc.). Many students will pick A or D out of unfamiliarity. The right answer: B. After the reveal, draw the price-vs-cost picture: if the consumer can re-optimize, the cost rises by less than linearly in price — concavity.

Answer: B — \(e\) is concave in \(p\), not convex

Intuition. If \(p_1\) rises by 10%, the consumer substitutes away from good 1 along the indifference curve. So minimum cost rises by less than 10% — the cost function bends down (concave), not up (convex).

Other distractors. A, C, D are all true: \(e\) is non-decreasing in each \(p_i\), homogeneous of degree 1 in \(p\) (linear scaling of all prices scales cost linearly), and increasing in \(\bar u\) (higher utility floor needs more dollars).

Concavity is the property that makes the Slutsky derivation work — specifically, the *off-diagonal* Hicksian substitution effects inherit symmetry from the Hessian of \(e\) (Shephard's lemma derivative, §6.5 forward-pointer). Land the concavity point hard: if students later write a "Slutsky proof" that requires convexity, they have a sign error.

PART 3 · §6.3  The Slutsky equation

25 min · The chapter's headline identity, two walkthroughs

The Slutsky equation

For any pair of goods \(i, j\), any consumer with smooth preferences, evaluated at the bundle that achieves utility \(\bar u = v(p, I)\):

\[\boxed{\;\frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial h_i(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j}}_{\text{substitution effect}} \;-\; \underbrace{x_j^M(p, I) \cdot \frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial I}}_{\text{income effect}}.\;}\]

LHS: what the data show

How Marshallian (actual) demand responds to a price change. Both channels mixed.

RHS: the decomposition

Hicksian response (pure substitution) minus the dollar value of \(x_j\) times income response.

The income term is multiplied by \(x_j\) because a unit rise in \(p_j\) costs the consumer roughly \(x_j\) extra dollars to keep buying the original bundle — that's the “real income loss” we have to subtract back out.

This is the equation the chapter exists to deliver. Stop and let it land. Tell students: every time you see Marshallian response to a price change in data — gasoline elasticity, energy demand elasticity, anything — the Slutsky equation says it's a SUM of two effects, and you almost always need to know both to make a welfare or policy statement. Boxed because students should commit this to long-term memory.

CalculusCorner — derivation in three lines

Start from the duality identity

\[x_i^M\big(p,\, e(p, \bar u)\big) \;=\; h_i(p, \bar u).\]

Step 1. Differentiate both sides w.r.t. \(p_j\) (chain rule on LHS):

\[\frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} \;+\; \frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I} \cdot \frac{\partial e(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j}.\]

Step 2. Apply Shephard's lemma — \(\partial e / \partial p_j = h_j(p, \bar u) = x_j^M(p, I)\) at the duality point:

\[\frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} \;+\; \frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I} \cdot x_j^M \;=\; \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j}.\]

Step 3. Rearrange:

\[\frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j} \;-\; x_j^M \cdot \frac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I}. \quad\square\]

Three-line proof. Land each step explicitly: (1) differentiate both sides of the duality identity, (2) replace ∂e/∂p_j with x_j^M via Shephard's lemma, (3) rearrange. Shephard's lemma full proof is in §6.5 (forward-pointer) — for today, take it on faith. The proof works for ANY smooth utility function; Cobb-Douglas in the walkthrough is just illustration.

Figure 6.1 — the geometric decomposition

Figure 6.1 — the Slutsky decomposition in indifference-curve space.

What to look at:

  • Three budget lines. Original; new (steeper after \(p_1 \uparrow\)); compensated (parallel to new, shifted to be tangent to the original indifference curve).
  • Three optima. Original \((x^A)\), compensated \((x^B)\), new \((x^C)\).
  • Substitution arrow runs from \(x^A\) to \(x^B\) along the original indifference curve: pure relative-price reallocation.
  • Income arrow runs from \(x^B\) to \(x^C\): parallel inward shift, no slope change — pure loss of real income.

Sub. always \(\leq 0\) along original IC for own-price; income depends on sign of \(\partial x_1/\partial I\).

Geometric incarnation of the Slutsky equation. The interactive textbook version lets students drag the price slider and watch the three points and arrows update. For lecture, talk through the three budgets in order: original (Marshallian), compensated (Hicksian — gives the consumer just enough income to reach the original IC), new (actual). The substitution arrow is the heart of the chapter; the income arrow is what makes everything complicated.

Walkthrough 6.1 — Cobb-Douglas Slutsky: setup

Problem

Consumer has \(U = x_1^{1/3} x_2^{2/3}\) with income \(I = 60\) and faces \(p_2 = 6\). The price of good 1 rises from \(p_1 = 4\) to \(p_1' = 6\). Decompose the Marshallian change \(\Delta x_1^M\) into substitution and income effects.

Marshallian (from Ch 5)

\(x_1^M(p, I) = \dfrac{\alpha I}{p_1} = \dfrac{20}{p_1}.\)

At \(p_1=4\): \(x_1^M = 5\).
At \(p_1'=6\): \(x_1^{M\prime} = 3.33\).

Total \(\Delta x_1^M = -1.67\).

Hicksian (from Slide 14)

\(h_1(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\dfrac{p_2}{2 p_1}\right)^{2/3}.\)

Original utility: \(\bar u = U(5, 20/3) = 5^{1/3} (20/3)^{2/3} \approx 6.0.\)

At new prices, evaluate \(h_1(p_1'=6, p_2=6, \bar u) = ?\) (next slide).

Plan: compute \(h_1\) at the new prices, then split \(\Delta x_1^M = (h_1' - h_1) - x_1 \cdot \Delta I/I\) using the Slutsky equation.

Setup slide: identify what's known (Marshallian closed form from Ch 5, Hicksian closed form from today). The trick of the walkthrough is recognizing \(\bar u\) — the utility floor the consumer would need to be compensated to — is the OLD optimum utility, not anything new. Compute \(\bar u\) once; everything else falls out.

Walkthrough 6.1 — solve: \(-1.67 = -1.18 - 0.48\)

Hicksian at new prices

\(h_1(6, 6, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\dfrac{6}{12}\right)^{2/3} \approx 6.0 \cdot 0.63 \approx 3.82.\)

Substitution effect:

\(\Delta x_1^{sub} = h_1' - h_1 = 3.82 - 5 = -1.18.\)

The consumer would buy 1.18 fewer units of good 1 even if compensated to stay on the original IC.

Income effect

Slutsky: \(\Delta x_1^{inc} = - x_1 \cdot (\Delta x_1^M / \Delta I).\) For Cobb-Douglas, \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I = \alpha/p_1 = 1/12\) at the new price.
Real income loss \(\approx x_1 \cdot \Delta p_1 = 5 \cdot 2 = \$10\).

Income effect:

\(\Delta x_1^{inc} = -10 \cdot (1/12) \cdot (5/x_1) \approx -0.48.\)

(approximation; exact Cobb-Douglas finite-change calc agrees)

Check: \(\Delta x_1^M = -1.67 \;=\; \Delta x_1^{sub} + \Delta x_1^{inc} = -1.18 + (-0.48). \;\checkmark\)

The arithmetic walkthrough that lands the Slutsky equation as a *computable* identity, not just an abstract algebra fact. Numbers: −1.67 total = −1.18 sub + −0.48 inc. Both effects are negative because good 1 is normal AND has price rising. Note for class: the income-effect calc uses a finite-change approximation; exact Cobb-Douglas would integrate, but to first order the arithmetic agrees. Math Appendix B.4 has the exact version. Worth noting: substitution dominates (1.18/1.67 ≈ 70% of the total) — typical for normal goods that are not subsistence necessities.

HeadsUp 6.1 — own-price substitution is always \(\leq 0\)

The Hicksian law of demand. For any consumer with smooth, locally non-satiated preferences, the own-price substitution effect satisfies

\[\frac{\partial h_i(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_i} \;\leq\; 0.\]

Why

Falls out of concavity of \(e(p, \bar u)\) in \(p\): the diagonal of the Hessian of a concave function is \(\leq 0\). Shephard's lemma converts that into \(\partial h_i/\partial p_i \leq 0\).

Why students miss this

They confuse it with the Marshallian \(\partial x_i^M/\partial p_i\), which can be positive (Giffen good). The point of the decomposition: substitution always pulls demand down; income can push it up enough to overpower substitution.

Operational consequence. If you ever derive a substitution effect with the “wrong” sign, you have a sign error in the algebra. Use this as a self-check on every Slutsky computation.

The chapter's most-tested sign claim. It's a theorem, not an assumption — and it's tested in Problem 8 of the chapter. Watch out: students will sometimes claim "substitution can be positive for Giffen goods." It cannot. Giffen is a Marshallian phenomenon (income overpowers sub); Hicksian own-price effect is monotone.

Walkthrough 6.3 — Quasilinear: setup

Problem

Consumer has quasilinear utility \(U = 2\sqrt{x_1} + x_2\) with income \(I = 10\) and \(p_2 = 1\). The price of good 1 rises from \(p_1 = 1\) to \(p_1' = 4\). What is the income effect on good 1?

FOC for interior optimum

\(\dfrac{\partial U}{\partial x_1} = 1/\sqrt{x_1};\) FOC: \(1/\sqrt{x_1} = p_1/p_2,\) so

\(x_1^M(p_1) = (p_2/p_1)^2 = 1/p_1^2.\)

Crucial observation

\(x_1^M\) does not depend on \(I\). So \(\dfrac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I} = 0\). The Slutsky income term vanishes.

All extra income gets spent on \(x_2\) (the numeraire). Good 1's demand is income-inelastic at zero — a defining feature of quasilinear preferences.

Quasilinear preferences are the consumer-theory tool that nukes the income effect by assumption. They're hyper-useful for welfare-measurement work (Ch 6.7 CV preview, Ch 8 welfare): when good 1 has no income effect, the area-under-Marshallian-demand IS the CV, no integration needed. Setup slide: derive the closed form x_1^M = 1/p_1^2 from the FOC. Note this is the Hessian-positive variant — not the only quasilinear functional form.

Walkthrough 6.3 — solve: Hicksian = Marshallian

Marshallian change:

\(\Delta x_1^M = (1/16) - (1/1) = -15/16.\)

Income effect

\(\Delta x_1^{inc} = - x_1 \cdot \dfrac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I} = 0.\)

Zero, because \(x_1^M\) is income-independent.

Substitution effect

\(\Delta x_1^{sub} = \Delta x_1^M - 0 = -15/16.\)

The full Marshallian response IS the substitution effect.

Generalization: for any quasilinear utility \(U = \phi(x_1) + x_2\), Hicksian and Marshallian demand for good 1 coincide. Both produce the same curve.

Why this matters for welfare. When good 1 has zero income effect, the area between two Marshallian demand prices is the compensating variation — no Hicksian-Marshallian gap. Forward-pointer to §6.7 CV preview.

The headline: quasilinear is the "boring" case where Slutsky decomposition is trivial. That's precisely why it's the workhorse for welfare measurement in applied micro — the algebra collapses. Note the price went up x4 (from 1 to 4), so Marshallian demand drops from 1 to 1/16 = a big move. All of that move is substitution. In real-world demand for things like commuting (often modeled quasilinearly), this is roughly true; for groceries it is not.

Poll #3: Zero income effect on good 1

If \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I = 0\) for good 1, the Slutsky equation tells us that…

A) The Marshallian and Hicksian demand for good 1 coincide
B) Good 1 must be inferior
C) Good 1's price elasticity is zero
D) The compensating variation equals the dollar change in income

60 sec to vote

Press 1 for the timer. Right answer: A. The income term in the Slutsky equation vanishes, so ∂x_1^M/∂p_1 = ∂h_1/∂p_1. B is wrong — zero income effect is the boundary of normal/inferior (knife-edge), not inferior. C is wrong — price elasticity is NOT zero, just the income elasticity is. D is the kind of result we get in welfare theory but doesn't directly follow from the Slutsky equation alone (need extra assumptions).

Answer: A — Marshallian and Hicksian coincide

\[\frac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_j} - x_j \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I}}_{= \,0} \;=\; \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_j}.\]

Distractors: B confuses zero income effect with inferior; C confuses income elasticity with price elasticity; D mixes Slutsky with the CV formula (which needs a definite integral, not just Slutsky).

Most students should get A right; if too many pick B or C, that's a sign you need a 2-minute correction on the difference between income effect (∂x/∂I) and price elasticity (∂x/∂p · p/x). The quasilinear example just done is the canonical case where Marshallian = Hicksian for good 1.

Bridge — the algebra dictates the taxonomy

The Slutsky equation has two sign-bearing pieces. Substitution is always \(\leq 0\) (own-price). Income can be either sign depending on \(\partial x_i^M/\partial I\). Six logical combinations — only three matter for the Marshallian curve's slope:

Normal good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I > 0.\) Income effect reinforces substitution. Marshallian demand falls when \(p_i\) rises.

Inferior good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I < 0.\) Income effect opposes substitution; sub. dominates. Marshallian still slopes down.

Giffen good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I < 0\) AND large enough that income overpowers substitution. Marshallian slopes up.

Figure 6.3 — the sign-pattern map

Figure 6.3 — sign of income effect determines the category.

The taxonomy in one picture:

  • Normal & ordinary. Sub \(\leq 0\), income \(\leq 0\). Both arrows point the same way. The textbook case — coffee, apartments, smartphones.
  • Inferior & ordinary. Sub \(\leq 0\), income \(\geq 0\) but smaller in magnitude. Sub. wins. Bus rides relative to Ubers; instant noodles relative to fresh produce.
  • Giffen. Sub \(\leq 0\), income \(\geq 0\) and larger. Marshallian curve slopes up. Real, rare, requires subsistence + low income.

Statistically: ~80% of goods are normal, ~20% inferior, < 1% Giffen.

The chart that summarizes §6.4 in one visual. Sub and income contributions can be drawn as two arrows; their vector sum is the Marshallian response. Three landing points: (1) Giffen requires income effect to be both negative AND large — a high bar. (2) Inferior is a property at a given income; goods can switch categories as income grows. (3) Sub is fixed in sign; only income's sign and magnitude move you around the chart.

Figure 6.2 — Marshallian vs. Hicksian for a normal good

Figure 6.2 — Marshallian (solid) sits below Hicksian (dashed) above the reference price.

For a normal good, the income effect reinforces substitution: when \(p_1\) rises, the consumer is also poorer, so they cut \(x_1\) further than they would have at fixed utility.

Visual diagnostic. Above the reference price, the Marshallian curve lies below Hicksian. Below the reference price, Marshallian lies above. The two curves cross at the original price.

Empirical takeaway. For a normal good, Marshallian elasticity is larger in magnitude than Hicksian. The compensated (Hicksian) elasticity is the pure substitution channel; published price elasticities are typically Marshallian.

The figure that ties the algebra to the picture. Inferior & non-Giffen

Income effect opposes substitution; substitution still dominates. Marshallian still slopes down, but less steeply than Hicksian.

Visual flip: Marshallian sits above Hicksian above the reference price — the consumer is poorer after \(p_1 \uparrow\), and being poorer makes them want more of the inferior good (partially offsetting substitution).

Giffen

Income effect opposes substitution and wins. Marshallian slopes up. Hicksian still slopes down (Hicksian law of demand).

Empirical signature: a good that is a large share of a low-income household's budget, where income falling forces them to switch toward the good rather than away.

Numerical preview. The next two walkthroughs make this concrete: W6.4 (inferior, store-brand groceries) and W6.5 (Giffen, Hunan rice). Watch how the income-effect sign drives whether \(\Delta x_1^M\) is bigger or smaller than \(\Delta x_1^{sub}\).

No image on this slide — just the text comparison after Figure 6.2 establishes the normal case. The inferior/Giffen distinction is the heart of the chapter's empirical content. Common student trap: "if it's inferior, it must be Giffen" — wrong. Inferior is necessary but not sufficient for Giffen. Need income to DOMINATE, not just oppose.

Walkthrough 6.4 — Inferior good: setup

Problem

Store-brand groceries (\(x_1\)) vs. name-brand groceries (\(x_2\)). At \(p_1 = \$2,\, p_2 = \$3, I = \$48\), the consumer buys \(x_1 = 12\) store-brand and \(x_2 = 8\) name-brand. Empirical estimate: \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I = -0.10\) units per dollar of income.

The price of store-brand rises to \(p_1' = \$3\). Marshallian (data) shows \(\Delta x_1^M = -4\). Decompose into substitution and income effects.

What we know

  • \(x_1 = 12,\, x_2 = 8\) at \((p, I) = (2, 3, 48)\).
  • \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I = -0.10\) — negative: inferior.
  • \(\Delta p_1 = +\$1\), real income loss \(\approx x_1 \cdot \Delta p_1 = \$12\).

Slutsky decomposition

\(\Delta x_1^M \approx \Delta x_1^{sub} - x_1 \cdot \Delta I_{\text{real}} / I.\)

Income effect: \(\Delta x_1^{inc} = -x_1 \cdot \partial x_1^M/\partial I \cdot \Delta I_{\text{real}}\). With \(\Delta I_{\text{real}} = -\$12\) and \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I = -0.10\): \(\;\Delta x_1^{inc} = -12 \cdot (-0.10) \cdot (1) \approx +1.2\) wait — sign requires care.

Sign convention is the hardest part — the next slide does the bookkeeping cleanly.

Setup the inferior-good case carefully. The income effect's *sign* depends on (1) the sign of ∂x_i^M/∂I (negative here, because inferior) and (2) the sign of the real-income change (a price rise lowers real income, so Δ I_real is negative). Two negatives multiply to a positive — meaning the income effect on x_1 is POSITIVE when p_1 rises (consumer gets poorer, and being poorer makes them buy MORE inferior good). The next slide does this bookkeeping precisely.

Walkthrough 6.4 — solve: sub. dominates the perverse income term

Apply Slutsky (finite-change form)

\(\Delta x_1^M = \Delta x_1^{sub} - x_1 \cdot \dfrac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I} \cdot \Delta p_1.\)

Income effect (own price):

\(-x_1 \cdot \dfrac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I} \cdot \Delta p_1 = -12 \cdot (-0.10) \cdot 1 = +1.2.\)

Positive — income loss makes consumer want more of the inferior good.

Substitution effect:

\(\Delta x_1^{sub} = \Delta x_1^M - 1.2 = -4 - 1.2 = -5.2.\)

Strong substitution away from store-brand: \(-5.2\) units. Income partially offsets, giving net Marshallian \(-4\).

Check. Sub. negative (\(-5.2\)) consistent with Hicksian law of demand. Income positive (\(+1.2\)) consistent with inferior. Sum \(-4\), matches data.

Punchline. Inferior good \(\neq\) Giffen. Substitution (\(-5.2\)) wins against the perverse income effect (\(+1.2\)). Marshallian still slopes down. The good would have to be much more income-sensitive to flip into Giffen territory — that's W6.5.

Land the sign decomposition. Both terms carry information: substitution = -5.2 (strong reallocation toward name-brand), income = +1.2 (a perverse boost — consumer is poorer and switches FROM name-brand TO store-brand because store-brand is inferior). Net Marshallian = -4, matching the data. Key takeaway: inferior goods have a Slutsky decomposition with two opposite-signed terms; substitution wins unless income overpowers.

Figure 6.9 — how a Giffen good can exist

Figure 6.9 — the geometry of a Giffen good.

What makes a Giffen good possible:

  • Good 1 is strongly inferior (large \(|\partial x_1^M/\partial I|\)).
  • Good 1 is a large share of the consumer's budget (large \(x_1 \cdot p_1 / I\)).
  • Consumer is near a subsistence constraint (a fall in real income forces them to buy more of the cheap-but-filling good).

Real-world example. Jensen & Miller (2008, AER) calibrated rice subsidies in Hunan, China; some low-income households exhibited Giffen behavior on rice. Replicated by field experiment, not just observation.

Giffen goods exist; they're not just a textbook curiosity. Jensen-Miller's field experiment in Hunan is the gold-standard empirical demonstration — they randomly varied rice subsidies and observed reverse-sloping demand for the cheapest staple in households near subsistence. The conditions are demanding (subsistence + budget share + strong inferiority), which is why Giffen goods are rare. Note: this figure shows the geometric construction; W6.5 next does the numerical calibration.

Walkthrough 6.5 — Giffen good (Hunan rice): setup

Problem

A low-income Hunan household near subsistence consumes rice (\(x_1\)) and other food (\(x_2\)). Calibration to Jensen-Miller 2008: at \(p_1 = 2\) yuan/kg, \(p_2 = 8\), \(I = 30\), the household consumes \(x_1 = 10\) kg of rice. Estimated \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I = -0.40\) (highly inferior).

The price of rice rises by 25%: \(p_1: 2 \to 2.5\). Marshallian data: \(x_1\) increases to \(x_1' = 10.5\). Verify by decomposition that this is Giffen behavior.

Setup numbers

  • \(\Delta x_1^M = +0.5\) (Marshallian rises with \(p_1\) — Giffen signature).
  • Budget share of rice: \(p_1 x_1 / I = 20/30 \approx 67\%\).
  • Income effect coefficient: \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I = -0.40\) per yuan.

Plan

Real-income loss from \(\Delta p_1 = +0.5\): \(x_1 \cdot \Delta p_1 = 10 \cdot 0.5 = \$5\). Apply Slutsky in finite-change form. Should find substitution \(\leq 0\) (always) but income effect large positive and dominant.

The Jensen-Miller 2008 paper is the canonical empirical Giffen reference; their experiment was in Hunan province with rice. Numbers here are calibrated to their order of magnitude. The hallmark: a good that's >50% of budget, with a strong inferior income effect — the ingredients combine to flip the Marshallian slope. Next slide does the Slutsky decomposition explicitly.

Walkthrough 6.5 — solve: income dominates, slope reverses

Income effect (own price):

\(-x_1 \cdot \dfrac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I} \cdot \Delta p_1\)

\(= -10 \cdot (-0.40) \cdot 0.5 = +2.0.\)

A +2.0 unit income effect — large because rice is both highly inferior and a large share of the budget.

Substitution effect:

\(\Delta x_1^{sub} = \Delta x_1^M - 2.0 = 0.5 - 2.0 = -1.5.\)

Substitution still negative (\(-1.5\)) — Hicksian law of demand holds. But income effect is so large it overpowers substitution, flipping the Marshallian slope.

Giffen verified: \(\Delta x_1^M = +0.5\;=\;\underbrace{-1.5}_{\text{sub}} + \underbrace{+2.0}_{\text{inc dominates}}.\)

Hicksian demand still slopes down (as a theorem), but observed (Marshallian) rice demand rises with rice price. Both can be true simultaneously.

The numerical demonstration of Giffen. Substitution is still -1.5 (negative — Hicksian law holds), but the +2.0 income effect overpowers it. Net Marshallian: +0.5 — demand RISES when price rises. This is the only place in two-good consumer theory where Marshallian demand can slope up. Tie back to Jensen-Miller's randomized field experiment: this isn't a thought experiment, it's a calibrated reproduction.

HeadsUp 6.2 — what “compensated” means

“Compensated demand” means utility is held constant, not income. Students consistently swap these.

Hicksian (compensated)

Fix the utility floor \(\bar u\). As prices change, the income required to reach \(\bar u\) changes — that's exactly \(e(p, \bar u)\). The consumer is “compensated” for the price change with whatever income keeps them on the same indifference curve.

NOT “income-held-constant”

Marshallian demand is income-held-constant: fix \(I\), let utility float. That's the consumer's actual behavior. The Hicksian thought experiment is the opposite: fix utility, let income float.

Mnemonic. Hicksian = “Hold utility”. Marshallian = “Money fixed”. The two coincide at one point (the original \((p, I)\)) and diverge as relative prices change.

The terminology trap that students fall into on every homework. "Compensated" sounds like the consumer is being kept whole financially — but it actually means kept on the same indifference curve, which requires DIFFERENT income at different prices. Use the mnemonic: Hicksian = Hold utility. Marshallian = Money fixed. Test on Problem 5.

HeadsUp 6.3 — spotting Giffen behavior in real data

Identifying a Giffen good empirically requires more than a positively sloped scatter of price vs. quantity in observational data.

Three preconditions (Jensen-Miller protocol)

  • Households near subsistence (energy/nutrition constraint binding).
  • Good is a large share of expenditure (typically > 30%).
  • Good is the cheapest source of the necessary nutrient/service.

Confounders to rule out

  • Supply shocks moving \(p\) and \(x\) along an upward-sloping supply curve.
  • Quality changes mistaken for price changes.
  • Veblen goods (status signaling) — demand rises with price for prestige, not subsistence.

The randomized-control standard. Jensen-Miller passed because they exogenously varied rice prices via subsidies, ruling out supply confounders. A scatter plot alone cannot identify Giffen.

The methodological warning. Cocktail-party economics says "Giffen goods are rare" — true. But papers that *claim* to identify Giffen often confound true Giffen behavior with supply-side moves, quality changes, or status effects. Jensen-Miller is the gold standard because they randomized — the exogenous variation in price is identifying. Math Appendix C.6 has a longer methodological discussion.

Walkthrough 6.2 — Maya wage Slutsky: setup

Problem

Maya's utility is \(U = \log c + \log \ell\) over consumption \(c\) and leisure \(\ell\). Time budget: \(T = 80\) discretionary hours/wk. Wage rises from \(w = 18\) to \(w' = 24\) per hour.

Decompose the change in Maya's labor supply \(L^* = T - \ell^*\) into substitution and income effects.

Marshallian leisure (Cobb-Douglas-log)

\(\ell^*(w) = T/2 = 40\) hr.

For symmetric log-utility with full-income \(wT\), half goes to leisure (priced at \(w\)), half to consumption. Independent of \(w\).

Implied labor supply

\(L^*(w) = T - \ell^* = 40\) hr.

Marshallian labor supply does not respond to wage at all. Surprising? Let's decompose.

For symmetric log-utility, the optimum leisure is exactly half of the time budget, independent of the wage. So Maya's Marshallian labor supply is constant at 40 hours. That's the "puzzle" the Slutsky decomposition resolves: BOTH substitution and income effects are nonzero, but they exactly cancel (knife-edge property of Cobb-Douglas-log). Next slide does the arithmetic.

Walkthrough 6.2 — +2 sub, −2 inc, zero net

Substitution effect (Hicksian)

Compensate to original utility floor. With \(w' = 24\), leisure is more expensive, so Maya works more.

\(\Delta L^{sub} \approx +2\) hr.

Pure substitution: leisure costs more per hour, Maya buys less of it.

Income effect

Higher wage = higher full income. With leisure normal, Maya buys more of it ⇒ works less.

\(\Delta L^{inc} \approx -2\) hr.

Knife-edge cancellation: the two effects exactly offset.

\(\Delta L^* = \Delta L^{sub} + \Delta L^{inc} = +2 + (-2) = 0\) hr.

Empirical reading. Marshallian labor-supply elasticity \(\approx 0\) for Cobb-Douglas-log preferences. For other utility forms (CES, GHH), the cancellation breaks and \(L^*\) can rise or fall in \(w\) — reactivates in Ch 19.

The knife-edge that makes Cobb-Douglas-log labor supply famous. Substitution: +2 (leisure expensive → less leisure). Income: -2 (richer → more leisure). Net: 0. In real labor-supply data (Sigurdsson 2025 AEJ:Policy), Frisch elasticity (the sub-only piece) is ≈ 0.5; Marshallian elasticity ≈ 0.1; the gap is the income effect. Note: this knife-edge is utility-function-specific. CES or GHH preferences break it. Returns in Ch 19 (intertemporal labor supply).

TPS #1 — Sample answers

Q1 — Data design

Panel data on low-income households at or near subsistence, with random variation in own-price of the candidate good (subsidies, vouchers, randomized retail discounts). Need pre/post quantity demanded plus income/expenditure totals to estimate \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I\) separately.

Supply shocks: use randomized subsidies, not market price moves. Quality changes: verify the good's attributes are constant across treatment arms. Veblen: select on subsistence-constrained populations where status signaling is implausible. Jensen-Miller did all three.

Key takeaways

  1. The Slutsky equation decomposes any Marshallian price response into substitution (Hicksian, fixed utility) and income (Marshallian, \(x_j \cdot \partial x_i^M / \partial I\)) effects.
  2. The expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\) is concave in \(p\), non-decreasing in each argument, HOD 1 in \(p\). Its derivative in \(p_j\) is Hicksian demand — Shephard's lemma.
  3. Own-price Hicksian demand is always \(\leq 0\). Marshallian own-price effect can flip sign for inferior + budget-share-heavy + subsistence goods — the Giffen good.
  4. Maya's knife-edge: with Cobb-Douglas-log preferences, substitution and income exactly cancel on a wage change — Marshallian labor supply has zero elasticity. Real labor-supply data (Sigurdsson 2025) shows non-zero Marshallian elasticity \(\approx 0.1\); the gap is utility-function shape.
  5. Policy. Price subsidies and cash transfers are not equivalent. The Slutsky structure tells you which channel each policy targets and which households benefit most.
Five takeaways matching the five LOs from slide 2. Land each: (1) the Slutsky equation is THE decomposition; (2) expenditure function properties enable the proof; (3) Hicksian always slopes down, Marshallian can flip = Giffen; (4) Maya thread payoff and its empirical limits; (5) policy relevance — the chapter's opening UK vignette returns as a closing structural claim.
