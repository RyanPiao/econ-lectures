A price spike shrinks the whole budget — not just energy

A higher energy price pivots the budget line inward around the unchanged “Other goods” intercept: income is the same, but each unit of energy now costs more.

  • The shaded wedge of bundles is now unaffordable.
  • To keep buying energy, the household must give up other goods — the bill reaches beyond the meter.
  • That squeeze on everything else is the income effect; a cash transfer pushes the whole line back out.

Two policies, two motions: a price cap kills the pivot (substitution); a cash transfer shifts the line out (income).

Build off the vignette. Draw the pivot live if you like: BC1 is the pre-shock line; raising p_energy rotates it clockwise around the vertical intercept to BC2. Key point students miss — an energy-price increase is NOT confined to the energy budget. Because income is fixed, buying the same energy leaves less for everything else, so the feasible set for "other goods" shrinks too (the shaded wedge). Geometrically: substitution effect ≈ the compensated pivot; income effect ≈ the parallel inward loss of purchasing power. This sets up why a price cap (kills the pivot) and a cash transfer (shifts the line back out) are different instruments — the whole point of §6.1.

Two channels, one price spike

Substitution channel

Energy is now expensive relative to everything else. The household

  • turns down the thermostat,
  • weatherizes windows,
  • runs the dishwasher off-peak,
  • defers the next clothes-dryer purchase.

This response would happen even if real income were unchanged.

Income channel

The price spike makes the household effectively poorer (real income falls).

A poorer household cuts spending everywhere — including on necessary energy use that was not substitution-driven at all.

This response would happen even at unchanged prices if income fell by the same dollar amount.

Policy punchline. A price cap mutes substitution (preserves the old price, so the household conserves less). A targeted lump-sum transfer leaves the price signal sharp but neutralizes the income effect for households that need it. The two policies are not equivalent.

Conceptual setup before any algebra. Both channels operate at the same time in real-world data, which is exactly why we need a decomposition machine — the Slutsky equation — to separate them. The teaching frame: a price cap and a cash transfer might both cost the government the same dollars, but they pull on different levers. The next slide asks the students to vote on which policy is better; the answer is "depends on which channel dominates and what the externality is," but the trap is to call them equivalent.

The same two channels — in symbols

Substitution effect

\(p_e \uparrow \;\Longrightarrow\; Q_e^{\,d} \downarrow\)

Energy is dearer relative to other goods, so the household substitutes away from it. Always this direction — the compensated move, with real income held fixed.

The guaranteed-negative piece behind the law of demand.

Income effect

A dearer energy bill erodes real income — the same paycheck buys less:

\$5,000 \$4,000 in real purchasing power

even if every price held constant:

\(Q_e^{\,d} \downarrow \quad \text{and} \quad Q_{\text{other}}^{\,d} \downarrow\)

Both fall for normal goods; if energy were inferior the income effect flips (the Giffen case, §6.2).

Total move = substitution + income. The demand response you actually observe stacks the always-negative pivot on top of the real-income loss — and that sum is the Slutsky equation, next.

Board-work companion to the poster. Substitution effect: the relative price of energy rises, so substitute away — Q_e falls. This is the compensated (utility-held-fixed) move, and it is ALWAYS negative. Theory-course framing: the "law of demand" is the TOTAL observed effect; the substitution effect is only its guaranteed-negative component. Income effect: a higher energy price makes the fixed budget worth less in real terms (the $5,000→$4,000 illustration) — even holding relative prices fixed, a fall in real income lowers demand for normal goods, so BOTH energy and other goods fall. Flag the normal-good qualifier out loud: if energy is inferior the income effect reverses and can overwhelm substitution (Giffen), which §6.2 develops. Total effect = substitution + income = the Slutsky decomposition on the next slides.

Poll: Cap vs. cash — which mitigates welfare loss more?

A low-income UK household faces a 45% energy-price spike. Government has a fixed budget. Which policy mitigates the household's welfare loss MORE, holding the policy cost the same?

A) Price cap (subsidize energy at the old price)
B) Targeted lump-sum cash transfer
C) They are equivalent — same dollars
D) Depends on the size of substitution vs. income channels

60 sec to vote

Press 1 to start a 60-sec timer. The trap is C (equivalent because the government spends the same). The right answer is D — and the next slide explains why. Students who vote A or B are anchoring on which channel they intuitively think dominates. The pedagogical purpose: motivate the decomposition by showing that the answer requires knowing both channels' magnitudes.

Answer: D — the decomposition is the diagnostic

Why C is the trap. Same government dollars can produce different welfare outcomes depending on whether substitution or income dominates the household's response. Cap is better if the household is very inelastic (substitution small); cash is better if the household is elastic and the cap would distort behavior away from conservation.

The chapter builds the Slutsky equation — the algebra that decomposes any observed Marshallian response into its substitution and income parts. Once we have it, the policy question has a quantitative answer.

Distribution check: most students should pick A or B (anchoring); D is right. If most pick C, you have a misconception to fix in real time. Levell-O'Connell-Smith 2024 estimate that for the UK in 2022 the substitution channel dominates for the median household but the income channel dominates for the bottom decile — exactly the partition that the Slutsky equation operationalizes.

Pure-income thought experiment: Sam's raise

Sam allocates \$60/month between Netflix (\$4) and Spotify (\$6). Cobb-Douglas with \(\alpha = 1/3\). His income doubles to \$120 — neither price changes.

Budget: \(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = I\) shifts outward in parallel.

  • Both axes rise. Sam spreads new income across both goods (he was buying both already).
  • Slope preserved: \(-p_1/p_2 = -2/3\) unchanged — no relative-price move.
  • Higher indifference curve: \(IC_0 \to IC_1\). Strictly higher utility (monotonicity, §4.1).

Cobb-Douglas

Utility: \(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha}\, x_2^{1-\alpha} = x_1^{1/3}\, x_2^{2/3}\).

Constant-share rule: \(x_1^M = \alpha I/p_1\), \(x_2^M = (1{-}\alpha) I/p_2\).

At \(I=60\): \(A = (5,\, 6.67)\).
At \(I=120\): \(B = (10,\, 13.33)\).

Both quantities double — Cobb-Douglas income elasticity \(=1\) on each good.

Figure 6.12 shows two parallel budget lines and the bundles \(A \to B\) moving northeast.

The pure-income case is the warm-up — only one of the three Marshallian arguments (income) changes. Three observations to land: (1) parallel shift (no slope change), (2) both axes move because both goods are normal under Cobb-Douglas, (3) Sam ends up on a strictly higher indifference curve. Income elasticity: necessity, luxury, inferior

\[\varepsilon_I \;=\; \frac{\Delta x / x}{\Delta I / I}\;=\;\frac{\partial x}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{I}{x}.\]

Sign of \(\varepsilon_I\) sets normal vs. inferior; magnitude inside the normal range further splits necessity vs. luxury.

Necessity

\(0 < \varepsilon_I < 1\)

Quantity rises with income, slower than income. Budget share falls. Examples: toothpaste, salt, food-at-home, electricity.

Luxury

\(\varepsilon_I > 1\)

Quantity rises faster than income. Budget share rises. Examples: jewelry, first-class tickets, vacation homes, restaurant meals.

Inferior

\(\varepsilon_I < 0\)

Quantity falls as income rises. Trade up to a preferred alternative. Examples: store-brand canned soup, bus rides, used cars, ramen.

Adding-up constraint. Not every good can be inferior simultaneously. If Sam cuts bus rides when his income rises, the saved dollars go somewhere. At least one good in the bundle must be normal.

Normal vs. inferior can flip with income level. A used car is normal at low income (any car beats the bus) and inferior at high income (new cars supplant used ones).

Three-way taxonomy. Connect to 1116 §6.3 (Income Elasticity of Demand) — students saw the formula and the necessity/luxury split there; today we tie it to the Slutsky equation's income term. Trap question for poll: "Can EVERY good in a consumer's bundle be inferior?" — answer NO (the income has to flow somewhere). Mention level-dependence: the same physical good can be normal at one income range and inferior at another — this is exactly what makes the Engel curve (next slide) bend.

Income Expansion Path and Engel curve

Income Expansion Path (IEP)

Trace the consumer's optimum bundle from \(I = 0\) (the origin) through every increasing income level. Connect the tangencies.

  • Two normal goods: IEP slopes upward — every income increase moves the bundle northeast.
  • Normal + eventually-inferior: IEP bends backward on the inferior axis past some income threshold.

Lives in the same \((x_1, x_2)\) plane as the indifference curves.

Engel curve (Engel, 1857)

Replot: income on the vertical axis, quantity of one good on the horizontal. One point per IEP point.

  • Positive slope ⇒ normal good at that income.
  • Negative slope ⇒ inferior at that income.
  • Backward bend ⇒ normal-to-inferior transition.

Engel curve = empirical workhorse. Household surveys (BLS CEX, USDA ERS) record income + spending; plotting the data gives the Engel curve directly. The slope at any point estimates \(\varepsilon_I\) at that income range. T&D 6.1 below uses BLS CEX 2024 food-share data to verify Engel's law (food share falls with income).

Two graphical tools, one piece of information. IEP lives in (x_1, x_2) space — sit next to indifference curves. Engel curve replots with income on the vertical axis and ONE good's quantity on the horizontal — easier to estimate empirically. Backward bend in either curve = normal-to-inferior transition. Land Engel's law: as income rises, food share FALLS (food is a necessity) — this is the cleanest empirical regularity in consumer theory and the topic of T&D 6.1 later. PART 2 · §6.2  How price changes affect consumption choices

Substitution and income effects on an indifference map

Deriving a demand curve

Just as income shifts the bundle (§6.1), changes in relative prices — holding income constant — also reshape consumer choices. We now build Sam's individual demand curve directly from his utility model.

The recipe

  • A demand curve maps quantity demanded to price, holding income and other prices fixed.
  • To trace it, change one price on the indifference-map / budget-constraint figure and read off the new optimum.
  • Each price → one optimum → one \((Q, P)\) point on the demand curve.
  • Connect the points to get the consumer's demand curve for that good.

The observed price represents the consumer's maximum willingness to pay for the last unit consumed — the inverse-demand interpretation we use in welfare analysis (Ch 2 CS, Ch 6.7 CV).

Bridge slide between §6.1 (income channel) and §6.2's substitution/income decomposition. Concept: a demand curve isn't a primitive — it's derived from the consumer's utility-max optimization by varying ONE price at a time. The next slide (Figure 6.16) walks through this construction for Sam: at three Netflix prices ($4, $6, $12), three optima on the indifference map → three points on the demand curve. Mention: the demand curve is a "summary" of how the consumer reacts to price changes, while the underlying IC map is the structure that produces it. Later in §6.2, we'll decompose any movement ALONG the demand curve into substitution (along IC₀) + income (parallel shift) parts.

Shifts in the demand curve

Movement along the demand curve answers “more Netflix at lower price.” A shift of the whole curve answers a different question: at the same price, has the consumer's demand changed?

What shifts the curve

  • Preferences change (taste shock; new info; ad campaign).
  • Income changes (the §6.1 channel).
  • Prices of other goods change (cross-price effects).

Sam's preference shock

Suppose Sam starts listening to more new music (podcasts, lossless audio) and values Netflix less, relatively speaking. His Cobb-Douglas weight \(\alpha\) on Netflix drops from \(1/3\) to \(1/5\).

At every Netflix price, Sam wants fewer Netflix subs — the whole demand curve shifts inward.

Mechanism: with lower \(\alpha\), Sam's indifference curves flatten in the Netflix direction — tangency on the same budget line now sits further left.

Movement ALONG vs shift OF the demand curve — the perennial confusion that students need to internalize. Movement along = same demand curve, different price → different quantity. Shift = same price, different quantity → different demand curve. Three things shift the curve: preferences, income, other prices. The Sam example uses preferences (taste shock toward Spotify/podcasts, away from Netflix). The Cobb-Douglas mechanics: drop α from 1/3 to 1/5 → x_N(p) drops from 20/p_N to 12/p_N at every price. Next slide shows the geometric picture.

Case study — Capuchin monkeys obey the law of demand

Chen, Lakshminarayanan & Santos (2006, JPE).

  • Concept: Capuchin monkeys were taught to trade metal washers as “money” for foods (Jell-O cubes, grapes).
  • Price change: The Jell-O “price” rose — originally 3 cubes per washer, then only 1 cube per washer (3× price hike).
  • Behavior: Monkeys responded exactly as theory predicts — they substituted toward the cheaper good (grapes) and consumed less Jell-O. The law of demand isn't a human cultural artifact; it falls out of optimization.

Lesson: the SE+IE machinery isn't species-specific. Any optimizing agent with stable preferences over bundles obeys the same law.

Chen-Lakshminarayanan-Santos 2006 JPE — the canonical capuchin-monkey experiment. Yale lab taught monkeys to use metal washers as a token currency, redeemable for different foods at posted "prices" (cubes per washer). When researchers raised the relative price of Jell-O (one cube per washer instead of three), the monkeys substituted toward the now-relatively-cheaper grapes. Same qualitative behavior as human consumers facing relative-price changes. Pedagogical land: substitution and income effects aren't human-specific psychology; they're features of optimization with stable preferences. The paper is open online if students want the full read.

Case study — Rats show inferior vs. luxury goods

Kagel, Battalio & colleagues, 1970s–80s (animal-economics tradition).

  • Concept: Rats in a Skinner box could press levers to receive either root beer (the preferred treat) or bitter quinine water. Daily presses available = the “budget.”
  • Income change: Researchers cut the daily press allowance — rats became “poorer.”
  • Behavior: When made poor, rats consumed less root beer and more quinine water. Root beer behaves as a luxury for rats; quinine water as an inferior good.

Lesson: the §6.1.2 taxonomy (normal/inferior, necessity/luxury) is a fact about preferences and budgets — reproducible in non-human animals.

Substitution effect — isolate channel #1

Alex's thought experiment. Hold Alex's utility at \(U_1\) (the original level). At the new price ratio, what bundle would Alex pick?

The trick

Shift \(BC_2\) inward (same new slope) until it's tangent to the old IC \(U_1\). Call it \(BC'\). Tangency point = \(A'\).

Substitution effect = movement \(A \to A'\) along the original indifference curve \(U_1\). Pure relative-price reallocation; real utility constant.

Always non-positive in own price. Good 1 became relatively cheaper, so Alex substitutes toward good 1. This is a theorem — convexity of preferences guarantees the direction.

In the figure. Good 1: \(3 \to 4.24\) (\(+1.24\) sub). Good 2: \(4 \to 2.24\) (\(-1.76\) sub).

First isolated effect: substitution. The trick is to construct a compensated budget BC' that has the new slope (new relative prices) but is shifted inward until it just barely touches U1 (the old indifference curve). The tangency A' is the compensated optimum — what Alex would choose at new prices IF utility had to stay at the original level. The move A → A' along U1 is the substitution effect: pure relative-price reallocation, no utility change. Because U1 is convex and we pick a different slope on the same curve, the move is in the direction of MORE of the cheaper good — always non-positive own-price effect, a theorem from convexity. Numbers in the figure: good 1 goes from 3 to 4.24 (+1.24 substitution), good 2 goes from 4 to 2.24 (-1.76 substitution). Income effect — isolate channel #2

Alex's second move. The price fell, so Alex's real purchasing power increased: with \(BC_2\), Alex can afford a bigger bundle than \(BC'\) allows.

The shift

\(BC' \to BC_2\): a parallel shift outward (same new slope). Alex moves from \(A'\) on \(U_1\) up to \(B\) on the higher \(U_2\).

Income effect = movement \(A' \to B\), the change in consumption from the purchasing-power change alone. Relative prices held fixed.

Sign can flip: positive for a normal good (reinforces substitution), negative for inferior (partially offsets), large negative for a Giffen good (overpowers substitution).

\(\text{Total} \;=\; \text{Sub} \;+\; \text{Inc}\)

Second isolated effect: income. From the compensated point A' (on the old IC U1) to the new optimum B (on the new IC U2), the change is a parallel BC shift — pure purchasing-power change, no relative-price change. The income effect's sign depends on income elasticity: positive (reinforcing substitution) for normal goods, negative (partial offset) for inferior, large negative (full reversal — Giffen) at the extreme. Numbers in the figure: good 1 goes from 4.24 to 5 (+0.76 income), good 2 goes from 2.24 to 5 (+2.76 income). Total = Sub + Inc summary completes the boxed identity. Combined picture — total effect of a price fall

Three steps to compute substitution + income effects

Start from the consumer's optimum bundle \(A\) on \(BC_1\) and \(U_1\):

  1. Draw the new budget constraint \(BC_2\) (the price change rotates / pivots \(BC_1\)). Find the new optimum bundle \(B\) on the higher indifference curve \(U_2\).
  2. Draw a line parallel to \(BC_2\) (same new slope) but shifted inward until it's tangent to the old IC \(U_1\). Call it \(BC'\). Its tangent point is \(A'\).
  3. Read off the decomposition.
    • Substitution effect = \(A \to A'\) (along \(U_1\)).
    • Income effect = \(A' \to B\) (parallel shift to \(U_2\)).
    • Total effect = \(A \to B\) = Sub + Inc.
The three-step procedure that operationalizes the Slutsky decomposition geometrically. Step 1: change the price → BC rotates → find new tangency B. Step 3: substitution = A → A' (along U1, pure relative-price reallocation); income = A' → B (parallel shift to U2, pure purchasing-power change). Total = A → B = Sub + Inc, exactly. Price changes — what about Giffen goods?

Giffen goods: goods for which a fall in price leads the consumer to want less of the good.

  • Inferior goods, but the income effect outweighs the substitution effect.
  • When the price of a Giffen good drops, the substitution effect (which acts to increase demand) is smaller than the income effect.
    • Results in an upward-sloping demand curve.
  • Economists sometimes question whether Giffen goods actually exist in the real world.
  • The few documented examples tend to focus on very poor households and commodity staples (e.g., rice, potatoes).

Bridge slide between the inferior-good case (page 30) and the Giffen extreme. A Giffen good is the pathological case where the income effect not only goes the "wrong way" (as for any inferior good) but is LARGE ENOUGH to overpower the substitution effect — flipping the sign of total demand response. Geometrically: the compensated budget BC' tangency A' is to the RIGHT of the new optimum B (income effect on the inferior good is so negative it pulls B BACK past where you started). This is the only way to get an upward-sloping demand curve from utility maximization. Empirically very rare — students often confuse "inferior" with "Giffen", so emphasize: every Giffen is inferior, but most inferior goods are NOT Giffen (W6.3 Carlos's canned soup is the inferior-non-Giffen archetype). A classic myth — the Irish potato famine

  • For decades, the potato during the Irish famine was the textbook example of a Giffen good. The story: as potato prices rose, poor families could no longer afford other foods (meat, dairy) and bought even more potatoes to survive.
  • Why it's wrong. Modern reanalysis of historical data disproved this. The potato blight was a massive supply contraction — if the demand curve were truly upward-sloping, supply shocks would have produced lower equilibrium prices. Instead, prices skyrocketed, which is consistent with a normal downward-sloping demand curve.

Lesson: “poor + staple + price rise” is necessary but not sufficient for Giffen. You also need the supply-side identification to rule out a normal demand curve.

The Irish potato famine (1845-1852) was the textbook Giffen example in every micro textbook for over a century. The narrative: potato prices rose due to blight, but because Irish peasants depended so heavily on potatoes for calories, they couldn't afford to substitute toward meat — they had to consume MORE potatoes. The empirical problem (raised by Dwyer & Lindsay 1984, and more rigorously by Gerald Bryan 1990): if the demand curve really sloped upward, a supply shock (the blight) should have produced LOWER prices, not higher. Historical data show prices rose dramatically — consistent with a normal downward-sloping demand curve and an inelastic but standard supply shock. The figure shows the THEORETICAL Giffen prediction matching the myth's narrative: as p_potato RISES, the budget pivots INWARD (around the meat intercept) from BC1 to the steeper BC2; the inferior good's dominant income effect makes the optimum move to MORE potatoes and LESS meat (A→B, onto a LOWER indifference curve — the consumer is worse off). That "price up, quantity up" is exactly the upward-sloping demand the myth claims. Lesson for students: empirical identification of Giffen requires more than just observing rising prices and rising quantities — could be supply-demand simultaneity. The Jensen-Miller experiment (next slide) gets around this by directly shifting price exogenously.

Modern proof — rice in Hunan, China

  • The experiment. Economists Robert Jensen and Nolan Miller ran a randomized experiment with very poor households in Hunan, China — they subsidized the purchase of rice, effectively lowering its price exogenously.
  • The surprising result. Households that received the subsidy and paid a lower price for rice actually reduced their rice consumption.
  • The explanation. Rice was a huge share of these families' budgets, so the subsidy acted like a large income boost. With higher real income, they chose to diversify their diet (more meat, vegetables) and bought less of the now-cheaper rice. The income effect — wanting less rice as income rose — outweighed the substitution effect.
  • An interesting exception. The very poorest households did not show this effect — their incomes were still too low to buy other foods, so when rice became cheaper they just bought more rice to meet basic calorie needs. (Classic downward-sloping demand at the subsistence corner.)

Punchline. Giffen behavior exists, but it lives in a narrow income band — rich enough that staples are a large budget share AND inferior, poor enough that the substitution channel is small. Jensen & Miller, AER 2008.

Jensen and Miller (2008, "Giffen Behavior and Subsistence Consumption", American Economic Review 98(4)). They ran a field experiment in two Chinese provinces — Hunan (rice-eating) and Gansu (wheat-eating) — randomly distributing vouchers that subsidized the price of the staple. For middle-poor households in Hunan, the price subsidy CAUSED a DECREASE in rice consumption: a positive own-price elasticity (Giffen behavior). The key channel: rice was such a large share of the budget that the subsidy felt like a large income boost; with the higher real income, families diversified toward meat and vegetables and bought less of the now-cheaper rice. CRITICAL: the very poorest households did NOT show Giffen behavior — they were still at the subsistence corner, where the substitution effect dominates and rice consumption rises when its price falls. This income band is exactly what theory predicts: Giffen requires inferior + large budget share + non-subsistence. Cleanly identified because the price variation is EXPERIMENTAL (random voucher assignment), avoiding the supply-demand simultaneity problem that doomed the Irish potato narrative. Simple rules — substitution effects

Substitution effects

  • Involve comparisons of bundles on the same indifference curve.
  • The direction of the effect on quantity consumed (for a given change in the relative price) is unambiguous.
  • If the good's relative price falls, the substitution effect causes the consumer to want more of it.
  • If the good's relative price rises, the substitution effect causes the consumer to want less of it.

Why “unambiguous”? Because indifference curves are convex (a theorem of preferences). The MRS along a fixed \(U\) always moves toward more of the cheaper good.

Summary slide for the substitution channel. The key adjective is "unambiguous" — the sign of the substitution effect is a THEOREM, not an empirical claim. Convexity of indifference curves (a maintained assumption since Ch 5) guarantees that as you slide along a fixed IC toward a new price ratio, you accumulate more of whatever became relatively cheaper. The convexity assumption is doing all the work — without it (e.g., kinked or concave ICs), the substitution effect could in principle be zero or even reverse, but that's outside the standard model. This is the LAW-LIKE property of demand that survives any income-elasticity weirdness. Forward-pointer: this is why Slutsky's "compensated own-price elasticity" is always non-positive, and why HeadsUp 6.1 (already in the deck) gets to call it a theorem rather than an empirical regularity.

Simple rules — income effects

Income effects

  • Involve comparisons of bundles on two different indifference curves.
  • The direction of the effect on quantity consumed is ambiguous — it depends on whether the good is normal or inferior.
  • If the good is normal: a fall in either its price or the price of the other good will cause the consumer to want more of it. (A drop in any price raises effective income.)
  • If the good is inferior: a price drop causes the consumer to want less of it (income effect goes the “wrong way”).
  • If the good is normal: a rise in either price will cause the consumer to want less. If the good is inferior, a rise in either price will cause the consumer to want more.

Punchline: the total effect (what we observe in data) = unambiguous substitution \(+\) ambiguous income.

Companion summary for the income channel. Contrast with the substitution-effect slide: here the direction is AMBIGUOUS in general — it depends on the good's income elasticity (normal vs. inferior). The four cases (normal/price-fall, normal/price-rise, inferior/price-fall, inferior/price-rise) span the §6.4 sign chart (Figure 6.3) coming up. The Giffen good is the extreme case where the income term overpowers the substitution term.

  • At original prices, this consumer purchases 15 liters of Pepsi and 5 liters of Coke at point \(A\).

  • Pepsi consumption falls by 67% (15 \(\to\) 5 liters), and Coke consumption rises by 100% (5 \(\to\) 10 liters) at new optimum \(B\).

Geometric demonstration of substitution. The key feature: BC pivots around the x-intercept (Coke axis) because only the y-good's price changed. The new optimum lies on a LOWER utility curve U2 (real income fell because one of the consumer's goods got more expensive) and at MORE Coke + LESS Pepsi. When the price of a complement good increases, we expect consumption of the primary good to decrease.

Cross-price effects block — second concept: complements. Goods consumed together (ice cream + hot fudge, peanut butter + jelly, cars + gasoline) move in the SAME direction when one of their prices changes. The cross-price elasticity \(\partial x_i/\partial p_j < 0\) defines complements. In the Slutsky framework these are "net complements" if \(\partial h_i/\partial p_j < 0\). Notice the asymmetry from substitutes: for substitutes, sub and inc work AGAINST each other on the cross good; for complements, sub and inc work TOGETHER, both pulling the cross good down. That's why complement classifications are usually unambiguous in data while substitute classifications can be sensitive to which decomposition you use.

Application — movies: theaters vs. home streaming

The pandemic as a natural experiment

COVID-19 effectively raised the “price” (risk + closures) of going to a theater. So which way did home streaming move?

  • If theaters and streaming were complements \(\Rightarrow\) streaming should have declined too.
  • If they were substitutes \(\Rightarrow\) streaming should have surged.

The verdict — they are substitutes

  • Theater box office: U.S. revenue collapsed from \(\$11.3\,\text{B}\) in 2019 to \(\$2.1\,\text{B}\) in 2020.
  • Home streaming: Netflix subscriptions grew +45%; Disney+ reached 40 M U.S. subscribers.

Not new — the substitution accelerated a historical pattern: in 1946 the average American went to the movies 28 times/year; a decade later, with TVs widespread, that number was cut in half. COVID just compressed decades of substitution into one year.

Combining individual demand curves

The final step linking consumer theory to market demand:

  • Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demand curves.
  • At each price, the market quantity demanded is the sum of individual quantities demanded at that price.

The market demand curve is found by summing horizontally the individual demand curves.

Consider the market for video games: Priya buys day-one; Diego only buys on sale.

Bridge from individual consumer theory to market-level analysis. The market demand curve is the HORIZONTAL sum of individual demand curves — at each price, we add up the quantities demanded by all consumers. Why horizontal? Because we're asking "at this fixed price, how many UNITS total?" Each consumer's individual quantity at that price gets added together. Geometrically: pick a price level, find each individual's quantity at that level, sum them, that's the market quantity at that price. Repeating across all prices traces out the market demand curve. KEY SUBTLETY: when consumers have different choke prices (the price at which their individual quantity drops to zero), the market demand curve has a KINK at each choke price — above that price, only consumers with higher choke prices contribute. The video-game example on the next slide illustrates this kink explicitly: Priya is a day-one buyer (choke $100), Diego only buys on sale (choke $52).

Market demand — the math

Mathematically connecting individual and market demand — Priya (day-one buyer) and Diego (sale hunter):

\[Q_{\text{market}} = Q_{\text{Priya}} + Q_{\text{Diego}} = (5 - 0.05P) + (13 - 0.25P) = 18 - 0.3P.\]

The two consumers have different choke prices (the price where individual quantity hits zero): Priya's is \(\$100\) — she pays deluxe-edition prices; Diego's is \(\$52\) — he never pays more than about fifty. The combined formula above is valid only when both are buying.

The piecewise market demand:

\[Q_{\text{market}} = 18 - 0.3P \quad \text{for } P < \$52,\]

\[Q_{\text{market}} = 5 - 0.05P \quad \text{for } \$52 \leq P \leq \$100.\]

The market demand has a kink at \(P = \$52\): above it only Priya buys; the moment a sale drops the price below \$52, Diego enters and demand bends sharply flatter — which is precisely why Steam sales exist.

Worked example of horizontal summation across two heterogeneous consumers. Priya is a day-one buyer (up to 5 games/yr, will pay up to $100 for a deluxe edition); Diego is a sale hunter (up to 13 games/yr, but never above ~$50). The key conceptual point: simply adding the demand formulas (5 - 0.05P) + (13 - 0.25P) = 18 - 0.3P is correct ONLY when both consumers are in the market (i.e., when P is below BOTH choke prices). Above the lower choke price ($52), Diego drops out, and the market demand equals just Priya's demand (5 - 0.05P). Above Priya's choke price ($100), no one is in the market. So the market demand has a KINK at $52: slope changes from -0.05 (steep, Priya-only) to -0.3 (flat, both consumers). Land the punchline: the kink IS the business case for Steam sales — cutting price below $52 doesn't just sell Priya a bit more, it switches Diego on entirely, so the marginal revenue from a price cut jumps discontinuously at the kink. At $100 the curve hits zero. This kink phenomenon generalizes: aggregating N heterogeneous consumers gives N kinks (one per choke price). In aggregate-economy theory we usually smooth over these by appealing to a continuum of consumers, but the bookkeeping is the same — sum quantities horizontally at each price.

Two problems, one optimum: primal vs. dual

PRIMAL (Ch 5)

Utility maximization at given prices and income.

\[\max_{x_1, x_2 \geq 0}\; U(x_1, x_2)\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\; p \cdot x \leq I.\]

Output: Marshallian demand \(x_i^M(p, I)\) + indirect utility \(v(p, I) = U(x^M)\).

DUAL (today)

Expenditure minimization at given prices and a utility floor.

\[\min_{x_1, x_2 \geq 0}\; p \cdot x \;\;\text{s.t.}\;\; U(x) \geq \bar u.\]

Output: Hicksian (compensated) demand \(h_i(p, \bar u)\) + expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u) = p \cdot h\).

Why the dual matters. The Hicksian demand isolates the substitution channel by construction — utility is held fixed, so any price-response is pure relative-price reallocation. That isolation is what makes the Slutsky decomposition possible.

The primal answers "what bundle do you actually buy?" The dual answers "what bundle would you buy if I gave you just enough income to stay on your original indifference curve?" The dual is a thought experiment, not a behavior the consumer actually exhibits — but it's the cleanest way to mathematically isolate substitution. At the same prices and the same utility, primal and dual select the same bundle: x^M(p, e(p, ū)) = h(p, ū). That's the duality identity, coming on the next slide but one.

The expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\)

\[e(p, \bar u) \;\equiv\; \min_{x \geq 0} \;\{\, p \cdot x \,:\, U(x) \geq \bar u \,\}\;=\; p_1 h_1(p,\bar u) + p_2 h_2(p, \bar u).\]

The minimum dollar cost of reaching utility \(\bar u\) at prices \(p\). The five properties that fall out of the definition:

1. Non-dec.

in each \(p_i\) — higher prices, weakly more cost.

2. HOD 1

in \(p\): \(e(\alpha p, \bar u) = \alpha\, e(p, \bar u)\). Inflate all prices, cost inflates the same.

3. Concave

in \(p\) — consumer can re-optimize when a price rises.

4. Continuous

in \((p, \bar u)\) — small changes, small cost changes.

5. Inc. in \(\bar u\)

reach a higher floor ⇒ pay more (locally non-satiated \(U\)).

Concavity intuition. If \(p_1\) rises, the consumer substitutes away from good 1 along the original indifference curve, so cost rises by less than the “keep buying the same bundle” counterfactual — that's concavity.

Definition — non-satiation

Non-satiation (or non-satiety) is the core assumption that consumers always prefer more of a good to less. “Locally non-satiated” weakens this: at every bundle, some nearby bundle is strictly preferred. It rules out flat or saturated indifference curves and pins the budget constraint as a binding equation at the optimum — which is why property 5 holds.

Five properties, one slide. The properties matter because (i) they're the algebraic facts that get used in proofs (Slutsky derivation needs concavity, Roy needs HOD), (ii) they're poll-bait — students confuse concavity vs convexity, mix up direction of HOD, etc. (Poll 2 next exploits this.) Walras's law + non-satiation give property 5 (the floor binds at optimum). Definition box explains non-satiation explicitly the first time it appears in the deck: consumers always prefer more to less; "locally non-satiated" is the technical weakening (some nearby bundle preferred at every point). This is what makes the budget constraint bind at the optimum.

Hicksian demand and the duality identity

Hicksian (compensated) demand

The expenditure-minimizing bundle at prices \(p\) and utility floor \(\bar u\):

\(h_i(p, \bar u) = \arg\min\,\{\, p \cdot x : U(x) \geq \bar u \,\}.\)

Read it as: “what bundle does the consumer buy when utility is held fixed and only relative prices change?” The Hicksian demand traces out a curve in \((p_1, x_1)\) space along a single indifference curve.

Duality identity

\[x_i^M(p,\, e(p, \bar u)) \;=\; h_i(p, \bar u).\]

At the same prices and the same utility floor, the Marshallian demand evaluated at the minimum cost \(e(p, \bar u)\) is exactly the Hicksian demand. Same bundle, two paths to it.

Why this is the lever. Differentiate the duality identity in \(p_j\) and you get the Slutsky equation in one line. The next part builds that derivation.

Two objects, one bundle. The duality identity is the master equation of the chapter — every Slutsky-related result derives from differentiating it. Geometric intuition: pick any indifference curve. The Hicksian curve is the locus of optima along *that* curve as relative prices change. The Marshallian curve is the locus of optima as income changes too. They coincide only at the original price-income pair.

CalculusCorner 6.1 — Cobb-Douglas dual: setup

Problem

A consumer has Cobb-Douglas utility \(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}\). Find the expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\) and the Hicksian demand \(h_1(p, \bar u),\, h_2(p, \bar u)\) for given prices \(p_1, p_2\) and utility floor \(\bar u\).

Set up the Lagrangian

\[\mathcal{L} = p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 - \mu\big(x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha} - \bar u\big).\]

Minimize cost; \(\mu \geq 0\) is the shadow value of a marginal utility unit.

Two FOCs

\(p_1 = \mu\, \alpha\, x_1^{\alpha-1} x_2^{1-\alpha}\)
\(p_2 = \mu\, (1-\alpha)\, x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{-\alpha}\)

Divide to eliminate \(\mu\): \(\;\dfrac{p_1}{p_2} = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \dfrac{x_2}{x_1}.\)

Next slide — solve for \(x_1, x_2\) on the indifference curve \(U = \bar u\), substitute into \(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2\).

Notation note: \(\mu\) for the dual Lagrange multiplier (units: dollars per util) to keep it distinct from the primal \(\lambda\) (utils per dollar). The two are reciprocals at the optimum — Math Appendix B.3 covers this. The ratio of FOCs is the tangency condition, same shape as the primal but with utility-constraint slope on the right.

CalculusCorner 6.1 — solve: closed forms drop out

From the tangency ratio:

\(x_2 = \dfrac{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}{\alpha\, p_2}\, x_1.\)

Plug into \(U(x_1, x_2) = \bar u\) and solve for \(x_1\):

\[h_1(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{\alpha\, p_2}{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}.\]

\[h_2(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{(1-\alpha)\, p_1}{\alpha\, p_2}\right)^{\alpha}.\]

Expenditure function

\[e(p, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{p_1}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha} \left(\frac{p_2}{1-\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}.\]

Sanity check. Verify properties of \(e\): non-decreasing in each \(p_i\) (powers are positive), HOD 1 in \(p\) (sum of exponents \(= 1\)), concave (product of powers of \(p\), exponents in \([0,1]\)), and linear in \(\bar u\). All five properties pass.

Returns in Walkthrough 6.1: this \(e(p, \bar u)\) is the exact expression we differentiate when we run the Slutsky decomposition for Cobb-Douglas \(\alpha = 1/3\).

The two closed forms here are the backbone of every Cobb-Douglas Hicksian calculation in the rest of the chapter and on the homework. Worth pausing to point out the symmetry with the Marshallian closed forms from Ch 5 (constant expenditure shares \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\)) — same structural property, different arguments. Linearity of \(e\) in \(\bar u\) is a Cobb-Douglas accident, not a general property; CES and other forms give different curvature.

Numerical example — Hicksian demand step by step

Quick reminder. For Cobb-Douglas \(U = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}\), the FOC ratio \(\tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\tfrac{x_2}{x_1} = \tfrac{p_1}{p_2}\) plus the budget gives \(\boxed{x_i^M = \alpha_i I / p_i}\) — the “constant expenditure share” signature. Cobb-Douglas only: quasilinear (W6.2) and linear demand (W6.3) give different forms.

The story — Sam, before the price hike

Sam likes Netflix (\(x_1\)) and Spotify (\(x_2\)) with weights \(\alpha = 1/3\) on Netflix. At baseline \(p_1 = 4, p_2 = 6, I = 60\), Marshallian \(x_1^M = \alpha I/p_1 = (1/3)(60)/4 = 5\) and \(x_2^M = (2/3)(60)/6 = 20/3\). Utility \(\bar u_0 = 5^{1/3}(20/3)^{2/3} \approx 6.06\).

Question

What is the cheapest bundle that keeps Sam at utility \(\bar u_0 = 6.06\) when prices are still \((p_1, p_2) = (4, 6)\)? That bundle is the Hicksian demand \(h_1, h_2\).

Step 1 — recall the closed forms (from CC 6.1)

\(h_1(p, \bar u) = \bar u \!\left(\dfrac{\alpha\, p_2}{(1-\alpha) p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}, \;\; h_2(p, \bar u) = \bar u \!\left(\dfrac{(1-\alpha) p_1}{\alpha\, p_2}\right)^{\alpha}.\)

\(\alpha = 1/3, \;1 - \alpha = 2/3, \;p_1 = 4, \;p_2 = 6, \;\bar u_0 = 6.058\):

\(h_1 = 6.058 \!\cdot\! \!\left(\dfrac{(1/3)(6)}{(2/3)(4)}\right)^{\!2/3} = 6.058 \!\cdot\! (0.75)^{2/3}.\)

Step 3 — crunch the arithmetic

\((0.75)^{2/3} = \exp(\tfrac{2}{3} \ln 0.75) \approx 0.8255\).

\(h_1 \approx 6.058 \!\cdot\! 0.8255 \approx 5.00.\)

\(h_2 = 6.058 \!\cdot\! (4/3)^{1/3} \approx 6.058 \!\cdot\! 1.1006 \approx 6.67.\)

Solution — Hicksian \(=\) Marshallian (at baseline!)

\(\boxed{\;h_1(4, 6, 6.058) = 5, \quad h_2(4, 6, 6.058) = 20/3.\;}\)

Same bundle as the Marshallian optimum — expected, because the Marshallian bundle already lies on \(U_0\) and minimizes expenditure to reach it at these prices.

Intuition. Marshallian: “You have \$60 — what makes you happiest?” Hicksian: “You need utility \(\bar u_0\) — what's the cheapest way?” Same answer at baseline (Sam was already optimizing); diverges when prices change — that gap is the Slutsky substitution effect.

PART 3 · §6.3  The Slutsky equation

25 min · The chapter's headline identity, two walkthroughs

The Slutsky equation

For any pair of goods \(i, j\), any consumer with smooth preferences, evaluated at the bundle that achieves utility \(\bar u = v(p, I)\):

\[\boxed{\;\frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial h_i(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j}}_{\text{substitution effect}} \;-\; \underbrace{x_j^M(p, I) \cdot \frac{\partial x_i^M(p, I)}{\partial I}}_{\text{income effect}}.\;}\]

LHS: what the data show

How Marshallian (actual) demand responds to a price change. Both channels mixed.

RHS: the decomposition

Hicksian response (pure substitution) minus the dollar value of \(x_j\) times income response.

The income term is multiplied by \(x_j\) because a unit rise in \(p_j\) costs the consumer roughly \(x_j\) extra dollars to keep buying the original bundle — that's the “real income loss” we have to subtract back out.

This is the equation the chapter exists to deliver. Stop and let it land. Tell students: every time you see Marshallian response to a price change in data — gasoline elasticity, energy demand elasticity, anything — the Slutsky equation says it's a SUM of two effects, and you almost always need to know both to make a welfare or policy statement. Boxed because students should commit this to long-term memory.

CalculusCorner 6.2 — derivation in three lines

Duality identity (start)

\(x_i^M\!\big(p,\, e(p, \bar u)\big) \;=\; h_i(p, \bar u).\)

Step 1. Differentiate both sides w.r.t. \(p_j\) (chain rule on LHS):

\(\dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} + \dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I} \!\cdot\! \dfrac{\partial e(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_j} = \dfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j}.\)

Step 2. Apply Shephard's lemma: \(\partial e / \partial p_j = h_j = x_j^M\):

\(\dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} + \dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I} \!\cdot\! x_j^M = \dfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j}.\)

Step 3. Rearrange to land the Slutsky equation:

\(\boxed{\;\dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial p_j} \;=\; \dfrac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j} \;-\; x_j^M \!\cdot\! \dfrac{\partial x_i^M}{\partial I}.\;}\)

Three-line proof. Land each step explicitly: (1) differentiate both sides of the duality identity, (2) replace ∂e/∂p_j with x_j^M via Shephard's lemma, (3) rearrange. Shephard's lemma full proof is in §6.5 (forward-pointer) — for today, take it on faith. The proof works for ANY smooth utility function; Cobb-Douglas in the walkthrough is just illustration.

The geometric decomposition

Figure 6.1 — the Slutsky decomposition in indifference-curve space.

What to look at:

  • Three budget lines. Original; new (steeper after \(p_1 \uparrow\)); compensated (parallel to new, shifted to be tangent to the original indifference curve).
  • Three optima. Original \((x^A)\), compensated \((x^B)\), new \((x^C)\).
  • Substitution arrow runs from \(x^A\) to \(x^B\) along the original indifference curve: pure relative-price reallocation.
  • Income arrow runs from \(x^B\) to \(x^C\): parallel inward shift, no slope change — pure loss of real income.

Sub. always \(\leq 0\) along original IC for own-price; income depends on sign of \(\partial x_1/\partial I\).

Geometric incarnation of the Slutsky equation. The interactive textbook version lets students drag the price slider and watch the three points and arrows update. For lecture, talk through the three budgets in order: original (Marshallian), compensated (Hicksian — gives the consumer just enough income to reach the original IC), new (actual). The substitution arrow is the heart of the chapter; the income arrow is what makes everything complicated.

HeadsUp 6.1 — own-price substitution is always \(\leq 0\)

The Hicksian law of demand. For any consumer with smooth, locally non-satiated preferences, the own-price substitution effect satisfies

\[\frac{\partial h_i(p, \bar u)}{\partial p_i} \;\leq\; 0.\]

Why

Falls out of concavity of \(e(p, \bar u)\) in \(p\): the diagonal of the Hessian of a concave function is \(\leq 0\). Shephard's lemma converts that into \(\partial h_i/\partial p_i \leq 0\).

Why students miss this

They confuse it with the Marshallian \(\partial x_i^M/\partial p_i\), which can be positive (Giffen good). The point of the decomposition: substitution always pulls demand down; income can push it up enough to overpower substitution.

Operational consequence. If you ever derive a substitution effect with the “wrong” sign, you have a sign error in the algebra. Use this as a self-check on every Slutsky computation.

The chapter's most-tested sign claim. It's a theorem, not an assumption — and it's tested in Problem 8 of the chapter. Watch out: students will sometimes claim "substitution can be positive for Giffen goods." It cannot. Giffen is a Marshallian phenomenon (income overpowers sub); Hicksian own-price effect is monotone.

Walkthrough 6.2 — Ana's soda: setup

Ana's situation — the soda double

Ana spends \$20/week on soda (\$1/can) and a generic “everything else” bundle (\$1/unit). Her tastes are quasilinear: \(U = 6\sqrt{x_1} + x_2\) — she enjoys soda with diminishing returns, and the rest scales 1-for-1 with spending. Soda doubles to \$2/can. How does Ana respond, and what dollar “raise” would make her whole?

Our job (two surprises ahead)

(1) Show the income piece on soda is exactly zero — the trademark of quasilinear tastes. (2) Compute Ana's compensating variation (CV) — how many extra dollars she'd need to be just as happy as before.

Compensating variation (CV) — quick definition

CV \(\equiv e(\mathbf{p}', \bar u_0) - I\) is the extra dollars the consumer would need at the new prices to stay at the old utility \(\bar u_0\). Here: after Ana's soda doubles, CV is the cash “raise” that would make her just as happy as before — a money-metric welfare loss (want to know more? read §6.7).

Set up the FOC (MRS = price ratio)

\(MU_1 = 3/\sqrt{x_1}\), \(MU_2 = 1\); set \(MU_1/p_1 = MU_2/p_2\):

\(x_1^M(p_1, p_2) = \!\left(\dfrac{3 p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\!2}.\)

Did you spot it?

The formula \(x_1^M = (3 p_2/p_1)^2\) has no \(I\) in it! So giving Ana more income doesn't change how much soda she buys:

\(\dfrac{\partial x_1^M}{\partial I} = 0.\)

Why? Every extra dollar flows into “everything else” \(x_2\) — that bundle has constant marginal utility 1, so it absorbs all income changes. Soda just hits its “happy point” and stops.

When does Marshallian \(=\) Hicksian in real life?

The two demands coincide whenever the income effect on the good is approximately zero. That happens in four real-world settings:

Small-share, low-frequency goods

Toothpaste, matches, salt, postage stamps. Even if the price doubles, total spending barely budges, so the income channel is negligible. The whole demand response is substitution.

Daily-routine fixed-quantity goods

Morning coffee, bus fare, parking pass. You buy the same quantity regardless of small income changes — demand is income-inelastic by habit.

Small policy changes

\$0.05 gas tax, \$2 movie-ticket fee, \$0.10 plastic bag fee. The dollar change is so small the income effect on the consumer's overall budget is essentially zero. Economists routinely use the Ch 2 consumer-surplus area as an approximate CV (Willig 1976, Hausman 1981).

Quasilinear models in IO / public finance

Antitrust analysis, optimal-tax models, mechanism design all assume \(U = \phi(x_1) + x_2\) precisely because welfare \(=\) the integral under the demand curve, no further correction needed.

Counter-examples (where Marshallian \(\ne\) Hicksian): housing, healthcare, college tuition, big-ticket durables. Large budget shares + strong income elasticity \(\Rightarrow\) Marshallian and Hicksian diverge sharply, and welfare analysis must use the full Slutsky machinery.

CV in practice — antitrust & merger analysis

Regulators and economists use CV to put a dollar number on consumer harm from a price increase — the core empirical input to merger and antitrust decisions.

The DOJ/FTC merger playbook

  1. Estimate the post-merger price increase (UPP / GUPPI / simulation).
  2. Multiply by quantity to get aggregate CV — the dollar value of consumer harm.
  3. Compare against merger-specific efficiencies (cost savings passed to consumers).
  4. Block / challenge / clear based on net welfare effect.

Concrete cases — CV at work

  • T-Mobile / Sprint (2020): DOJ estimated CV \(\approx\) \$1.5–\$5 B/yr in higher wireless bills; cleared with divestiture (Dish enters as 4th carrier).
  • Hospital merger studies (Cooper et al. 2019): CV from local hospital mergers averages 1–3% of healthcare spending in concentrated markets.
  • Ticketmaster / Live Nation (2010): CV estimates for higher concert prices were key to the conduct-remedy decree.

Bridge — the algebra dictates the taxonomy

The Slutsky equation has two sign-bearing pieces. Substitution is always \(\leq 0\) (own-price). Income can be either sign depending on \(\partial x_i^M/\partial I\). Six logical combinations — only three matter for the Marshallian curve's slope:

Normal good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I > 0.\) Income effect reinforces substitution. Marshallian demand falls when \(p_i\) rises.

Inferior good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I < 0.\) Income effect opposes substitution; sub. dominates. Marshallian still slopes down.

Giffen good

\(\partial x_i^M/\partial I < 0\) AND large enough that income overpowers substitution. Marshallian slopes up.

HeadsUp 6.2 — what “compensated” means

“Compensated demand” means utility is held constant, not income. Students consistently swap these.

Hicksian (compensated)

Fix the utility floor \(\bar u\). As prices change, the income required to reach \(\bar u\) changes — that's exactly \(e(p, \bar u)\). The consumer is “compensated” for the price change with whatever income keeps them on the same indifference curve.

NOT “income-held-constant”

Marshallian demand is income-held-constant: fix \(I\), let utility float. That's the consumer's actual behavior. The Hicksian thought experiment is the opposite: fix utility, let income float.

Mnemonic. Hicksian = “Hold utility”. Marshallian = “Money fixed”. The two coincide at one point (the original \((p, I)\)) and diverge as relative prices change.

The terminology trap that students fall into on every homework. "Compensated" sounds like the consumer is being kept whole financially — but it actually means kept on the same indifference curve, which requires DIFFERENT income at different prices. Use the mnemonic: Hicksian = Hold utility. Marshallian = Money fixed. Test on Problem 5.

HeadsUp 6.3 — spotting Giffen behavior in real data

Identifying a Giffen good empirically requires more than a positively sloped scatter of price vs. quantity in observational data.

Three preconditions (Jensen-Miller protocol)

  • Households near subsistence (energy/nutrition constraint binding).
  • Good is a large share of expenditure (typically > 30%).
  • Good is the cheapest source of the necessary nutrient/service.

Confounders to rule out

  • Supply shocks moving \(p\) and \(x\) along an upward-sloping supply curve.
  • Quality changes mistaken for price changes.
  • Veblen goods (status signaling) — demand rises with price for prestige, not subsistence.

The randomized-control standard. Jensen-Miller passed because they exogenously varied rice prices via subsidies, ruling out supply confounders. A scatter plot alone cannot identify Giffen.

The methodological warning. Cocktail-party economics says "Giffen goods are rare" — true. But papers that *claim* to identify Giffen often confound true Giffen behavior with supply-side moves, quality changes, or status effects. Jensen-Miller is the gold standard because they randomized — the exogenous variation in price is identifying. Math Appendix C.6 has a longer methodological discussion.

Walkthrough 6.5 — Maya's labor-leisure Slutsky: setup

Maya's situation — the wage raise

Maya is a freelance designer with 97 free hours/wk split between studying \(s\), freelance work \(h\), and leisure \(\ell\). From Ch 5, at \(w=\$18\)/hr she works \(h^* = 8\) hr and takes \(\ell^* = 61\) hr leisure. Her wage rises to \$24/hr. Does she work more or less?

Take a moment to predict

Two pulls fight each other when wages rise: leisure is more expensive (work more) AND you're richer per hour (work less). Which wins? Make a guess before clicking.

Reframe the problem to use our tools

We pretend study is fixed at \(s=28\) (so 69 hours remain for \(h\) and \(\ell\)). Define consumption \(c = wh\) (whatever she earns) and treat leisure as a good with price \(w\). The labor problem then looks just like a regular consumer problem:

\(\max\;\; 96\ln c + 732\ln \ell \quad \text{s.t.}\quad c + w\ell = 69 w.\)

The strange result

Solving the FOCs gives the Marshallian (observed) hours of work:

\(h^M = 8\) hr regardless of \(w\) \(\;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{\partial h^M}{\partial w} = 0.\)

Whether Maya earns \$18 or \$24 an hour, she works the same 8 hours. (Cobb-Douglas-log on \((c, \ell)\): consumption rises, leisure stays.)

But that zero hides a story. The substitution piece (“leisure costs more, work more”) is positive. The income piece (“I'm richer, take more leisure”) is negative. The two exactly cancel — a knife edge. Next slide breaks it open.

Walkthrough 6.5 — +2 sub, −2 inc, zero net

Step 1 — find the compensated bundle (point \(A'\))

(a) “Friend pays Maya just enough to stay at old happiness\(\bar u_0\) at the new wage.” Set up expenditure-minimization:

\(\min\;c + 24\ell\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\; 96\ln c + 732 \ln \ell = \bar u_0.\)

(b) Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L} = c + 24\ell + \mu\,[\bar u_0 - 96\ln c - 732\ln\ell]\). FOCs:

\(1 = \mu\!\cdot\! 96/c \Rightarrow c = 96\mu, \quad 24 = \mu\!\cdot\! 732/\ell \Rightarrow \ell = 30.5\mu.\)

(c) Compute the anchor utility from the original (\$18) optimum \((c, \ell) = (144, 61)\):

\(\bar u_0 = 96\ln 144 + 732\ln 61 \approx 3486.3.\)

(d) Plug \(c = 96\mu, \ell = 30.5\mu\) into the constraint:

\(96\ln(96\mu) + 732\ln(30.5\mu) = \bar u_0\)

Apply log rule \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\):

\(96\ln 96 + 96\ln\mu + 732\ln 30.5 + 732\ln\mu = \bar u_0\)

Combine \(\ln\mu\) terms (\(96 + 732 = 828\)) and move constants right:

\(828\ln\mu = \bar u_0 - 96\ln 96 - 732\ln 30.5\)

\(= 3486.3 - 438.2 - 2501.8 \approx 546.3\)

\(\Rightarrow\; \ln\mu \approx 546.3/828 \approx 0.660 \;\Rightarrow\; \mu \approx e^{0.660} \approx 1.934.\)

(e) Read off the Hicksian bundle and the compensated hours of work:

\(c^H = 96\mu \approx 185.78, \;\; \ell^H = 30.5\mu \approx 59.00.\)

\(\boxed{\,h^H = 69 - 59 = 10\text{ hr.}\,}\)

Step 2 — substitution piece (\(A \to A'\))

Same happiness, but leisure now costs \$24/hr instead of \$18. Compare original Maya (\(h^M = 8\)) to compensated Maya (\(h^H = 10\)):

\(\Delta h^{\text{sub}} = h^H - h^M = 10 - 8 = \boxed{+2}\) hr/wk.

Sign-intuition: leisure is more expensive \(\Rightarrow\) work more.

Step 3 — income piece (\(A' \to B\))

Drop the hypothetical compensation (no more “friend's cash”) — Maya is now at her actual new-wage optimum at \(w' = \$24\). Compute her real Marshallian bundle (full income \(69 \times 24 = \$1{,}656\), Cobb-Douglas shares \(96/828\) and \(732/828\)):

\(c^M = 192, \;\; \ell^M = 61, \;\; h^M = 8\) hr.

\(\Delta h^{\text{inc}} = h^M - h^H = 8 - 10 = \boxed{-2}\) hr/wk.

Sign-intuition: at the higher wage Maya is genuinely richer in real terms \(\Rightarrow\) wants more leisure (normal good) \(\Rightarrow\) works less.

\(\boxed{\;\underbrace{0}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{+2}_{\text{sub}} + \underbrace{-2}_{\text{inc}}\;\text{hr/wk}.\;}\)

Flat hours in data hide two real \(\pm 2\) hr forces. Marshallian elasticity \(\approx 0.1\), Frisch \(\approx 0.5\): the gap is the income effect (Sigurdsson 2025).

TPS — Sample answers

Q1 — Data design

Panel data on low-income households at or near subsistence, with random variation in own-price of the candidate good (subsidies, vouchers, randomized retail discounts). Need pre/post quantity demanded plus income/expenditure totals to estimate \(\partial x_1^M/\partial I\) separately.

Q2 — Ruling out confounders

Supply shocks: use randomized subsidies, not market price moves. Quality changes: verify the good's attributes are constant across treatment arms. Veblen: select on subsistence-constrained populations where status signaling is implausible. Jensen-Miller did all three.

Pattern. Identifying Slutsky-violating behavior empirically is hard; you need EXPERIMENTAL variation in price. Observational scatter plots are not enough.

If groups skip the supply-shock confounder, push them: "What if rice price goes up because of a typhoon? Does that show Giffen?" (Answer: no, it shows a supply shock — you can't separate from a demand-side response without exogenous variation.) Jensen-Miller's contribution was the randomization, not the observation.

Key takeaways

  1. The Slutsky equation decomposes any Marshallian price response into substitution (Hicksian, fixed utility) and income (Marshallian, \(x_j \cdot \partial x_i^M / \partial I\)) effects.
  2. The expenditure function \(e(p, \bar u)\) is concave in \(p\), non-decreasing in each argument, HOD 1 in \(p\). Its derivative in \(p_j\) is Hicksian demand — Shephard's lemma.
  3. Own-price Hicksian demand is always \(\leq 0\). Marshallian own-price effect can flip sign for inferior + budget-share-heavy + subsistence goods — the Giffen good.
  4. Maya's knife-edge: with Cobb-Douglas-log preferences, substitution and income exactly cancel on a wage change — Marshallian labor supply has zero elasticity. Real labor-supply data (Sigurdsson 2025) shows non-zero Marshallian elasticity \(\approx 0.1\); the gap is utility-function shape.
  5. Policy. Price subsidies and cash transfers are not equivalent. The Slutsky structure tells you which channel each policy targets and which households benefit most.
