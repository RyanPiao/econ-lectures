From Principles to Theory: Arrow → Number

ECON 1116 — the arrow

  • A tax shifts supply up — price rises, quantity falls
  • Marginal benefit vs marginal cost, compared in one-unit steps
  • Diagrams give the direction of the change

ECON 2316 — the number

  • Solve the optimization to find how much — the exact \(P^{*},\,Q^{*}\)
  • Marginal \(=\) the derivative; the optimum is \(f'(x^{*})=0\)
  • And then measure the effect in data — is the number real?

Three ideas organize the whole book. (1) Almost every question is optimization under constraint. (2) Turning a graphical prediction into a numerical one needs calculus — hence a Calculus Corner in every chapter. (3) Modern micro treats those numbers as empirical objects to be measured, not just derived.

This is the orientation slide. Students arrive fluent in the qualitative diagrams of 1116. The promise of 2316 is threefold: compute the magnitude, do it with calculus quarantined to Calculus Corners (so the economics stays front and center), and take the resulting numbers to data. Keep returning to "arrow vs number" today — it is the unifying motif and it is exactly what the opening congestion-pricing story dramatizes.

Scarcity: The Economic Problem

Recall from Principles — 1116 §1.1

Before we start: what single problem do a student, a household, a firm, and the MTA all share?

Economics

The study of how individuals, firms, and societies make choices under scarcity.

Scarcity

The problem of having too many wants but too few resources to achieve them all.

Scarcity forces choices. If you could have everything you wanted, exactly when you wanted it, economics would not exist. It is the one problem every economic agent faces.

This course is microeconomics — we zoom in on the individual decision-maker (a consumer, a firm, one market), not macroeconomics’ economy-wide aggregates (GDP, inflation, unemployment).

Anchor slide before the hook. Open with the recall question (cold-call or think-pair): what do a student, a household, a firm, and the MTA share? Answer: scarcity. Define economics and scarcity, then the punchline — scarcity is THE problem; no scarcity, no economics. Sets up the next slide (scarcity is not the same as "scarce"/rarity) and then the Nine-Dollar Question as a concrete scarce-resource allocation.

Scarcity Is Not Rarity

Rarity — absolute quantity

A pink diamond is rare. But if you do not own one, it never touches your day — it forces no choices.

Scarcity — universal

June 2026: after the SpaceX IPO, Elon Musk becomes the world's first trillionaire — about \$1.3T. He still cannot buy more engineers, more land, or more hours than exist.

Money is a claim on resources, not a resource. Even \$1.3 trillion only bids for what already exists — so the question is never “how do we get more?” but “how do we get the most out of what there is?” That question has a name: optimization under scarcity.

The Musk example replaces the older "Bezos only has 24 hours" version, and it is a sharper point: 24 hours is a personal constraint students may read as trivia, whereas the factors of production are the constraint the whole economy runs into. Land the distinction explicitly - money is a CLAIM on resources, not a resource. Printing or earning more dollars does not create another engineer, another acre, or another factory; it only changes who gets to bid for them. Ask the room: "if the government handed everyone \$1M tomorrow, would there be more housing?" That is scarcity, and it is why the answer is always allocation rather than production of more. Numbers are verifiable: SpaceX listed on the Nasdaq 12 June 2026 at \$135/share (~\$1.77T valuation); Musk crossed \$1T that day and hit \$1.3T on 15 June after the stock rose 20%. Update the figure if it has moved by the time you teach this. This slide is the bridge into §1.1 - the next slide asks how allocation actually happens.

So How Do We Allocate Something Scarce?

Allocative efficiency: a scarce resource ends up with whoever values it most — the right goods reach the right people, and the last unit used is worth at least what it costs society.

Markets are usually how

A price steers a scarce resource to its highest-valued use — no central planner deciding who deserves it. This is one of the most reliable ideas in economics (and Ch 10 will say exactly when it works).

The MTA's goal

Put a price on scarce road space so the trips worth most get in — the delivery on a deadline drives; the optional joyride waits. That is allocative efficiency in action.

We formalize allocative efficiency — and when markets reach it — in §6 and Ch 10. (When they fail: Ch 17. Fittingly, congestion is itself a market failure the toll is fixing.) First, the headline number.

Delivers the punchline of the scarcity arc: economics allocates scarce resources, and the default tool is a market price. State the allocative-efficiency definition plainly (resource goes to whoever values it most), then the principle that prices usually do this well without a planner, hedged with "usually" because Part V is all about when they don't. The MTA frame: the $9 toll is a price on scarce road space whose JOB is to ration it to the highest-value trips. Note the nice irony for later (Ch 17): congestion is a negative externality, so the toll is a market-based fix for a market failure. Forward-ref the formal treatment (efficiency-two slide in the tour, plus Ch 10 welfare theorem) so this stays an intuitive first encounter, not a repeat. Then cue the Nine-Dollar Question.

The Nine-Dollar Question

The policy

Jan 5, 2025 — NYC switches on the first U.S. congestion-pricing program. Gantries below 60th St charge a peak \$9 to enter the central business district.

MTA Congestion Relief Zone — official program

The economics

A toll on a good with a congestion externality. Any Principles student can draw it — effective supply shifts up \$9, and trips fall.

But drawing the direction was never the hard part.

Policy intro + hook. Set the scene: after decades of proposals and lawsuits, the first U.S. cordon toll goes live below 60th St at a $9 peak. Frame it as a textbook congestion-externality toll - the kind any Principles student can draw. The cliffhanger is the last line: the direction (trips fall) was never in doubt; the whole policy fight was about the magnitude, which the next slide takes up.

It Needed a Number, Not a Direction

The MTA wasn't asking the direction. It needed a number: how much does traffic fall at \$9? At \$12? At \$15? Bond a \$15B capital plan against the revenue — that forces a minimum. Keep ambulances moving — another minimum. Don't enrage commuter-belt voters — a ceiling.

First-month data came fast. Graphs told the MTA the arrow. Calculus — arithmetic on real elasticities — told them where to stop.

§1.1

The Economic Problem

Optimization under scarcity — one template, every chapter

Why Nobody Builds a Sandwich From Scratch

Specialization and the gains from trade. A deli sandwich costs a few dollars because countless specialists — wheat farmers, beekeepers, dairies, bakers, toolmakers — each do the one thing they do best, then exchange.

Markets make it possible. Prices let specialists who will never meet coordinate — turning specialization into a sandwich you can actually buy.

No one designed that chain. No one runs it. Every market in this book is machinery for coordinating specialization — and the next slide draws the whole thing as one loop.

Andy George's How To Make Everything: a chicken sandwich for ~\$1,500 in 2015 by sourcing every ingredient himself; for the ten-year anniversary he forged his own tools and a flintlock and extracted raw materials, pushing it to ~\$12,680. Don't state the escalation aloud - the caption already does. Ask instead: "what exactly is he paying for?" The answer is everyone else's specialization, which he opted out of. The beat to stress is that the MARKET is the institution facilitating specialization and trade; prices let strangers coordinate with nobody in charge. That previews comparative advantage and hands directly to the circular-flow diagram next, which is the whole-economy version of the same coordination. Optional thread-seeding if you have time: the wage on labour and the rental on capital are the prices in this story, and they drive cost minimisation in Chs 7-9 - but do NOT put that on the slide on day one, and do not promise Ch 19 factor markets, which is off the fall schedule.

The Simplified Economy: Circular-Flow Diagram

Think of something you bought today — a coffee, a bagel, a snack. You will never meet the farmer who grew the beans, the baker, or the barista. Yet it reached you.

Millions of strangers, each pursuing their own ends, coordinated by prices — with no one in charge. Adam Smith called it the invisible hand.

To make a system this big and decentralized analyzable, economists lean on a few assumptions about how agents behave.

The zoom-out before the assumptions. Cold-call: what did you buy today? Coffee, bagel, snack. Who grew it, baked it, shipped it? You have no idea - and never will. The circular flow shows the structure: households and firms swap money for goods (product market, top) and money for inputs (factor market, bottom). Nobody coordinates it; prices do - Adam Smith's invisible hand (1776). The payoff line sets up the next slide: to model this giant decentralized system, we make simplifying behavioral assumptions - the Three Working Assumptions. Factor market (bottom loop) is labour and capital pricing - not covered this term; mention it only as the loop that closes the diagram.

Three Working Assumptions

Intermediate micro leans on these far harder than Principles did. Every model in the book rests on them.

1 — Agents optimize

Choose the best feasible option, not merely a good-enough one.

2 — Respond at the margin

Change a price, wage, or payoff and behavior adjusts — the adjustment is what calculus computes.

3 — Largely self-interested

Each pursues its own objective — yet prices can still coordinate them into a coherent outcome.

These are baselines, not laws of nature. Behavioral economics is all about where real people systematically break them.

Two Agents, the Same Problem

Consumer (you)

Wants: the most utility (satisfaction) from the bundle she buys.

Limited by: her income — the budget.

Formalized in Chapter 5.

Firm (business)

Wants: the most profit — which means producing efficiently, at least cost.

Limited by: its resources — funding, investment, inputs.

Formalized in Chapters 7–9.

Different objective, different constraint — but the identical structure: maximize something subject to a limit. One template covers both.

The two canonical agents, set up as a matched pair right before the general template. Consumer: maximize utility subject to the budget (Ch 5). Firm: maximize profit, which forces cost minimization / productive efficiency, subject to limited resources (Chs 7-9). The punchline is structural: both are "maximize an objective subject to a constraint" - which is exactly the max f(x) s.t. g(x) template on the next slide. Concrete agents first, abstraction second.

One Template Runs the Whole Book

\[ \max_{x \in X}\; f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g(x) \le 0 \]

Choose the feasible option \(x\) that makes the objective \(f(x)\) as large as possible, subject to a constraint that encodes the scarcity. Each chapter just changes the labels:

The constraint \(g\) is the scarcity (see the opening slides). Opportunity cost is the next-best alternative forgone (explicit + implicit). In Principles we named opportunity costs in words; here we quantify the tradeoff with a number.

The single most important slide of the chapter. The constraint binds — that is what makes the problem interesting; if scarcity weren't tight, no theory would be needed. Read the table left to right: the math problem is identical across consumer, producer, and policymaker; only the labels change. That is why one toolkit serves the whole book. The time-budget figure makes it concrete: 97 free hours split between study and paid work, with the frontier \(S+W=97\). Walkthrough 1.1 solves exactly this in a moment.

Application — Harvard & MIT Go Tuition-Free (2025)

From 2025–26, Harvard made tuition free under \$100K family income and waived full cost of attendance (over \$82K/yr) under \$200K. It's a textbook constrained-optimization problem:

Objective

maximize access / yield / socioeconomic diversity

Instrument

the aid schedule — expected family contribution at each income

Constraint

a finite aid budget (endowment + higher-income tuition)

Lowering the threshold raised enrollment in the \$50K–\$100K band, dropped its average family contribution to zero, and raised total outlay. Holding the budget fixed, Harvard implicitly judged the extra access worth more than the next-best use of the marginal dollar.

This is the exact decision logic we formalize in Chapter 5 with the Lagrangian — one tool for every problem of this shape, from tuition-waiver rules to a consumer's choice between coffee and rent. Optimization is not confined to firms: households, governments, and students all run it.

MIT ran the same play. In Nov 2024 it made tuition free for families under \$200K (up from \$140K) and a full ride — tuition, housing, dining, fees — under \$100K, backed by ~\$167M in need-based aid. Same objective, instrument, and constraint; different thresholds. [MIT News]

The Harvard case makes explicit what the congestion story left implicit: optimization is universal. The actors and constraints differ — a household with a grocery cart, a government setting a carbon tax, a student choosing the next hour — but the mathematical problem is the same. "Implicitly deciding the marginal access was worth more than the marginal dollar's alternative use" is precisely the Lagrange-multiplier interpretation students will meet in Ch 5: \(\lambda\) is the shadow value of relaxing the budget. Source: Harvard College Financial Aid, 2025–26 policy; MIT News, "Undergraduates with family income below $200,000 can expect to attend MIT tuition-free starting in 2025" (Nov 20, 2024), news.mit.edu/2024/mit-tuition-undergraduates-family-income-1120.

Walkthrough 1.1 — A Student's Time Budget

Problem. Maya splits 97 free hours between study \(S\) and paid work \(W\) at \$18/hr. Study's value falls in blocks: \$30/hr (hrs 1–10), \$20 (11–20), \$15 (21–30), \$0 after. How should she split the 97 hours?

Solve it in steps:

1. List each study block's marginal value (MV).

2. Compare each block to the flat \$18 wage.

3. Give each hour to whichever pays more at the margin — study while MV beats the wage.

Study beats the wage through hour 20; at hour 21 its value drops below \$18.

Setup slide. Read the problem, then lay out the method as three steps. The table is the engine: each study block's marginal value next to the flat $18 wage. Cold-call: where does study stop being worth it? The next slide solves it visually with the marginal-value staircase.

Walkthrough 1.1 — Solution

Study wins through hour 20 (\$20 > \$18); at hour 21 it drops to \$15 < \$18 — the crossover.

\(S^{*}=20\) (study), \(W^{*}=77\) (work)

\[ V^{*} = \underbrace{10(30)+10(20)}_{\text{study}} + \underbrace{77(18)}_{\text{work}} = \$1{,}886 \]

Takeaway — the optimum is where the margins meet

The last hour of study is worth as much as the last hour of work — this compare-at-the-margin, stop-at-the-crossover rule is the marginal thinking we formalize in §3. Raise the wage to \$25 and the switch flips to hour 11 — higher wages crowd out studying. In Ch 5 we swap the step function for a smooth utility function and solve the same problem with the Lagrangian. Maya recurs in Chs 5, 6, and 19.

The chart carries the intuition: study's marginal value is the descending blue staircase ($30, $20, $15, $0); the wage is the flat orange line at $18. Take every hour whose bar sits ABOVE the wage line and switch the moment it drops below - the optimum is the crossover at S* = 20, W* = 77, value $1,886. The comparative static (wage to $25 shifts S* from 20 to 10) is the engine of the whole book.

The Marginal Is the Slope of the Tangent

Marginal cost of the \(q\)th unit \(\approx C(q)-C(q-1)\) — the slope of a secant. Shrink its span to zero and the secant becomes the tangent:

\[ MC(q) = \frac{dC(q)}{dq} \]

Harmless formula, big consequences. "Where should the firm stop?" becomes "where is \(\frac{dR}{dq}-\frac{dC}{dq}=0\)?" — a one-line solve that in Principles needed a hand-drawn diagram or an exhaustive table.

The same first-order logic runs the rest of the book: \(p = MC\) in Ch 9, \(MR = MC\) in Ch 11, and \(f'(x^{*}) = 0\) (the FOC) in every optimization from Ch 5 on.

Recall from 1116 — §1.3

A marginal quantity is the change in a total from one more unit — compared in discrete one-unit steps in Principles. Here it is almost always a derivative: if total benefit is \(B(q)\), marginal benefit is \(MB(q)=dB/dq\). Cleaner algebraically, sharper intuitively.

The mindset jump. A Principles student can say "produce where \(MR=MC\)"; an intermediate student can find that quantity given the functions. The gap is calculus. The leverage is compression: a sentence in graphs becomes a line in math. The figure foreshadows HeadsUp 1.3 — the tangent slope (marginal) and the chord slope (average) are visibly different objects, and using one when you want the other is a classic trap.

§1.2

Models and Evidence

Simplified maps — useful because they leave things out — and how to judge a claim

Deciding What to Leave Out

Reality is billions of interacting decisions — no one measures them all at once. Economists look through models: deliberately simplified maps that keep only what matters for the question at hand.

Assumptions are the filter. Not a claim the world is simple — a choice about what to ignore so the forces you care about stand out.

Combines the old complexity slide with the assumptions filter — one idea, one slide. The funnel image was cut; this filter image carries both halves. Give a concrete pair: ignoring transport costs is harmless if you want the direction of a tariff's effect, and disastrous if you want to know who loses when a factory closes. Same assumption, different question, opposite verdict — which is exactly why the test is "would relaxing it change THIS answer," not "is it true." The subway map on the next slide is the worked example of this principle.

A Model Is a Subway Map, Not a Photograph

Useless for walking. Perfect for deciding where to change trains.

A good model picks the actors and their objectives, the environment (fixed / variable / random / strategic), and — hardest — what to ignore.

The map keeps

station order · interchanges · which line

The map throws away

true distances · street geometry · everything above ground

Heads Up 1.4 — models are tools, not portraits

Don't ask “is this assumption true?” (almost none are) — ask “would relaxing it change the result I care about?” Chs 11–17 exist to relax the perfect-competition assumptions, one at a time.

The keeps/throws-away pair is the whole lesson — run it as call-and-response before revealing: "what does a subway map deliberately get WRONG?" Someone always says distances. A model is useful because it leaves things out. The art is knowing when an assumption is safe and when it is load-bearing: ignoring transport costs is harmless if you want the direction of a tariff's effect, disastrous if you want to know who loses when a factory closes. Same assumption, different question, opposite verdict. Comparative statics — how the equilibrium moves when a parameter changes — is introduced properly in Ch 2 §2.3; don't open it here. Application thread: Amazon's warehouse robots are a firm choosing the cost-minimising \(K,L\) mix (Chs 7–8); the model strips out hiring frictions and morale and still predicts robots per dollar of wage increase.

What We Assume — and Where We Relax It

Consumer theory · Chs 4–6

Preferences are complete, transitive and stable. Prices are known. Income is fixed.

Producer theory · Chs 7–9

Firms minimise cost and maximise profit. Technology is given.

Perfect competition · Ch 10 — the benchmark: many small firms · identical goods · free entry · full information · price-taking.

Then relax one at a time

Ch 11–12drop price-taking → one seller sets the price
Ch 13–14drop rivals ignored → strategic interaction
Ch 15drop outcomes known → risk and expected utility
Ch 16drop everyone knows the same → lemons, signalling
Ch 17drop the decider bears all costs → externalities
This is Heads Up 1.4 made concrete, and the single most useful slide to point back to all term. The move: Ch 10 is not "the chapter where markets work" — it is the chapter that states the five assumptions everything afterwards dismantles. When a student asks in Week 10 "why are we doing this?", the answer is always "we dropped one assumption from the Ch 10 list; which one?" Ask them here which assumption the \$9 toll violates (the decider bears all costs — drivers impose delay on everyone else), and they have pre-slotted Ch 17 on day one. Consumer and producer theory sit above the line because their assumptions are not relaxed in this course — behavioural economics would do that, and it is not on our schedule.

Correlation Is Not Causation

When \(X\) and \(Y\) move together, the data alone can't tell you which story is true. Three very different worlds produce the same scatter:

Real causation — \(X\) genuinely drives \(Y\). The sun melts the ice cream.
Hidden common cause — a third variable \(Z\) drives both. Rain brings out umbrellas and boots; neither causes the other.
Coincidence — no link at all, just chance or a shared time trend. A cactus and a telephone.

A correlation coefficient can't tell these apart — only a research design can.

Visual intro to the empirical section: same scatter, three worlds. Real causation (sun to ice cream), confounding by a hidden Z (rain to umbrellas and boots), and pure coincidence or shared time trend (cactus and telephone). This sets up the next slide's drill, where the suspects become X to Y, Y to X (reverse), and Z to X and Y (omitted variable). The punchline that carries the rest of the chapter: a correlation coefficient is design-free and cannot separate these cases; only a research design can. Do not go further - the design vocabulary is deliberately out of scope for 2316.

Positive vs. Normative

Positive — what is

“At \$9, about a million fewer vehicles entered.”

Checkable against data. Can be wrong.

Normative — what ought

“The toll should be \$9.”

Needs a value judgement. No dataset settles it.

This course is mostly positive. It sharpens normative arguments — it does not decide them.

Placed immediately before Poll #2 because the poll is exactly this distinction: \$30 maximises revenue (positive), the MTA chose \$9 (normative — they were optimising something else). Give the toll example, then one from their own lives. Students routinely smuggle "should" into an answer that only asked "what happens" — flag it now and you can point back to this slide all term. Ch 2 revisits it once they can solve for equilibrium algebraically.

Drive It: The Toll-Revenue Curve

\(Q_d(P)=300{,}000-5{,}000P\) · \(R(P)=300{,}000P-5{,}000P^{2}\) · marginal revenue \(R'(P)=300{,}000-10{,}000P\)

Press the presets or drag the toll. The curve is the revenue parabola; the moving dot is the current toll; the readout shows daily entries, daily revenue, and \(R'(P)\). The teaching beat: below \$30, \(R'(P)>0\) — raising the toll still raises revenue; at \$30, \(R'(P)=0\) — the FOC peak; above \$30, \(R'(P)<0\) — past the top, raising the toll loses revenue. The \$9 marker sits well left of the peak, which is the whole point: the MTA is not on the revenue-max toll because revenue is only one of several constraints. Poll #2 — The Math Says \$30. They Chose \$9.

Walkthrough 1.5 found the revenue-maximizing toll is \$30, yet the MTA set \$9. What's the best explanation?

A) The MTA made an arithmetic mistake
B) \$9 optimizes a different, constrained objective (revenue target + traffic + politics)
C) Demand is perfectly inelastic at \$9
D) A \$30 toll would violate the law of demand

45 sec to vote

Press 1 for a timer; down arrow reveals. Answer is B. The \$30 toll maximizes revenue alone; the MTA's actual objective is multi-objective and constrained — raise ~\$550M/yr to service \$15B of bonds, cut traffic enough for ambulances and speeds, but not so much that the commuter-belt coalition revolts. \$9 is the optimum of that problem. A is wrong: the \$30 figure is correct for the revenue objective. C is wrong: at \$9 demand is downward-sloping, not vertical. D misstates the law of demand — a \$30 toll simply moves up the same demand curve to lower quantity.

Answer: B — A Different, Constrained Objective

\$30 is the answer to "maximize revenue." The MTA solves a harder problem: hit a bonding target and a traffic floor without a political revolt. \$9 is that problem's optimum — the gap between the two is the whole lesson of the chapter.

The takeaway: a simple model gives a sharp number for a simple objective. Real policy adds constraints — and the constrained optimum is a different number. Both are "optimization."

Chapter 1 in a Nutshell

1. Scarcity → choice → optimization. Every chapter is \(\max f(x)\) s.t. \(g(x)\le 0\) — only the labels change.

2. Graphs give the arrow; calculus gives the number. Intermediate micro is mostly about the magnitude.

3. Marginal thinking. The marginal is the derivative; the optimum is the first-order condition \(f'(x^{*})=0\).

4. Models are purposeful simplifications. Judge by what they help you see; ask if a relaxed assumption changes the result.

5. Claims need evidence — and a category. Correlation is not causation; positive questions are settled by data, normative ones are not.

6. Positive ≠ normative. "The market cleared" is a fact; "the price is right" is a value judgment. Keep them apart.

The six moves of the chapter, in order.
