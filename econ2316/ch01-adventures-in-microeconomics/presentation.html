Scarcity: The Economic Problem

Recall from Principles — 1116 §1.1

Before we start: what single problem do a student, a household, a firm, and the MTA all share?

Economics

The study of how individuals, firms, and societies make choices under scarcity.

Scarcity

The problem of having too many wants but too few resources to achieve them all.

Scarcity forces choices. If you could have everything you wanted, exactly when you wanted it, economics would not exist. It is the one problem every economic agent faces.

Anchor slide before the hook. Open with the recall question (cold-call or think-pair): what do a student, a household, a firm, and the MTA share? Answer: scarcity. Define economics and scarcity, then the punchline — scarcity is THE problem; no scarcity, no economics. Sets up the next slide (scarcity is not the same as "scarce"/rarity) and then the Nine-Dollar Question as a concrete scarce-resource allocation.

Scarcity Is Not Rarity

Rarity — absolute quantity

A pink diamond is rare. But if you do not own one, it does not touch your daily life — it forces no choices.

Scarcity — universal

Jeff Bezos has more wealth than entire nations, yet he is bound by 24 hours a day — he cannot buy a 25th. His time, energy, and attention are scarce.

So what is economics about? Addressing scarcity — allocating scarce resources to their best uses. Road space below 60th St is one of them — and in 2025 the MTA finally had to put a price on it.

The classic distinction, with the book's own example (1116 §1.1: scarce even for billionaires). Rarity = absolute physical quantity, forces no choices on a non-owner. Scarcity = universal, binds even the richest person because time/attention cannot be bought past the limit. Land the bridge: economics allocates scarce resources; Manhattan road space is exactly such a resource; the MTA's congestion toll is an allocation mechanism. That cues the Nine-Dollar Question on the next slide.

So How Do We Allocate Something Scarce?

Allocative efficiency: a scarce resource ends up with whoever values it most — the right goods reach the right people, and the last unit used is worth at least what it costs society.

Markets are usually how

A price steers a scarce resource to its highest-valued use — no central planner deciding who deserves it. This is one of the most reliable ideas in economics (and Ch 10 will say exactly when it works).

The MTA's goal

Put a price on scarce road space so the trips worth most get in — the delivery on a deadline drives; the optional joyride waits. That is allocative efficiency in action.

We formalize allocative efficiency — and when markets reach it — in §6 and Ch 10. (When they fail: Ch 17. Fittingly, congestion is itself a market failure the toll is fixing.) First, the headline number.

Delivers the punchline of the scarcity arc: economics allocates scarce resources, and the default tool is a market price. State the allocative-efficiency definition plainly (resource goes to whoever values it most), then the principle that prices usually do this well without a planner, hedged with "usually" because Part V is all about when they don't. The MTA frame: the $9 toll is a price on scarce road space whose JOB is to ration it to the highest-value trips. Note the nice irony for later (Ch 17): congestion is a negative externality, so the toll is a market-based fix for a market failure. Forward-ref the formal treatment (efficiency-two slide in the tour, plus Ch 10 welfare theorem) so this stays an intuitive first encounter, not a repeat. Then cue the Nine-Dollar Question.

The Nine-Dollar Question

The policy

Jan 5, 2025 — NYC switches on the first U.S. congestion-pricing program. Gantries below 60th St charge a peak \$9 to enter the central business district.

The economics

A toll on a good with a congestion externality. Any Principles student can draw it — effective supply shifts up \$9, and trips fall.

But drawing the direction was never the hard part.

The cliffhanger is the last line: the direction (trips fall) was never in doubt; the whole policy fight was about the magnitude, which the next slide takes up.

§1.1

The Economic Problem

Optimization under scarcity — one template, every chapter

Why Nobody Builds a Sandwich From Scratch

Specialization and the gains from trade. A deli sandwich costs a few dollars because countless specialists — wheat farmers, beekeepers, dairies, bakers, toolmakers — each do the one thing they are relatively best at, then exchange.

Markets make it possible. A market is what facilitates specialization and trade — prices and exchange let specialists who will never meet coordinate, turning specialization into a sandwich you can actually buy.

Every market in this book is machinery for coordinating that specialization. The deeper you go alone, the more the cost explodes. The wage paying for all that effort returns as a factor price in Ch 19.

The gains-from-trade hook with the HTME thumbnail. Andy George's How To Make Everything: a chicken sandwich for ~$1,500 in 2015 by sourcing every ingredient; for the 10-year anniversary (2025) he forged his own tools and a flintlock and extracted raw materials, pushing the cost to ~$12,680. The escalation IS the lesson: self-sufficiency is absurdly expensive. The new beat to stress: the MARKET is the institution that facilitates specialization and trade - prices let strangers coordinate without anyone in charge. That previews comparative advantage and Ch 19 factor markets, and leads into the circular-flow diagram next (the whole-economy version of the same coordination).

The Simplified Economy: Circular-Flow Diagram

Think of something you bought today — a coffee, a bagel, a snack. You will never meet the farmer who grew the beans, the baker, or the barista. Yet it reached you.

Millions of strangers, each pursuing their own ends, coordinated by prices — with no one in charge. Adam Smith called it the invisible hand.

To make a system this big and decentralized analyzable, economists lean on a few assumptions about how agents behave.

The zoom-out before the assumptions. Cold-call: what did you buy today? Coffee, bagel, snack. Who grew it, baked it, shipped it? You have no idea - and never will. The circular flow shows the structure: households and firms swap money for goods (product market, top) and money for inputs (factor market, bottom). Nobody coordinates it; prices do - Adam Smith's invisible hand (1776). The payoff line sets up the next slide: to model this giant decentralized system, we make simplifying behavioral assumptions - the Three Working Assumptions. Factor market (bottom loop) is where Ch 19 will live.

Three Working Assumptions

Intermediate micro leans on these far harder than Principles did. Every model in the book rests on them.

1 — Agents optimize

Choose the best feasible option, not merely a good-enough one.

2 — Respond at the margin

Change a price, wage, or payoff and behavior adjusts — the adjustment is what calculus computes.

3 — Largely self-interested

Each pursues its own objective — yet prices can still coordinate them into a coherent outcome.

These are baselines, not laws of nature. Ch 18 (Behavioral) is all about where real people systematically break them.

Two Agents, the Same Problem

Consumer (you)

Wants: the most utility (satisfaction) from the bundle she buys.

Limited by: her income — the budget.

Formalized in Chapter 5.

Firm (business)

Wants: the most profit — which means producing efficiently, at least cost.

Limited by: its resources — funding, investment, inputs.

Formalized in Chapters 7–9.

Different objective, different constraint — but the identical structure: maximize something subject to a limit. One template covers both.

The two canonical agents, set up as a matched pair right before the general template. Consumer: maximize utility subject to the budget (Ch 5). Firm: maximize profit, which forces cost minimization / productive efficiency, subject to limited resources (Chs 7-9). The punchline is structural: both are "maximize an objective subject to a constraint" - which is exactly the max f(x) s.t. g(x) template on the next slide. Concrete agents first, abstraction second.

One Template Runs the Whole Book

\[ \max_{x \in X}\; f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g(x) \le 0 \]

Application — Harvard & MIT Go Tuition-Free (2025)

From 2025–26, Harvard made tuition free under \$100K family income and waived full cost of attendance (over \$82K/yr) under \$200K. It's a textbook constrained-optimization problem:

Objective

maximize access / yield / socioeconomic diversity

Instrument

the aid schedule — expected family contribution at each income

Constraint

a finite aid budget (endowment + higher-income tuition)

Lowering the threshold raised enrollment in the \$50K–\$100K band, dropped its average family contribution to zero, and raised total outlay. Holding the budget fixed, Harvard implicitly judged the extra access worth more than the next-best use of the marginal dollar.

This is the exact decision logic we formalize in Chapter 5 with the Lagrangian — one tool for every problem of this shape, from tuition-waiver rules to a consumer's choice between coffee and rent. Optimization is not confined to firms: households, governments, and students all run it.

MIT ran the same play. In Nov 2024 it made tuition free for families under \$200K (up from \$140K) and a full ride — tuition, housing, dining, fees — under \$100K, backed by ~\$167M in need-based aid. Same objective, instrument, and constraint; different thresholds. [MIT News]

The Harvard case makes explicit what the congestion story left implicit: optimization is universal. The actors and constraints differ — a household with a grocery cart, a government setting a carbon tax, a student choosing the next hour — but the mathematical problem is the same. "Implicitly deciding the marginal access was worth more than the marginal dollar's alternative use" is precisely the Lagrange-multiplier interpretation students will meet in Ch 5: \(\lambda\) is the shadow value of relaxing the budget. Source: Harvard College Financial Aid, 2025–26 policy; MIT News, "Undergraduates with family income below $200,000 can expect to attend MIT tuition-free starting in 2025" (Nov 20, 2024), news.mit.edu/2024/mit-tuition-undergraduates-family-income-1120.

Solve it in steps:

1. List each study block's marginal value (MV).

2. Compare each block to the flat \$18 wage.

3. Give each hour to whichever pays more at the margin — study while MV beats the wage.

The comparative static (wage to $25 shifts S* from 20 to 10) is the engine of the whole book.

§1.2

Equilibrium

When does the system rest? — mutual best response, not stillness

Market Equilibrium

Equilibrium is the price \(P^{*}\) where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: \(Q^{d}=Q^{s}\). The market clears — no one wants to change.

Above \(P^{*}\): surplus. \(Q^{s}>Q^{d}\) — unsold stock piles up, so sellers cut the price.

Below \(P^{*}\): shortage. \(Q^{d}>Q^{s}\) — buyers compete, so the price gets bid up.

Both forces point back to \(E\) — the self-correction that makes \(E\) a rest point, the "mutual best-response" we unpack next.

The concrete picture behind 'the market clears.' Walk the cross: demand down, supply up, they meet at E = (Q* = 70,000 lbs/day, P* = $1.75/lb). Then the off-equilibrium forces: ABOVE P*, quantity supplied beats quantity demanded - a surplus - unsold stock forces sellers to cut price (the orange wedge). BELOW P*, quantity demanded beats quantity supplied - a shortage - buyers bid the price up (the red wedge). Both arrows point back to E, which is exactly why E is a rest point. That sets up the next slide's abstraction: equilibrium = mutual best-response. Superscripts: Q^d demand, Q^s supply; star = equilibrium value.

Equilibrium Is a Mutual Best Response

Wrong intuition

"Equilibrium" = a system at rest because nothing is happening.

Right intuition

Each agent's action is already the optimal response to every other's. No one wants to change. Shock it → agents re-optimize → a new mutual best-response.

The interesting questions

Which way does the system move? How fast does it return?

Equilibrium is at rest not because nothing happens but because each agent's action is already the optimal response to everyone else's. The egg market (2022-25): avian flu culled ~168M hens, an inward supply shift against steep demand that landed almost entirely on price. Theory (the S/D shift) predicts the direction; data (the FRED spike to $4.95, +96%) shows the size. The short-run/long-run distinction returns in Chs 8-10. FRED APU0000708111.

Information Is the Key

When markets are large and information is transparent, even self-interested individuals can produce socially efficient outcomes — and when it isn't, they don't.

The Movie Theater — it works

Roughly equal lines — nobody is in charge.

Self-interest: minimize my own wait time.

Information: full — I see every line at a glance.

Result: each person picking the shortest line yields the shortest collective wait. The invisible hand works.

The Traffic Jam — it fails

Lane-changing — can't see what's beyond the car ahead.

Self-interest: get ahead of the car ahead of me.

Information: incomplete — I can't see the system-level jam I'm causing.

Result: the same rational behavior worsens the jam — everyone slower. The invisible hand fails.

Same self-interest. Same rationality. Different information → different outcome.  Markets need good information to clear efficiently — Part V (Chs 15–18) is the theory of when they don't.

The invisible hand isn't magic - it depends on information. In the theater everyone sees every line, so picking the shortest line individually produces the shortest TOTAL ticket time. On the highway drivers see only the car ahead, so lane-changing to gain one slot creates a brake wave that slows everyone behind - a negative externality. Same rationality, opposite outcomes. Bridge: this is exactly why Ch 10 (the welfare theorem and its limits), Ch 16 (asymmetric information), and Ch 17 (externalities) exist - they catalog the conditions under which the invisible hand fails.

Surge Pricing = Equilibrium in Real Time

A rainstorm hits; a concert lets out. Demand spikes. Uber's surge multiplier lifts the price until riders willing to pay match drivers willing to drive — the market clears.

More efficient: shorter wait, cars actually show up.

Less "fair": riders pay a higher fare.

Equilibrium only clears the market

It tells us the price that makes quantity demanded meet quantity supplied — not whether that price is fair or "right." That is a normative question. (Modeled in Ch 2; as price discrimination in Ch 12.)

The surge example carries two payoffs. First, surge pricing IS equilibrium on a five-minute clock - the price moves until the market clears, exactly like the egg market. The figure shows why a price cap backfires during a surge: at the normal fare, quantity demanded (22) far exceeds quantity supplied (4), so you get a shortage - long waits, no cars - while the surge price clears it at a shorter wait but a higher fare. Second, the normative caveat (HeadsUp): clearing is a positive fact; whether the surge price is FAIR is a separate value judgment the model cannot answer. This reinforces the positive-vs-normative firewall and the efficiency-vs-equity tradeoff from earlier.

Theory and Data — Does Surge Pricing Actually Work?

Two natural experiments from Uber's own data: the same model, tested against reality.

Surge OFF → the system breaks

New Year's Eve, NYC (Dec 31, 2014). A glitch left surge pricing off for a stretch. With the fare stuck at its normal level, riders far outnumbered available drivers — completion rates collapsed and waits spiked. A textbook shortage.

Surge ON → the system works

Ariana Grande concert, MSG (Mar 21, 2015). As the crowd poured out, surge kicked in. The higher fare drew more drivers and rationed rides to those who valued them most — completion rates held near 100 percent. The market kept clearing.

The model's prediction, in Uber's own data: let the price rise → the market clears; freeze it → shortage. That is an efficiency result — it still says nothing about whether the surge fare is fair.

Source: Hall, Kendrick & Nosko, "The Effects of Uber's Surge Pricing: A Case Study" (2015); covered in The Economist, "A fare shake" (May 14, 2016).

Theory meets data, and it closes the surge arc. The Hall-Kendrick-Nosko case study uses two events as natural experiments. (1) NYE 2014: a surge OUTAGE - the algorithm failed to raise prices - so the fare was effectively capped; the result was exactly the shortage the S/D diagram predicts: completion rates crashed, waits spiked. (2) The Ariana Grande concert: surge worked normally and completion rates stayed near 100 percent. The takeaway is the chapter's arrow-to-number and positive-vs-normative thread: the data confirm surge CLEARS the market (efficiency) but the study cannot tell us whether the price is fair - that is a value judgment. If eggs cleared at \$5, that does not make \$5 the "right" price. Equilibrium means the market cleared — a positive fact. Poll #1 — Did the Market Get the "Right" Price?

The egg market cleared at about \$5 a dozen during the avian-flu shock. Is \$5 the "right" price?

A) Yes — a market price is always the efficient price
B) No — "cleared" is a positive fact; "right" is a separate normative question
C) No — \$5 is too high, so it can't be a real equilibrium
D) Only if buyers and sellers both like the price

45 sec to vote

Press 1 for a timer; down arrow reveals. Answer is B. Equilibrium is a positive statement — the quantity sellers want to sell equals the quantity buyers want to buy at the prevailing price. It carries no claim about fairness or efficiency. A states the welfare theorem as if it were automatic; it holds only under the Ch 10 conditions and breaks under the Ch 17 ones. C confuses a value judgment ("too high") with the definition of clearing. D describes neither a positive nor a normative condition correctly — clearing does not require anyone to "like" the price.

Answer: B — Positive ≠ Normative

"The market cleared" describes what happened. Whether the outcome is fair or efficient is a value-laden question that needs welfare analysis — efficient under perfect competition (Ch 10), inefficient under externalities (Ch 17).

Trap A: the welfare theorem (competitive equilibrium maximizes surplus) is a result with conditions, not a definition. Spillovers, market power, or missing markets break it.

§1.3

Marginal Thinking

Why we need calculus — the marginal is the derivative

How Do People Make Decisions?

Economists have a simple answer: people compare the additional benefits and additional costs of an action.

Economists use the word “marginal” to mean one additional.

Rational people take an action if and only if the marginal benefit outweighs the marginal cost.

Go

Marginal Benefit > Marginal Cost

→ take the action

Stop

Marginal Cost > Marginal Benefit

→ don't

The decision rule that runs the whole book, stated in plain words before the calculus. "Marginal" = one additional. Rational agents act if and only if the marginal benefit of the next unit exceeds its marginal cost. Go when MB > MC; stop when MC > MB. The optimum is exactly where they meet - which the next slide shows as a graph and the rest of the chapter sharpens into f'(x*) = 0.

The Logic of the Snooze Button

Each day you're tempted to sleep in instead of attending your morning class. How do you decide?

Marginal analysis

Not the total enjoyment of sleep vs the total value of class — it's the benefit of one more morning of sleep (which falls) versus the cost of missing one more class (which rises).

You stop when the cost of one more action outweighs its benefit — the same logic whether you're a student sleeping in or a firm hiring one more worker.

The canonical marginal-thinking example. The trap is comparing TOTALS (I love sleep / class matters) instead of MARGINS. Reframe: the benefit of ONE MORE hour of sleep falls; the cost of missing ONE MORE class rises. Optimal = where MB meets MC. The same rule scales to a firm hiring one more worker (Ch 19) - the universality is the point.

First, the Simple Case: A Straight Line

Suppose weight loss were linear — the same pounds lost every hour. The slope is constant: rise / run \(= 2/4 = 0.5\) lb per hour.

Why it's tempting

One number describes the whole relationship — no calculus needed.

Why it's not realistic

It never slows down: 100 hrs → 50 lbs, 1,000 hrs → 500 lbs. Real bodies hit diminishing returns and plateau — so the line bends into a curve.

Start with the case students already know from algebra: a straight line has ONE slope everywhere (rise/run = 0.5 lb/hr). It is tempting because a single number captures everything - no calculus. But it is unrealistic: a constant rate means you would lose weight forever at the same pace (1,000 hours, 500 pounds), which bodies obviously do not do. Reality has diminishing returns - the first hours shed a lot, later hours little - so the line bends into a curve. That sets up the next slide: on a curve the slope changes point to point, and capturing it needs the derivative.

The Realistic Case: Slope That Changes

A real curve bends, so its slope differs at every point. A derivative is that slope at a single point — the rate of change right there.

Diminishing returns: steep early (slope ≈ 1), nearly flat later (slope ≈ 0) — no single number works.

Notation

Weight lost \(= f(\text{hours})\), i.e. \(y = f(x)\). Its derivative — how a change in \(x\) affects \(y\) — is written \(\dfrac{df(x)}{dx}\) or \(f'(x)\).

The payoff of the linear setup, with notation folded in. On a curve the slope is different at every point - the derivative is the slope AT A POINT. Weight loss shows diminishing returns: early tangent steep (about 1, losing fast), late tangent flat (about 0, plateau), so no single rise/run number captures it. Notation: weight lost = f(hours) is just y = f(x); the derivative is df/dx (Leibniz) or f'(x) (prime) - same thing. Hands into the secant-to-tangent slide and the FOC.

Calculus Corner 1.1 — First-Order Conditions

At any interior optimum of a smooth \(f\): \[ f'(x^{*}) = 0 \]

At the top of a smooth hill the tangent is flat. If \(f'(x^{*})>0\), move right and \(f\) rises; if \(f'(x^{*})<0\), move left — so the slope must be zero. The SOC sorts max from min: \(f''(x^{*})\le 0\) for a maximum.

Worked example — maximize \(\pi(q)=10q-0.5q^{2}\)

FOC  \(\pi'(q)=10-q=0 \Rightarrow q^{*}=10\)

SOC  \(\pi''(q)=-1<0\) ✓ (max)

Profit  \(\pi(10)=\$50\)

Forgot how to take a derivative? Press ↓ for the toolkit — constant, power rule, and sums cover nearly everything we use.

The workhorse of the whole book, paired with the derivative toolkit below it (press down). FOC f'(x*)=0 is necessary, not sufficient; the SOC sorts max from min. Figure: the profit parabola peaks at q*=10 with a flat tangent. Worked example reads left to right: FOC gives q*=10, SOC confirms a max, profit is $50. Generalizes to many variables (Lagrangian, Ch 5) and corners later.

A. Derivative of a Constant

In general, if we have

\(f(x)=c \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dfrac{df(x)}{dx}=f'(x)=0\)

Example

\(f(x)=1000\;\) so \(\;c=1000\), and \(\;f'(x)=0\)

next rule

A flat line has zero slope - a constant does not change, so its rate of change is zero. f(x)=1000 -> f'(x)=0.

B. Power Rule

In general, if we have

\(f(x)=c\,x^{\alpha} \quad\Rightarrow\quad f'(x)=c\,\alpha\,x^{\alpha-1}\)

Example

\(f(x)=20x^{3}\;\) so \(\;c=20,\ \alpha=3\), and \(\;f'(x)=20(3)x^{2}=60x^{2}\)

next rule

The workhorse: bring the exponent down as a multiplier, then subtract one from the exponent. f(x)=20x^3 -> f'(x)=60x^2. This single rule handles almost every cost, revenue, and utility function in the course.

C. Addition & Subtraction Rule

In general, if we have

\(f(x)=g(x)\pm h(x) \quad\Rightarrow\quad f'(x)=g'(x)\pm h'(x)\)

Example

\(f(x)=30x+5x^{2}\;\Rightarrow\; f'(x)=30+10x\)

That's the toolkit — press → to continue.

Differentiate term by term: the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives. g(x)=30x -> 30; h(x)=5x^2 -> 10x; so f'(x)=30+10x. Combine with the power rule and you can differentiate any polynomial - which is most of what this course needs.

The Marginal Is the Slope of the Tangent

Marginal cost of the \(q\)th unit \(\approx C(q)-C(q-1)\) — the slope of a secant. Shrink its span to zero and the secant becomes the tangent:

\[ MC(q) = \frac{dC(q)}{dq} \]

Harmless formula, big consequences. "Where should the firm stop?" becomes "where is \(\frac{dR}{dq}-\frac{dC}{dq}=0\)?" — a one-line solve that in Principles needed a hand-drawn diagram or an exhaustive table.

The same first-order logic runs the rest of the book: \(p = MC\) in Ch 9, \(MR = MC\) in Ch 11, and \(f'(x^{*}) = 0\) (the FOC) in every optimization from Ch 5 on.

Recall from 1116 — §1.3

A marginal quantity is the change in a total from one more unit — compared in discrete one-unit steps in Principles. Here it is almost always a derivative: if total benefit is \(B(q)\), marginal benefit is \(MB(q)=dB/dq\). Cleaner algebraically, sharper intuitively.

The mindset jump. A Principles student can say "produce where \(MR=MC\)"; an intermediate student can find that quantity given the functions. The gap is calculus. The leverage is compression: a sentence in graphs becomes a line in math. The figure foreshadows HeadsUp 1.3 — the tangent slope (marginal) and the chord slope (average) are visibly different objects, and using one when you want the other is a classic trap.

Walkthrough 1.4 — Netflix's Ad-Tier Price Hike

Problem. 80M ad-tier subs at \$7.99/mo. A \$1 hike to \$8.99 causes 2% to churn over the year. Net change in annual subscription revenue (ignore ads)?

1. Old: \(80\times 7.99\times 12 \approx \$7{,}670\)M.

2. New (after 2% churn): \(0.98\times 80\times 8.99\times 12 \approx \$8{,}459\)M.

3. Net: \(8{,}459-7{,}670 \approx +\$790\)M/yr from subscriptions alone.

The margin, not the average

Each retained sub pays \$12 more/yr; each churner costs the \$96/yr they would have paid. Net per original sub: \(\;12(1-c)-96c\).

break-even churn: \(\; c^{*}=\dfrac{12}{108}\approx 11\%\)

Netflix's 2% churn is far below it — the hike clearly pays.

The decision isn't "raise prices?" (direction obvious) but how much — marginal revenue from each \$1 vs marginal churn loss. That tradeoff, in the limit, defines the monopolist's optimal price in Chapter 11.

Same marginal logic as W1.3, bigger stakes. Netflix raised the US ad tier $6.99 -> $7.99 (Jan 2025) -> $8.99 (Mar 2026), ~94M global ad-tier users. The right comparison is never average-revenue-vs-average-cost; it is marginal revenue from a $1 rise against marginal loss from churn. Break-even churn c* about 11% is the firm-side mirror of Maya's switch point; Netflix's 2% is far below. The full-width takeaway is the bridge to monopoly pricing (Ch 11). Source: Netflix investor letters Q1 2025 / Q1 2026.

Heads Up 1.3 — Marginal Is Not Average

Exam trap. A firm has total revenue \(\$1{,}000\) and total cost \(\$800\) at \(q=100\). Should it produce the 101st unit?

By the average → "sell more!"

\(AR=\dfrac{TR}{q}=\dfrac{1000}{100}=\$10\)
\(AC=\dfrac{TC}{q}=\dfrac{800}{100}=\$8\)

\(\Rightarrow\) \(+\$2\) profit per unit on average. Tempting: "still earning — expand."

At the margin → "contract."

\(MR(100)=\$4\)
\(MC(100)=\$5\)

\(\Rightarrow\) the 101st unit nets \(4-5=-\$1\). It loses money.

Same firm: the average looks great (\(+\$2\)/unit) yet the next unit loses \(\$1\). The average tells you about history; the margin tells you about the next decision. Whenever you see "average" in a problem, ask whether you actually want the marginal.

The single most exam-relevant trap of the chapter, now with both calculations side by side. Average: AR = 1000/100 = $10, AC = 800/100 = $8, so +$2/unit - looks profitable, tempting to expand. Marginal: MR(100)=$4 < MC(100)=$5, so the 101st unit loses $1 - the firm should contract even though it is profitable overall. The average is about history; the margin is about the next decision. Same confusion underlies the AR-vs-MR peak in W1.3 and firm shutdown in Ch 9.

§1.4

Models

Simplified maps of complex worlds — useful because they leave things out

The Problem of Infinite Complexity

Reality is billions of daily decisions and countless interacting, chaotic variables — no one can measure them all at once.

To make any of it tractable, economists look at the world through models — deliberately simplified maps that keep only what matters for the question at hand.

Deciding What to Leave Out

How do economists decide which details to strip away? They use assumptions — a filter that removes irrelevant noise so the underlying mechanics of a system become visible.

An assumption isn't a claim that the world is simple — it's a choice about what to ignore so the forces you care about stand out. Test one by asking whether relaxing it would change your answer, not whether it's "realistic."

A Model Is a Subway Map, Not a Photograph

The photo is more realistic; the map is more useful for navigating.

A good model picks the actors and their objectives, the environment (fixed / variable / random / strategic), and — hardest — what to ignore. We then do comparative statics: how does the equilibrium shift when a parameter changes?

Recall from 1116 — §2.2

A positive statement is about what is (testable); a normative one is about what ought to be (value-laden). Economics is mostly positive analysis in service of normative questions.

Heads Up 1.4 — models are tools, not portraits

Don't ask "is this assumption true?" (almost none are) — ask "would relaxing it change the result I care about?" Chs 11–18 exist to relax the perfect-competition assumptions, one at a time.

A model is useful because it leaves things out, the way a subway map omits every building between stations. The art is knowing when an assumption is safe and when it is load-bearing — ignoring transport costs is harmless for a tariff's direction, disastrous for who loses when a factory closes. Application thread for the slide: Amazon's millionth warehouse robot is a firm choosing the cost-minimizing capital/labor mix \(q=f(K,L)\) subject to \(wL+rK\le C\) (Chs 7–8) — the model strips out hiring frictions and morale yet still tells you how many robots per dollar of wage increase. Comparative statics is the engine of nearly every end-of-chapter problem.

§1.5

The Empirical Turn

How economists test theories — from the arrow to a measured number

Correlation, Causation, and the Counterfactual

Modern micro is saturated with empirical work — RCTs, natural experiments, administrative data, DiD, regression discontinuity. The "credibility revolution" is ~three decades old.

The core problem is causal inference: does \(X\) cause \(Y\), or do they merely move together? States with more teachers/student also have higher scores — but they also differ in income, demographics, curricula. Any of those could be doing the work.

The counterfactual. The causal effect of \(X\) on a unit is \(Y\) with \(X\) minus \(Y\) without \(X\), holding all else fixed. The fundamental problem: only one of those is ever observed for the same unit. Every empirical design is a strategy to estimate the missing one.

Heads Up 1.5 — "correlation is not causation"

Everyone's heard it; almost everyone slips on the exam. When you see "\(X\) is associated with \(Y\)," ask: what is the counterfactual, and how was it constructed?

The naive approach — "places with \(X\) also have \(Y\)" — conflates cause with confounded correlation. The counterfactual framework sharpens the question to a comparison the same unit can never both experience. The whole apparatus of modern micro is a set of strategies for constructing a credible estimate of the unobservable counterfactual. The two designs on the next slides — RCTs and natural experiments / DiD — are the two workhorses, and each maps directly to a real 2023–24 study.

Why Two Things Move Together

Correlation

Two variables move together — positive, negative, or zero. A pattern in the data.

Causation

One variable directly produces a change in the other. Rule out the alternatives below.

If \(X\) and \(Y\) correlate, there are three suspects:

X → Y

More teachers per student cause higher test scores

Y → X

Reverse: high-scoring districts attract & fund more teachers

Z → X, Y

Omitted variable: district income drives both

Spurious correlations — high \(r\), zero meaning

Margarine consumption ↔ Maine's divorce rate (\(r=0.99\)); websites online ↔ wind-power capacity (\(r=0.98\)). Both just share a time trend. An algorithm flags these as "significant"; a trained economist asks what is the omitted \(Z\)?

Three reasons, three arrows — this is the concrete drill that makes the counterfactual on the previous slide stick. The classic exam version: ice-cream sales and drowning deaths correlate strongly; the answer is neither causal direction but the omitted variable \(Z=\) hot weather, which drives both. The teacher/test-score example is deliberately the same one from the previous slide, now decomposed into all three suspects. The omitted-variable case (\(Z\to X,Y\)) is the sneakiest and the most common reasoning error in pop economics. Spurious correlations (Tyler Vigen's catalog) make the point vivid: high \(r\) is cheap; a credible causal claim needs a design that rules out reverse causation and confounders, which is exactly what the RCT (next slide) and difference-in-differences buy you. Tee up: the whole empirical apparatus exists to find or neutralize \(Z\).

Theory & Data — A Guaranteed-Income RCT

An RCT builds the counterfactual on purpose: randomize who gets \(X\), so treatment and control differ only in \(X\). Vivalt et al. (2024, NBER WP 32719) enrolled 3,000 low-income adults; 1,000 got \$1,000/mo for 3 years, 2,000 got \$50/mo.

Random assignment lets us read the 1.3-hour gap as a causal effect of the cash, not a correlation about who happens to receive it.

Forward pointer — Ch 5

This is the direct empirical counterpart to a theory result we derive in Ch 5: a rise in non-labor income reduces labor supply when leisure is a normal good. Theory predicts the sign; the RCT measures the size.

RCTs were imported from medicine and development and are now standard in labor, development, and public economics. The headline here is the gap between the transfer (\$12,000/yr) and the earnings response (−\$1,500/yr): recipients did not simply stop working. Some shifted into education and caregiving; labor-force participation fell slightly. The design is what licenses the causal reading — absent randomization, "people who got cash worked less" could just mean cash went to people already cutting hours.

Theory & Data — Generative AI at Work (Natural Experiment)

RCTs are often impossible — you can't randomize tax rates across states. Instead, find a natural experiment + difference-in-differences: compare the change in \(Y\) for a treated group to the change for a control, over the same window (assuming parallel trends).

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2023, NBER WP 31161): staggered rollout of an AI assistant to 5,172 support agents. Productivity rose ~15% — and least-experienced agents gained most.

The pre-period flatness validates parallel trends; the post-period jump is the treatment effect. This is what a causal estimate looks like in a natural experiment — the average 15% is the causal effect; the distributional detail (novices gain most) is the interesting discovery.

Access wasn't randomized by the researchers, but the firm rolled it out in waves on a timeline uncorrelated with prior performance — so agents just-treated can be compared to agents scheduled later (their own controls). The event-study figure is the canonical DiD picture: read the flat pre-trend as the parallel-trends check and the discontinuous jump as the effect. Real empirical papers spend most of their length defending the parallel-trends clause; we take it as given for now and build the machinery from Ch 2 onward.

Walkthrough 1.5 — The MTA's Revenue Arithmetic

Problem. Daily entries \(Q_d(P)=300{,}000-5{,}000P\). (a) Revenue at \(P=9\) and \(P=12\)? (b) Revenue-maximizing toll? (c) Why might the MTA pick below it?

(a) \(R(9)=9(255{,}000)=\$2.295\)M; \(R(12)=12(240{,}000)=\$2.88\)M per weekday.

(b) \(R(P)=300{,}000P-5{,}000P^{2}\); \(R'(P)=300{,}000-10{,}000P=0 \Rightarrow P^{*}=\$30\). SOC \(<0\) ✓. \(R(30)=\$4.5\)M/day.

(c) At \$30, entries halve to 150,000 — crowding, disruption, equity, and political backlash. The MTA solves a constrained problem, not pure revenue.

Graph → arrow. Calculus → number.

A diagram says revenue first rises then falls in the toll. The FOC pins the peak exactly at \(P^{*}=\$30\). We redo this in Ch 3 with elasticity — revenue is maximized where \(\varepsilon_D=-1\).

Real policymakers rarely maximize one thing. The gap between "the math says \$30" and "they chose \$9" is the space between a simple model's prediction and the multi-objective problem policymakers actually run.

Four reveals — the chapter's thesis made literal. Units check: dollars/vehicle × vehicles/day = dollars/day. Monotonicity: revenue rises in the toll up to \$30, then falls. The revenue-max toll (\$30) is far above the chosen toll (\$9) precisely because the MTA's objective is not revenue — it must hit a ~\$550M/yr bonding target and a traffic-reduction floor without collapsing the political coalition. Poll #2 — The Math Says \$30. They Chose \$9.

Walkthrough 1.5 found the revenue-maximizing toll is \$30, yet the MTA set \$9. What's the best explanation?

A) The MTA made an arithmetic mistake
B) \$9 optimizes a different, constrained objective (revenue target + traffic + politics)
C) Demand is perfectly inelastic at \$9
D) A \$30 toll would violate the law of demand

45 sec to vote

Press 1 for a timer; down arrow reveals. Answer is B. The \$30 toll maximizes revenue alone; the MTA's actual objective is multi-objective and constrained — raise ~\$550M/yr to service \$15B of bonds, cut traffic enough for ambulances and speeds, but not so much that the commuter-belt coalition revolts. \$9 is the optimum of that problem. Answer: B — A Different, Constrained Objective

\$30 is the answer to "maximize revenue." The MTA solves a harder problem: hit a bonding target and a traffic floor without a political revolt. \$9 is that problem's optimum — the gap between the two is the whole lesson of the chapter.

The takeaway: a simple model gives a sharp number for a simple objective. Real policy adds constraints — and the constrained optimum is a different number. Both are "optimization."

One Word Runs Through All Four Parts: Efficiency

Economists mean two distinct things by it.

Productive efficiency

A given output made at the lowest resource cost — no inputs wasted.

Firm-side half · Chs 7–10

Allocative efficiency

The right goods for the right people — the last unit is worth at least its social cost.

Ch 10 welfare theorem ✓ · Chs 11 & 17 break it

Every deadweight loss in this book is a measurement of allocative inefficiency.

The Efficiency–Equity Tradeoff

Bigger pie

A lighter tax, a freer market — often enlarges total surplus but slices it less equally.

Fairer slices

A minimum wage, a progressive tax — shares the pie more evenly but can shrink it.

Positive analysis sizes the tradeoff at each point. It cannot pick a point — that depends on values.
