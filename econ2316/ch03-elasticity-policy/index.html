What Was Elasticity? Quantity Reaction vs Price Reaction

The law of demand says quantity reacts to price. Elasticity is just how big that reaction is — the quantity reaction set against the price reaction.

\[ \text{elasticity} = \frac{\text{quantity reaction}}{\text{price reaction}} = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta p} \]

Key Insight

A sensitivity test: does quantity react less than price, or more?

Necessity / Inelastic

Price +96% → quantity only −2%
quantity reacts less than price

Luxury / Elastic

Price +25% → quantity −30%
quantity reacts more than price

Rusty on ratios or percentage change? Math Refresher →

The one-slide review of Principles elasticity: it is nothing more than the quantity reaction compared to the price reaction. Quantity moves less than price (eggs: −2% vs +96%) ⇒ inelastic; quantity moves more (dining: −30% vs +25%) ⇒ elastic. Keep it conceptual here — §3.1 sharpens this ratio into a point derivative and keeps the sign. The "sensitivity test" framing carries through the whole chapter.

Two elasticities, two signs

Same ratio as the last slide — but there are two of them, and their signs are not the same. Both are unit-free, which is the whole reason the measure exists: you can compare eggs and petrol without dozens and litres getting in the way.

Price elasticity of demand: PED: \(\%\Delta Q^D\) for 1% \(\Delta P\)

Percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percent change in price.

\(\displaystyle E^D = \frac{\%\Delta Q^D}{\%\Delta P}\)

−

Price elasticity of supply: PES: \(\%\Delta Q^S\) for 1% \(\Delta P\)

Percentage change in quantity supplied divided by percent change in price.

\(\displaystyle E^S = \frac{\%\Delta Q^S}{\%\Delta P}\)

+

Principles reported \(|\text{PED}|\) as a positive number and talked about magnitude only. From §3.1 on we keep the sign — it is doing real work, and dropping it is the single most common error on this chapter. That is Heads Up 3.1.

Anchor: PED is negative (law of demand), PES is positive. Principles of Micro reported |PED| as positive; Micro Theory keeps the sign — that's the HeadsUp 3.1 to come. Unit-free is the whole reason it exists - you can compare eggs and petrol without dozens and litres getting in the way. PED and PES are the same construction with a different quantity on top.

The elasticity spectrum

Same \(\Delta p\) in the middle two panels — only the quantity response differs. The outer two are the limiting cases.

(a) Perfectly elastic

\(E=\infty\) — horizontal

Quantity is infinitely responsive. Commodity crops, to any one farmer.

(b) Elastic

\(|\varepsilon|>1\) — shallow

A small price rise brings a large drop in quantity.

(c) Inelastic

\(|\varepsilon|<1\) — steep

A large price rise brings only a small drop in quantity.

(d) Perfectly inelastic

\(E=0\) — vertical

Quantity does not move at all. Life-saving drugs.

Reading left to right, quantity gets less willing to move. Both ends matter in §3.5: with perfectly inelastic supply the seller eats the whole tax; with perfectly elastic supply the buyer does.

Merged from two review slides that split this spectrum in half - the elastic/inelastic pair and the two limiting cases were separated by five slides, so students never saw it as one continuum. Read it left to right: quantity gets progressively less willing to move. The middle two share the same delta-p on the left axis and differ only in delta-q; that is what "more elastic" means visually. Say explicitly that steepness on a diagram is NOT elasticity, because the axis scale is arbitrary - that trap is Heads Up 3.2 in section 3.2. The two ends are the benchmarks for tax incidence later: perfectly inelastic supply means sellers bear all of it, perfectly elastic supply means buyers do, and perfectly elastic supply is also the 100 percent pass-through case in section 3.7.

Elasticities — Terminology

\(\displaystyle E^D = \frac{\%\Delta Q^D}{\%\Delta P}\)

  • Inelastic: \(0 < |E^D| < 1\)
  • Unit elastic: \(|E^D| = 1\)
  • Elastic: \(|E^D| > 1\)
  • Perfectly elastic: \(|E^D| = \infty\)
  • Perfectly inelastic: \(|E^D| = 0\)

Important: Elasticities do not have units attached.

  • Allows comparison across goods and markets.
  • Same classifications apply to supply (\(E^S\)).

Time is what makes demand elastic

Gas goes from \$3.00 to \$4.00 — a \(+33\%\) price rise. Same shock, same driver, two horizons.

Short run — this week

You already own a gas car. Skip a trip, and that is about it.

\(\varepsilon_D \approx -0.05\) ⇒ quantity falls 1.7%

Demand is nearly vertical: the \((p,Q)\) pair barely moves.

Long run — ten years

Now the car itself is a choice, and so is the commute.

\(\varepsilon_D \approx -0.30\) ⇒ quantity falls 10%

What ten years lets you change: buy a hybrid or an EV · move closer to work · switch to transit · take the job that lets you work from home. None of that is available on Tuesday. Gasoline elasticities from Hughes, Knittel & Sperling (2008).

The cleanest intuition in the chapter, and worth slowing down for. The good has not changed, the price change has not changed, the person has not changed. The only thing that changed is how long they have had to react. On Tuesday you own the car you own - skip a trip or two and that is about it, so quantity barely moves and demand is nearly vertical. Give the same household ten years and the whole choice set opens up: they replace the car, maybe with a hybrid or an EV, they move, they change jobs, the employer goes remote. Same price rise, six times the quantity response. Numbers to voice: a 33 percent price rise cuts quantity about 1.7 percent in the short run and about 10 percent in the long run. Tie it forward twice - this is why a fuel or cigarette tax raises steady revenue now but deters much more later, which is section 3.5, and it returns in Chapter 8 as the short-run versus long-run cost distinction, where the firm side has the same structure.

§3.1  From Midpoint to Point Elasticity

Promoting elasticity from a percent-change to a derivative

Arc elasticity — the midpoint method, renamed

You already know this one. Principles called it the midpoint method; its formal name is arc elasticity.

\[\varepsilon^{\text{arc}}_D=\frac{(Q_2-Q_1)\big/\overline{Q}}{(p_2-p_1)\big/\overline{p}}\]

The bars are the midpoints:
\(\overline{Q}=\dfrac{Q_1+Q_2}{2}\),   \(\overline{p}=\dfrac{p_1+p_2}{2}\).

Dividing by the midpoint rather than the starting value is what makes the answer the same going up as coming down — the reason Principles teaches it this way.

No calibrated curve, just two scanner observations? Then there is nothing to differentiate — average the endpoints instead.

Have a function \(Q^d(p)\)? → point elasticity.

Have two data points? → arc (= midpoint).

Lead with the renaming, because students do not recognise "arc" as something they already own - they spent a whole Principles chapter on the midpoint method and never heard the word. Same formula, new name. Arc is a finite-difference approximation to the point elasticity evaluated somewhere between the two observations, which is why the midpoint denominators appear and why it gives the same answer in both directions where the naive percentage formula does not. That direction-independence is the reason Principles teaches it. In this chapter the walkthroughs use the point formula because we always hand students a calibrated demand curve; the end-of-chapter problems on real scanner data are where arc earns its keep, and Walkthrough 3.1 four slides on puts the two side by side.

Why bother promoting elasticity to a derivative?

Midpoint / arc (Principles)

An average over a price segment:

"How elastic between \(p_1\) and \(p_2\)?"

Point (Micro Theory)

A derivative at one price:

"How elastic right here, right now?"

The tax-incidence formula in §3.5 needs a point elasticity at the equilibrium — the midpoint can't deliver that. Same intuition, sharper math.

The point-elasticity formula

\[\varepsilon_D = \lim_{\Delta p \to 0} \frac{\Delta Q/Q}{\Delta p/p} = \frac{dQ}{dp} \cdot \frac{p}{Q}.\]

Two pieces

\(dQ/dp\) is the quantity response — units per \$1. We draw \(p\) up the vertical axis, so this is the reciprocal of the slope you see, not the slope itself. \(p/Q\) rescales it into percentage units.

Sign matters

Law of demand: \(dQ/dp < 0\), so \(\varepsilon_D < 0\). Micro Theory keeps the sign. No absolute-value hiding.

Same vocabulary: \(|\varepsilon|>1\) elastic · \(|\varepsilon|<1\) inelastic · \(|\varepsilon|=1\) unit elastic · \(0\) perfectly inelastic · \(\infty\) perfectly elastic.

A first case: locked egg market

From Ch 2, demand \(Q^d = 200 - 5p\), supply \(Q^s = 50 + 10p\), pre-shock \(p^* = 10\), \(Q^* = 150\).

\(\varepsilon_D = (-5) \cdot (10/150) = -\tfrac{1}{3}\)

\(\varepsilon_S = (10) \cdot (10/150) = +\tfrac{2}{3}\)

Same point, so the same \(p/Q=10/150\) for both — they differ only in \(dQ/dp\), \(-5\) against \(+10\). The ratio \(|\varepsilon_S|/|\varepsilon_D| = 2\) does most of the work in §3.5. Inelastic demand + moderately inelastic supply is the staple-food story.

Heads Up 3.1 — Our \(\varepsilon_D\) is negative

Principles often quoted \(|\varepsilon_D|\) as positive numbers. Micro Theory keeps the sign. Mix conventions and the incidence formula in §3.5 will produce negative pass-through shares — a guaranteed clue you've sign-flipped.

Translating from Principles of Micro

Principles of Micro's “elasticity 0.5” is our \(\varepsilon_D = -0.5\).
Principles of Micro's “elasticity 1.2” is our \(\varepsilon_D = -1.2\).

Sanity rule

Pass-through and burden shares should land in \([0,1]\). If you see a negative or \(>1\) result, check the sign on \(\varepsilon_D\) first.

Walkthrough 3.1 — NYC congestion: arc vs point

Problem. \(Q^d(P)=300{,}000-5{,}000P\). Compare the two elasticity measures between \(\$9\) and \(\$12\).

Arc — across \(\$9 \to \$12\)

1. Both endpoints

\(Q_1=300{,}000-5{,}000(9)=255{,}000\)
\(Q_2=300{,}000-5{,}000(12)=240{,}000\)

2. Changes, and their midpoints

\(\Delta Q=-15{,}000\), \(\overline{Q}=247{,}500\)
\(\Delta p=+3\), \(\overline{p}=\$10.50\)

3. Divide

\(\varepsilon^{\text{arc}}=\dfrac{-15{,}000/247{,}500}{3/10.50}=\dfrac{-0.0606}{0.2857}=\mathbf{-0.212}\)

Point — at \(\$12\)

1. Differentiate

\(\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}=\mathbf{-5{,}000}\) — constant, because demand is linear.

2. Quantity at that price

\(Q^d(12)=300{,}000-5{,}000(12)=\mathbf{240{,}000}\)

3. Rescale by \(p/Q\)

\(\varepsilon_D=-5{,}000\cdot\dfrac{12}{240{,}000}=\mathbf{-0.250}\)

Same slope both times. Off the two dots, \(\Delta Q/\Delta p=-15{,}000/3=-5{,}000\) — exactly the derivative. The only difference is where you rescale: arc divides by the midpoint \(\overline{p}/\overline{Q}\), point divides by \(p/Q\) at \$12.

Check: \(-5{,}000\cdot\dfrac{10.50}{247{,}500}=-0.212\) — the arc answer, to the digit. Both sit well inside \(|\varepsilon|<1\): commuters cannot easily stop driving, which is why the toll raises steady revenue.

Both measures worked in three steps so students can see they are the same construction. Arc: get both endpoint quantities, form the changes and the midpoints, divide. Point: differentiate once, evaluate Q at the price you care about, rescale by p over Q. Watch the pairing - the point elasticity at \$12 uses Q2 = 240,000, the quantity AT \$12; pairing \$12 with Q1 = 255,000 mixes the two endpoints and is a common slip. The payoff is the closing check: because demand is linear the slope is -5,000 whichever way you get it, so arc and point differ ONLY in the price-quantity pair used to rescale, and rescaling the derivative at the midpoint reproduces the arc answer exactly. They converge as the interval shrinks. Both land near -0.2, deep in inelastic territory, which explains why the toll raises steady revenue and why traffic fell only modestly.

Calculus Corner 3.1 — Elasticity = slope of \(\ln Q\) vs \(\ln p\)

\(\varepsilon_D = \dfrac{d\ln Q}{d\ln p}\)

Chain-rule check

\(\dfrac{d\ln Q}{d\ln p} = \dfrac{1}{Q}\cdot\dfrac{dQ}{dp}\cdot p = \dfrac{dQ}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{p}{Q}\). ✓

Why empiricists love this

Run a regression of \(\ln Q\) on \(\ln p\) — the coefficient is the elasticity. Gas demand: \(\hat\beta \approx -0.2\) (Hughes-Knittel-Sperling 2008).

§3.2–3.3  Linear Demand Zones & Revenue

14 min · Where on the curve are you?

Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve

Nothing new needed — just the point-elasticity formula, read carefully.

\(\varepsilon_D=\underbrace{\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}}_{\text{fixed}}\cdot\underbrace{\dfrac{p}{Q^d}}_{\text{moves}}\)

For \(Q^d=a-bp\), \(dQ^d/dp=-b\). That is a constant — it is the same at every price on the curve.

So the only thing that changes as you walk along the line is the \((p,Q)\) pair:

\(\varepsilon_D=\dfrac{-bp}{a-bp}\)

\(p \to 0\): \(p/Q\to0\), so \(\varepsilon_D \to 0\) — perfectly inelastic at the \(Q\)-intercept

\(p \to a/b\): \(Q\to0\), so \(\varepsilon_D \to -\infty\) — perfectly elastic at the \(p\)-intercept

\(p = a/(2b)\): \(\varepsilon_D = -1\) (unit elastic, midpoint) — the revenue-max price, derived in §3.3

Figure 3.2 — Walk down the curve: \(dQ/dp\) never moves, \(\varepsilon_D\) does

Drag the price: the curve never bends, yet \(\varepsilon_D\) runs from \(0\) to \(-\infty\).

Do not introduce anything new here - this whole slide is the point-elasticity formula read carefully. Epsilon is a product of two factors; on a straight line the first one is nailed down at -b, so every bit of the variation has to come from p over Q. Walk the slider from the bottom of the curve to the top and narrate it: at the quantity intercept p is nearly zero so p/Q is nearly zero and demand is perfectly inelastic; at the price intercept Q is nearly zero so p/Q blows up and demand is perfectly elastic; exactly halfway the two effects balance and epsilon is -1. That midpoint is the revenue-maximising price, which is section 3.3 two slides on. Heads Up 3.2 next is the same lesson from the other side - there the price is held fixed and the curve changes.

Heads Up 3.2 — \(dQ/dp\) is not elasticity

Same \(dQ/dp\), two demand curves, very different elasticities at the same price.

\(Q = 200 - 5p\) at \(p = 5\):
\(dQ/dp=-5\), \(\varepsilon_D = (-5)(5/175) \approx -0.143\)

\(Q = 40 - 5p\) at \(p = 5\):
same \(dQ/dp=-5\), \(\varepsilon_D = (-5)(5/15) \approx -1.667\)

Same \(dQ/dp\). Elasticities differ by a factor of 12. The \(p/Q\) ratio is doing all the work.

The previous slide held the curve fixed and moved along it. This one holds the price fixed and swaps the curve. Either way, \(dQ/dp\) is not what decides the elasticity — \(p/Q\) is.

The mirror of the previous slide, and worth saying so out loud. There: one curve, dQ/dp pinned at -b, p/Q moving as you walk down, so epsilon varies. Here: two curves, dQ/dp pinned at -5 in both, but p/Q differs because the quantities at p = 5 are 175 and 15 - so the elasticities differ by a factor of twelve. Both slides make the same point, that dQ/dp alone tells you nothing about elasticity. The second curve is deliberately the near-choke case: at p = 5 there are only 15 units left out of a 40 intercept, so p/Q is enormous.

Answer: B — \(p = 25\)

No shortcut needed — the definition does it in three steps.

1. Read \(dQ/dp\) straight off the demand function:  \(\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp} = -2\)

2. Put \(p=25\) into the curve:  \(Q^d = 100 - 2(25) = 50\)

3. Apply the definition:  \(\varepsilon_D = \dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{p}{Q^d} = -2\cdot\dfrac{25}{50} = \mathbf{-1}\) ✓

\(dQ/dp\) was \(-2\) the whole way along. What made \(\varepsilon_D=-1\) here is the \(p/Q\) factor — which is the entire content of Heads Up 3.2.

Trap (D). “Need more info” is wrong: the curve alone fixes both \(dQ/dp\) and \(p/Q\) at every price.

Deliberately NOT the midpoint shortcut p = a/(2b). That formula gets the right answer and teaches nothing - students memorise it, then cannot do the next problem where demand is not linear. These three steps are the same three they will use for every elasticity in the course: read dQ/dp, find the quantity at that price, multiply by p over Q. Land the closing point hard - dQ/dp never changed, so dQ/dp cannot be what made this unit elastic; only p/Q moved. If someone volunteers the a/(2b) shortcut, confirm it works for linear demand and then ask what they would do without it.

Revenue and the unit-elasticity rule

Revenue is price times quantity — a product, so differentiate it as one.

1. Product rule on \(R(p)=p\cdot Q^d(p)\):
\(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p) + p\,\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\)

2. Factor out \(Q^d\):
\(= Q^d(p)\left[\,1 + \dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\dfrac{p}{Q^d}\,\right]\)

3. That bracket is \(\varepsilon_D\):

\(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p)\,[\,1+\varepsilon_D\,]\)

\(Q^d>0\) always — so the bracket alone decides the sign.

So where you sit decides what to do

Inelastic

\(\varepsilon_D>-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp>0\) — raise the price

Unit elastic

\(\varepsilon_D=-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp=0\) — revenue peaks

Elastic

\(\varepsilon_D<-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp<0\) — lower the price

Laffer logic for an excise tax: revenue climbs with the rate only until the market turns elastic — past that, a higher rate collects less.

Merged from two slides that ran in the wrong order - the old pair showed the result first and then said "press down for the derivation". Derive, then read the regimes off it. Eight steps, but they are two runs of four: the derivation (1-4), then the three zones with their heading (5-7) and the tax note (8). Move briskly through the derivation. The payoff is step 4: Q is always positive, so the bracket alone carries the sign, which is why the whole rule reduces to where epsilon sits relative to -1. Tie back to Poll #1 two slides earlier - that was the unit-elastic point on this same curve, and this is why it is also the revenue-maximising price. Walkthrough 3.2 — The revenue-max NYC toll

Problem. \(Q^d(P)=300{,}000-5{,}000P\). Find the revenue-maximising toll.

Set up — revenue is one variable

\(R(P)=P\,Q^d(P)=300{,}000P-5{,}000P^{2}\)

FOC — first-order condition

\(R'(P)=300{,}000-10{,}000P=0\)
\(\Rightarrow \mathbf{P^{*}=\$30}\)

SOC — is it a max?

\(R''=-10{,}000<0\) ✓ a maximum, not a minimum.

Check

\(\varepsilon_D = -5{,}000\cdot\dfrac{30}{150{,}000}=\mathbf{-1}\) ✓

The peak sits exactly where \(\varepsilon_D=-1\). The real \$9 toll is deep in the inelastic zone.

§3.4  Income & Cross-Price Elasticities

8 min · Two more elasticities, same template

Income elasticity

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} = \dfrac{\partial Q^d_i}{\partial I} \cdot \dfrac{I}{Q^d_i}\)  —  the sign classifies the good, the size ranks it

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} > 0\) — Normal

• Necessity: \(0 < \varepsilon < 1\) (food)
• Luxury: \(\varepsilon > 1\) (restaurant meals)

Restaurant meals, \(\varepsilon_I \approx 1.0\) — right on the luxury edge.

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} < 0\) — Inferior

Quantity falls as income rises — buyers substitute toward something better.

MBTA Orange Line — as income rises, riders switch to cars.

U.S. food: \(\varepsilon_I \approx 0.1\)–\(0.3\). Bus travel can flip from inferior to normal at different income levels.

Same partial-derivative template as own-price elasticity, just with respect to income. The split to hammer: the SIGN says normal or inferior, the SIZE says necessity or luxury - two different questions off one number. Photos are the Principles anchors: fine dining for the luxury edge, and the Orange Line as the local Boston example students actually ride. Bus travel is the honest complication - it is inferior over some income ranges and normal over others, so the label belongs to a range, not to the good forever.

Income elasticity — two goods, opposite signs

\(\varepsilon_I=\dfrac{\%\Delta Q^d}{\%\Delta I}\). Income rises \(\$30\text{k}\to\$50\text{k}\) — a \(+66.7\%\) change.

Concert tickets

\(4\to 8\) a year \((+100\%)\)
\(\varepsilon_I=\mathbf{+1.5}\) → luxury

Instant noodles

\(10\to 6\) a week \((-40\%)\)
\(\varepsilon_I=\mathbf{-0.6}\) → inferior

Sign classifies, size ranks. Negative means inferior; above \(+1\) means luxury; between \(0\) and \(+1\) means necessity.

Same template as price elasticity, different denominator - say that rather than showing it. The classification is the examinable part: sign splits normal from inferior, magnitude splits necessity from luxury. Worth one sentence of realism: goods move between categories with income level and country, so "inferior" is a statement about a population, not about a product.

Cross-price elasticity

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} = \dfrac{\partial Q^d_i}{\partial p_j} \cdot \dfrac{p_j}{Q^d_i}\)  —  does \(i\) sell more or less when \(j\) gets pricier?

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} > 0\) — Substitutes

\(j\) gets pricier, buyers move to \(i\). Tea/coffee, iPhone/Galaxy, ride-hail/rental.

Raise the price of one console, buyers shift to the other.

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} < 0\) — Complements

\(j\) gets pricier, demand for \(i\) falls too. Printer/ink, console/games.

Console and cartridge — price up on one, demand down on both.

\(\varepsilon_{i,j}=0\) — unrelated goods, no cross-relationship at all.  •  Antitrust use: a large positive \(\varepsilon_{i,j}\) puts two products in the same relevant market for merger analysis.

The sign rule is the whole lesson: positive means substitutes, negative means complements, zero means unrelated. The console pair is worth dwelling on because both photos are the same console - what changes is what it is sitting next to, which is the point: the relationship lives between the two goods, not inside either one. Antitrust is the live application - a large positive cross-price elasticity is how regulators argue two products compete in the same market, and it comes back in Ch 12.

Cross-price elasticity — and how antitrust uses it

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} = \dfrac{\%\Delta Q^d_i}{\%\Delta p_j}\) — does good \(i\) sell more, or less, when \(j\) gets pricier?

Substitutes — Netflix & Disney+

\(p_{D+}\uparrow 10\%\) → \(Q_N: 250\to 262.5\)M
\(\varepsilon = \mathbf{+0.5}\) — positive, so substitutes.

Complements — PS5 & its games

\(p_{PS5}\uparrow 15\%\) → \(Q_G: 200\to 176\)M
\(\varepsilon = \mathbf{-0.8}\) — negative, so complements.

This number decides merger cases. A high \(\varepsilon\) between two products means they compete — so they sit in the same market, and the merger gets scrutiny.

The sign is the whole test: positive means substitutes, negative means complements. Antitrust is where this stops being a classroom exercise - the DOJ and FTC use cross-price elasticity to define the "relevant market" for merger review, because market share is meaningless until you have decided what the market IS. Two cases worth naming aloud: FTC v Microsoft-Activision (2023), where regulators measured elasticity between Xbox, PlayStation and cloud gaming to decide whether consoles form one market; and FTC v Whole Foods-Wild Oats (2007), where a high elasticity between "premium organic supermarkets" and ordinary grocers was the contested question. Returns in Ch 11-12.

§3.5  Tax Incidence by Total Differentiation

14 min · The wedge equation

People respond to incentives — elasticity says how much

One price rise fires two incentives at once.

Buyers — a penalty

Quantity demanded falls. How much? \(|\varepsilon_D|\).

Sellers — a reward

Quantity supplied rises. How much? \(\varepsilon_S\).

Elasticity is the strength of the response — which is why §3.5 can predict who bears a tax from those two numbers alone.

Definition to say rather than show: an incentive is anything that motivates an action - a reward or a penalty - and in a market the price is the incentive. The move that matters is realising the same price change is a penalty on one side and a reward on the other, so a single tax has two behavioural responses to track. That is exactly why the incidence formula needs both elasticities and not just one.

A tax is an incentive — and elasticity is the response

A per-unit tax \(t\) raises what buyers pay to \(p_c\) and cuts what sellers keep to \(p_s\). Who moves?

Inelastic — weak response

Eggs \(-0.23\), cigarettes \(-0.4\), insulin \(\approx 0\).
Buyers cannot walk away → \(p_c\) rises by nearly all of \(t\).
Fat revenue, thin DWL.

Elastic — strong response

Luxury yachts \(-2\), one cereal brand, soda with substitutes.
Buyers leave → the tax lands on sellers.
Thin revenue, fat DWL.

Two halves of one story — and §3.5 turns it into a formula.

The sentence to land is that a tax is just a price change, so everything students already know about elasticity applies unchanged. Voice the trade-off explicitly: a government that wants REVENUE taxes inelastic things (cigarettes, fuel, salt historically); a government that wants BEHAVIOUR CHANGE taxes elastic things - but then collects little, which is the point. §3.5 formalises this as dp_c/dt = eS/(eS - eD), and the two columns here are just that formula's two extremes.

The tax wedge

The wedge

\(p_c\) = price buyer pays
\(p_s\) = price seller receives
\(p_c - p_s = t\)

Statutory incidence (whose paycheck the deduction shows up on) is economically irrelevant.

Equilibrium with tax

Statute on sellers:
\(Q^d(p_c) = Q^s(p_c - t)\)

One equation in one unknown \(p_c\) for each \(t\). At \(t = 0\): \(p_c = p_s = p^*\).

The comparative-statics question: how do \(p_c\) and \(p_s\) move as \(t\) rises? Total differentiate.

Calculus Corner 3.2 — Deriving the incidence formula

Differentiate \(Q^d(p_c) = Q^s(p_c - t)\) w.r.t. \(t\). Both sides need the chain rule — \(p_c\) is itself a function of \(t\):

\[\frac{dQ^d}{dp_c}\cdot\frac{dp_c}{dt} = \frac{dQ^s}{dp_s}\left(\frac{dp_c}{dt} - 1\right)\]

Solve for \(dp_c/dt\), then multiply num/denom by \(p^*/Q^*\) to turn the derivatives into elasticities:

\[\boxed{\dfrac{dp_c}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}, \qquad \dfrac{dp_s}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_D}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}}\]

\(\varepsilon_S > 0\) ⇒ \(dp_c/dt > 0\) (consumer price rises)

\(\varepsilon_D < 0\) ⇒ \(dp_s/dt < 0\) (seller price falls)

For finite nonzero elasticities: \(0 < dp_c/dt < 1\). Pass-through is strictly between 0% and 100% in the competitive linear model.

First chain rule of the course. Math Refresher →

Reading the formula

\(\dfrac{dp_c}{dt}=\dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S-\varepsilon_D}\) — a ratio of one side's responsiveness to the total.

Demand inelastic

\(\varepsilon_D\to 0 \Rightarrow dp_c/dt\to 1\)
Buyers pay it all. Cigarettes, eggs, insulin.

Supply inelastic

\(\varepsilon_S\to 0 \Rightarrow dp_c/dt\to 0\)
Sellers eat it. Land, port slots, sports stars.

The side that cannot walk away bears the tax.

The denominator is total responsiveness across both sides; the numerator is the seller's share of it. So the formula is just "how much of the combined flexibility sits on the other side". Land is the cleanest inelastic-supply case - you cannot make more of it, so a tax on land value falls entirely on landowners, which is the whole Georgist argument. Sanity rule worth repeating from Heads Up 3.1: shares must land in [0,1]; anything outside means a sign slip.

Walkthrough 3.3 — Eggs: \(2/3\) price, \(1/3\) quantity

Before any tax: the elasticity-share rule is about who absorbs a shock — any shift in supply or demand, not just a tax. Here the shock is bird flu.

Problem. \(Q^d = 200-5p\), \(Q^s = 50+10p\), \(p^*=10\), \(Q^*=150\). HPAI cull shifts supply in by \(\Delta\alpha=-30\).

(a) Elasticities at \(p^*=10\)

\(\varepsilon_D = (-5)\dfrac{10}{150} = \mathbf{-\tfrac{1}{3}}\) · \(\varepsilon_S = (10)\dfrac{10}{150} = \mathbf{+\tfrac{2}{3}}\)
\(|\varepsilon_S|/|\varepsilon_D| = 2\)

(b) The split

Price share \(=\dfrac{2/3}{2/3+1/3} = \mathbf{\tfrac{2}{3}}\)
Quantity share \(= \mathbf{\tfrac{1}{3}}\)

(c) Ch 2 agrees

\(\Delta p = +2\), \(\Delta Q = -10\) — the same \(2\!:\!1\) split.

The more elastic side escapes — shock or tax, the rule does not care which. Supply is twice as elastic here, so demand absorbs twice the share.

First numerical workout of the incidence idea, and deliberately on a NON-tax shock - the HPAI cull from Chs 1 and 2 - so students see the formula is about elasticities, not about taxes. Say the lead-in rather than showing it. Part (c) is the payoff: Ch 2 solved this by re-solving the whole system and got dp = +2, dQ = -10; the elasticity route gets the same 2:1 split in one line. Same answer two ways - the §3.5 elasticity formula and Ch 2's total differentiation are the same mathematics wearing different clothes. Sanity check to voice: supply here is twice as elastic as demand, so demand should eat twice the burden, and it does.

Walkthrough 3.4 — The Logan rideshare fee

Problem. On 1 July 2025 Massport raised the per-trip rideshare fee at Logan from \(\$3.25\) to \(\$5.50\): a unit tax of \(t=\$2.25\) a ride, billed to the trip. For airport rideshare take \(\varepsilon_D=-0.6\), \(\varepsilon_S=+2.0\).  (a) Rider’s share and \(\Delta p_c\).  (b) Driver’s share and \(\Delta p_s\).  (c) Swap the elasticities — what changes?

(a) & (b) The split

\(\dfrac{dp_c}{dt}=\dfrac{2.0}{2.0+0.6}=\mathbf{0.769}\)
\(\Delta p_c = \mathbf{+\$1.73}\), \(\Delta p_s = \mathbf{-\$0.52}\)

(c) Now swap them

\(\varepsilon_D=-2.0\), \(\varepsilon_S=+0.6\) → \(\dfrac{0.6}{2.6}=\mathbf{0.231}\)
Rider \(\mathbf{+\$0.52}\), driver \(\mathbf{-\$1.73}\). The shares flip.

Whoever can walk away, walks away. Nothing changed about who hands over the money — only the two elasticities.

The given \(\varepsilon\)’s are the estimated ones, so (a) is the real case: riders are the inelastic side and pay about \(\$1.73\) of every \(\$2.25\).

\(\varepsilon_D=-0.57\) for UberX: Cohen et al. (2016, NBER w22627). \(\varepsilon_S\): driver supply highly elastic — Hall, Horton & Knoepfle.

Local, current, and a clean unit tax students have actually paid. Read the setup out: the fee went three twenty-five to five fifty on 1 July 2025, so t is two twenty-five a ride, and the two elasticities are GIVEN the way they are in every incidence question - the work is applying the formula, not guessing the parameters. Each bar now appears WITH its own case, so nobody sees the answer to (c) while you are still doing (a) and (b). Stress that the given numbers are anchored, not invented: Cohen and co-authors used Uber surge pricing as a regression discontinuity across four US markets and got about minus zero point five seven for UberX, and Hall, Horton and Knoepfle find driver supply highly elastic because there is no hours restriction and essentially free entry. Part (c) is the whole walkthrough - swap the two numbers and the burden reverses with no change to who hands over the money. Anyone still thinking "the driver pays, it is billed to the trip" cannot hold that afterwards. The airport nuance worth voicing: parking, the Silver Line and a friend at the curb are real substitutes, so you might expect demand to be elastic here - the estimates say otherwise, and that gap between intuition and evidence is the useful moment. Hands to section 3.6, where Massport wanting seven fifty instead of five fifty is exactly the t-squared question.

Heads Up 3.3 — Statutory incidence is irrelevant

Whether the statute puts the tax on the buyer or the seller, the same \(dp_c/dt = \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)\) emerges from total differentiation. Mathematical identity, not coincidence.

What is conventional

The sign on \(\varepsilon_D\). If you let the absolute value slip and write \(\varepsilon_S + |\varepsilon_D|\) in the denominator, the formula still gives the right answer. Mix the two conventions and you'll compute negative shares.

The Cobra Effect

Colonial Delhi: the government offers a bounty for every dead cobra.

1. It works — people hunt cobras.

2. People start breeding cobras — the margin nobody was measuring.

3. Bounty ends — breeders release them.

More snakes than before the policy. People respond to what you measure, not to what you meant.

Review from Principles Ch 1, so keep it fast - three beats and move. The panels run left to right in the same order as the three boxes. The ending is the part students forget: the snake population ended up HIGHER than before the policy. The transferable lesson is that incentives are powerful, so designing one means thinking about every margin the target can move on, not just the one you intended - and beat two is the one to name out loud, because breeding cobras is the unmeasured margin, the thing the bounty paid for without meaning to. Note where this block now sits - at the END of 3.5, after the formula, so it reads as the caution rather than the warm-up: we have just spent the section computing exactly who bears a tax in the visible market, and this is the reminder that the visible market is not the only margin people have. The next slide asks the obvious follow-up - does this happen to the taxes we just computed?

The Cobra Effect today — one tax leaked, one did not

Breeding cobras was a margin nobody measured. Ask the same question of two real taxes: what can people move to that you are not counting?

Pay-As-You-Throw — the margin was open

Charge per bag of trash. Charlottesville 1992: official trash −37%, real waste only −14%.

The gap went to fly-tipping — roadsides, neighbours’ bins — and the “Seattle Stomp.” You cannot bill what you cannot see.

Logan’s \(\$5.50\) — the margin was closed

The obvious dodge is to meet your driver off airport property. Massport shut it: ride-app pickups are allowed only in the Central Garage and Terminal B Garage.

And the substitute they want is priced below the tax, not outside it — shared rides at \(\$1.50\).

Delhi’s mistake, in one line: a tax leaks when the untaxed margin is cheaper and unwatched. Close it — or price the good substitute below the tax — and the incidence formula in §3.5 describes the whole market, not just the visible half.

Fullerton & Kinnaman (1996, AER) on PAYT · Massport ride-app policy, 2025.

This answers the question a student always asks after the Cobra slide - fine, but does that happen to the taxes we just computed? Two answers, and the contrast is the lesson. PAYT: Charlottesville went per-bag in 1992; Fullerton and Kinnaman found official trash down 37 percent but real waste down only about 14, the gap being illegal dumping in roadsides, business dumpsters and neighbours' bins, plus Seattle's residents compacting cans - the Seattle Stomp. The margin was cheap and nobody was watching it. Logan is the designed contrast. The obvious avoidance is to walk off airport property and get picked up on a side street, and Massport closed it structurally: ride-app pickups are permitted only in the Central Garage and the Terminal B garage, so the taxed behaviour is the only available behaviour. Better still, the substitute they actually want - shared rides - is priced BELOW the fee at a dollar fifty rather than left untaxed, so the cheap margin is the one that serves the policy goal of fewer single-occupancy trips. That is the design principle worth stating: you do not beat the Cobra Effect by moralising, you beat it by making the margin either unavailable or aligned. Do be honest that PAYT is not a failed policy - it works when paired with cheap recycling, monitored drop sites and credible fines. Same fix: close the avoidance margin.

§3.6  Deadweight Loss as a Welfare Integral

10 min · The triangle in dollars

DWL = the integral of the wedge

\[\text{DWL} = \int_{Q_t}^{Q^*}\big[\,p^d(q) - p^s(q)\,\big]\,dq\]

Geometry

Area between inverse demand and inverse supply, between post-tax \(Q_t\) and pre-tax \(Q^*\).

Economics

Value of trades that didn't happen — not transferred, just gone.

Linear special case: \[\text{DWL} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot t \cdot \Delta Q\] — the Harberger triangle (1964 AER).

Why the integral collapses to a triangle

Both curves are straight lines, so the gap between them is a straight line too — and the area under a straight line is a triangle.

\[\int_{Q_t}^{Q^*}\!\big[p^d-p^s\big]dq=\tfrac12\,t\,\Delta Q\]

The gap is \(\mathbf{t}\) at \(Q_t\) and \(\mathbf{0}\) at \(Q^*\) — height \(t\), base \(\Delta Q\).

Curved demand? The triangle is still a good approximation — Harberger, 1964.

The point is to retire the integral, not to practise it: for linear supply and demand the triangle is exact, so students should always reach for geometry. Endpoints are the argument - at the taxed quantity the vertical gap between inverse demand and inverse supply is exactly t, and at the untaxed equilibrium it is zero, so a linear function running from t down to 0 over a base of delta-Q has area one-half t delta-Q. Harberger's 1964 result is the footnote: for nonlinear curves a second-order Taylor expansion preserves the triangle to first approximation, which is why the formula survives well outside the linear case.

Doubling the tax more than doubles the loss

Walkthrough 3.4's fee again. Massport went \(\$3.25\to\$5.50\); the original plan was \(\$7.50\), frozen in the March 2025 deal. What would that second step have cost?

DWL more than doubles. The wedge widens and the base shrinks — the triangle grows on both sides at once.

Revenue is ambiguous. More per ride, but fewer rides. Which wins depends on elasticity.

Every tax rise buys revenue at a worsening exchange rate. That is the \(t^{2}\) result, seen on a real fee.

Photo: David Wilson / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.

Geometry to narrate off the diagram rather than label on it: when t doubles, the old DWL triangle is not simply scaled - the new region covers the old one plus the strips either side, which is where "more than doubles" comes from. Revenue splits into two opposing pieces: the state gains the extra fee on rides that still happen, and loses the fee entirely on rides that stop. Net direction depends on how elastic airport rideshare demand is - and at an airport, with parking and transit as substitutes, it is more elastic than people expect. Walkthrough 3.5 — A \$3 egg tax: DWL two ways

Before any tax: the elasticity-share rule is about who absorbs a shock — any shift in supply or demand, not just a tax. Here the shock is bird flu.

Problem. \(Q^d = 200-5p\), \(Q^s = 50+10p\), seller-side tax \(t=\$3\).

(a) Post-tax equilibrium

\(200-5p_c = 50+10(p_c-3)\)
\(\mathbf{p_c=\$12}\), \(\mathbf{p_s=\$9}\), \(\mathbf{Q=140}\)

(b) Triangle

\(\tfrac12 \cdot t \cdot \Delta Q = \tfrac12(3)(10) = \mathbf{\$15}\)M/wk

(c) Integrate the wedge

\(\displaystyle\int_{140}^{150}\!\left(45-\tfrac{3q}{10}\right)dq = \mathbf{\$15}\)M/wk

Both routes give \$15M. For a straight-line market the triangle is the integral — so use the triangle.

The point of doing it twice is to retire the integral, not to teach it: on linear curves the triangle is exact, so students should reach for geometry every time. Numbers worth voicing rather than showing - DWL of \$15M is only about 3.6% of the tax revenue collected here, which is what "a small tax on inelastic demand" looks like. Sanity check: the wedge is \$3 and quantity falls by 10, so half the rectangle is the loss. If a student asks why it is a triangle and not a rectangle, that is the difference between transfer (revenue, a rectangle) and destruction (DWL, the triangle) - Heads Up 3.4 next makes the t-squared point off the same figure.

Heads Up 3.4 — DWL grows with \(t^2\), not \(t\)

“Double the tax, double the DWL” — wrong. \(\Delta Q\) scales with \(t\), so \(\text{DWL} = \tfrac{1}{2} t \Delta Q\) scales with \(t^2\).

§3.7  Pass-Through Regimes

8 min · The 0–100% benchmark and what breaks it

Three pass-through regimes

\(0 < dp_c/dt < 1\)

Sub-100%

Linear competitive baseline. Both finite elasticities.

\(dp_c/dt = 1\)

Full 100%

Perfectly elastic supply (\(\varepsilon_S \to \infty\)). Small open economy on world market.

\(dp_c/dt > 1\)

Over-shifting

Cannot arise in competitive linear model. Fingerprint of market power.

Pass-through is a testable empirical quantity. When the data show \(> 100\%\), Ch 11 (monopoly) gives the formula that explains why.

Theory & Data 3.2 — Philly vs Boulder soda

Philadelphia (1.5¢/oz, 2017): pass-through \(\approx \mathbf{97\%}\) (Seiler-Tuchman-Yao 2021)

Boulder (2¢/oz, 2017): \(\approx \mathbf{50\%}\) (scanner) to \(\mathbf{75\%}\) (audit) (Cawley et al. 2021)

Same theory, different numbers. Some is measurement (data type matters); some is structural (Philly chain-grocery vs Boulder mix).

Poll #3: When does pass-through exceed 100%?

2025 tariff data show some categories with pass-through estimates above 1.0. What does this most likely indicate?

A) Measurement error in the data
B) Market power on the seller side
C) The competitive linear model is wrong

60 sec to vote

Answer: D — over-shifting needs market power

In competition the wedge splits between the two sides — so pass-through is capped at 100%.

With market power price is a markup over cost. Raise the cost and the markup rises with it — so the price can move by more than the tax.

Over-shifting is a fingerprint of market power, not a puzzle about taxes.

Ch 11 derives the monopolist's pass-through and the exact curvature condition.

The trap is thinking over-shifting is impossible because the tax is only t. It is possible, but only outside the competitive model - a firm charging markup x MC passes on the cost AND re-marks it up, so with convex enough demand the price rises by more than t. Concrete hook if wanted: several 2025 tariff categories showed retail price increases exceeding the tariff, which is diagnostic of concentrated sellers rather than of the tariff itself. Flag that this is the first time in the course the competitive assumption visibly fails, and that Chs 11-12 are where it gets relaxed properly.

Key Takeaways

Elasticity is a point, not a slope. \(\varepsilon=(dQ/dp)(p/Q)\) — it varies along a straight line.

Revenue peaks at \(\varepsilon_D=-1\). Inelastic → raise price. Elastic → cut it.

Whoever can walk away, walks away. Who writes the cheque is irrelevant.
