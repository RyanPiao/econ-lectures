The law of demand says quantity reacts to price. Elasticity is how big that reaction is.

\[ \varepsilon_D = \frac{\text{quantity reaction}}{\text{price reaction}} = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta p} \]

reacts less than price

reacts more than price

§3.1  From Midpoint to Point Elasticity

Promoting elasticity from a percent-change to a derivative

The point-elasticity formula

\[\varepsilon_D = \frac{dQ}{dp} \cdot \frac{p}{Q}.\]

Two pieces

\(dQ/dp\) is the quantity response — units per \$1. We draw \(p\) up the vertical axis, so this is the reciprocal of the slope you see, not the slope itself. \(p/Q\) rescales it into percentage units.

Sign matters

Law of demand: \(dQ/dp < 0\), so \(\varepsilon_D < 0\). Micro Theory keeps the sign. No absolute-value hiding.

Same vocabulary: \(|\varepsilon|>1\) elastic · \(|\varepsilon|<1\) inelastic · \(|\varepsilon|=1\) unit elastic · \(0\) perfectly inelastic · \(\infty\) perfectly elastic.

Units discipline. \(\varepsilon_D\) is unit-free — but \(dQ/dp\) and \(Q\) are not. Every demand function in this chapter is printed with its units, and you should read the numbers out in full: “two hundred forty thousand entries a day,” never “240.” Write \(240\) where the curve means \(240{,}000\) and \(\varepsilon=-0.25\) becomes \(-250\) — off by a factor of a thousand, with nothing in the algebra to catch it.

Farewell to the Midpoint Method

You spent a chapter of Principles on this. Two minutes to retire it — and to say exactly what replaces it.

What Principles did

\[\varepsilon^{\text{arc}}_D=\frac{(Q_2-Q_1)\big/\overline{Q}}{(p_2-p_1)\big/\overline{p}}, \quad \overline{Q}=\frac{Q_1+Q_2}{2}\]

Its formal name is arc elasticity. Dividing by the midpoint rather than the starting value is what makes the answer come out the same going up as coming down — the one real reason to teach it.

It is a finite-difference approximation to the point elasticity somewhere between the two observations. Shrink the interval and it becomes the derivative.

Why one derivative replaces it

Hand us a calibrated curve \(Q^d(p)\) and there is nothing to average. Differentiate once, evaluate anywhere: \(\varepsilon_D=\dfrac{dQ}{dp}\dfrac{p}{Q}\). One line, exact, and it works when demand is not a straight line — which arc cannot do at all.

Where arc still earns its keep: two raw scanner observations and no model. No function, nothing to differentiate — average the endpoints. That is the only case.

For the record, on the toll demand below: arc across \(\$9\to\$12\) is \(-0.212\), point at \(\$12\) is \(-0.250\). Same slope \(-5{,}000\) both times — the only difference is which \((p,Q)\) you rescale by. That is the whole story, and it is why we stop computing the first one.

From here on: point elasticity only. If you have a function, differentiate it. That sentence is most of the reason this course is taught with calculus.

§3.2–3.3  Linear Demand Zones & Revenue

14 min · Where on the curve are you?

Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve

Nothing new needed — just the point-elasticity formula, read carefully.

\(\varepsilon_D=\underbrace{\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}}_{\text{fixed}}\cdot\underbrace{\dfrac{p}{Q^d}}_{\text{moves}}\)

For \(Q^d=a-bp\), \(dQ^d/dp=-b\). That is a constant — it is the same at every price on the curve.

So the only thing that changes as you walk along the line is the \((p,Q)\) pair:

\(\varepsilon_D=\dfrac{-bp}{a-bp}\)

\(p \to 0\): \(p/Q\to0\), so \(\varepsilon_D \to 0\) — perfectly inelastic at the \(Q\)-intercept

\(p \to a/b\): \(Q\to0\), so \(\varepsilon_D \to -\infty\) — perfectly elastic at the \(p\)-intercept

\(p = a/(2b)\): \(\varepsilon_D = -1\) (unit elastic, midpoint) — the revenue-maximising price, derived in §3.3. Not the profit-maximising one.

Figure 3.2 — Walk down the curve: \(dQ/dp\) never moves, \(\varepsilon_D\) does

Drag the price: the curve never bends, yet \(\varepsilon_D\) runs from \(0\) to \(-\infty\).

Do not introduce anything new here - this whole slide is the point-elasticity formula read carefully. Epsilon is a product of two factors; on a straight line the first one is nailed down at -b, so every bit of the variation has to come from p over Q. Walk the slider from the bottom of the curve to the top and narrate it: at the quantity intercept p is nearly zero so p/Q is nearly zero and demand is perfectly inelastic; at the price intercept Q is nearly zero so p/Q blows up and demand is perfectly elastic; exactly halfway the two effects balance and epsilon is -1. That midpoint is the revenue-maximising price, which is section 3.3 two slides on. Heads Up 3.2 next is the same lesson from the other side - there the price is held fixed and the curve changes.

Answer: B — \(p = 25\)

No shortcut needed — the definition does it in three steps.

1. Read \(dQ/dp\) straight off the demand function:  \(\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp} = -2\)

2. Put \(p=25\) into the curve:  \(Q^d = 100 - 2(25) = 50\)

3. Apply the definition:  \(\varepsilon_D = \dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{p}{Q^d} = -2\cdot\dfrac{25}{50} = \mathbf{-1}\) ✓

\(dQ/dp\) was \(-2\) the whole way along. What made \(\varepsilon_D=-1\) here is the \(p/Q\) factor — which is the entire content of Heads Up 3.2.

Trap (D). “Need more info” is wrong: the curve alone fixes both \(dQ/dp\) and \(p/Q\) at every price.

Deliberately NOT the midpoint shortcut p = a/(2b). That formula gets the right answer and teaches nothing - students memorise it, then cannot do the next problem where demand is not linear. These three steps are the same three they will use for every elasticity in the course: read dQ/dp, find the quantity at that price, multiply by p over Q. Land the closing point hard - dQ/dp never changed, so dQ/dp cannot be what made this unit elastic; only p/Q moved. If someone volunteers the a/(2b) shortcut, confirm it works for linear demand and then ask what they would do without it.

Revenue and the unit-elasticity rule

Revenue is price times quantity — a product, so differentiate it as one. Everything on this slide is about a revenue-maximiser.

1. Product rule on \(R(p)=p\cdot Q^d(p)\):
\(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p) + p\,\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\)

2. Factor out \(Q^d\):
\(= Q^d(p)\left[\,1 + \dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\dfrac{p}{Q^d}\,\right]\)

3. That bracket is \(\varepsilon_D\):

\(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p)\,[\,1+\varepsilon_D\,]\)

\(Q^d>0\) always — so the bracket alone decides the sign.

So where you sit decides what to do

Inelastic

\(\varepsilon_D>-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp>0\) — raise the price

Unit elastic

\(\varepsilon_D=-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp=0\) — revenue peaks

Elastic

\(\varepsilon_D<-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp<0\) — lower the price

Laffer logic for an excise tax: revenue climbs with the rate only until the market turns elastic — past that, a higher rate collects less.

Say it precisely

A revenue-maximiser stops at \(\varepsilon_D=-1\). A profit-maximiser with positive marginal cost stops earlier, in the elastic region \(|\varepsilon_D|>1\), where marginal revenue is still positive and can equal \(MC\). Unit elasticity is the profit rule only in the special case \(MC=0\). Chs 9 and 11 do the profit version properly.

Elasticities — Terminology

\(\displaystyle E^D = \frac{\%\Delta Q^D}{\%\Delta P}\)

  • Inelastic: \(0 < |E^D| < 1\)
  • Unit elastic: \(|E^D| = 1\)
  • Elastic: \(|E^D| > 1\)
  • Perfectly elastic: \(|E^D| = \infty\)
  • Perfectly inelastic: \(|E^D| = 0\)

Important: Elasticities do not have units attached.

  • Allows comparison across goods and markets.
  • Same classifications apply to supply (\(E^S\)).

Review · Principles of Microeconomics

Application — Innovation, Substitutes and Market Power

Consumer and producer surplus are how we measure the value a new product creates — e.g., a solar-powered car. The CS triangle is the welfare gain to buyers.

Thought experiment. Hold \(P^*\) and \(Q^*\) fixed. Draw two demand curves with different slopes, both through E. Which generates more CS?

1. Steep demand — inelastic

\(|\varepsilon_D| < 1\). Some inframarginal buyers will pay far above \(P^*\) ⇒ tall CS triangle. The profile of a brand-new product with few substitutes.

2. Shallow demand — elastic

\(|\varepsilon_D| > 1\). Substitutes are easy; buyers' WTP clusters near \(P^*\) ⇒ short CS triangle. The profile of a mature market.

Figure 3.3 — Consumer Surplus & Elasticity of Demand

Blue = a new innovative product (few substitutes ⇒ steep). Red = a mature market (many substitutes ⇒ flat). As rivals arrive, demand flattens blue→red and CS shrinks.

Role of elasticity. At the same \((P^*, Q^*)\), more inelastic demand ⇒ more CS. Elasticity is the geometric translator between “steepness” and “welfare.”

Role of innovation

New products whose buyers have highly varied WTP (insulin pumps, iPhones, EVs) face steep demand ⇒ a large CS triangle ⇒ big welfare gains.

Heads up — the word runs the other way

A dozen firms make eyeglasses, so the demand any one faces is elastic — nudge price up and buyers walk. Patent the contact lens and the substitutes are gone, so the demand you face becomes LESS elastic, not more. That is what market power is (Chs 9, 11–12).

Speak: "same P*, Q* — but the steeper curve hides inframarginal buyers willing to pay MUCH more. Their excess WTP is consumer surplus." Pedagogical link: when we measure the welfare gain of a new technology, we INTEGRATE under the new demand curve above the new price — i.e., the CS triangle. §3.5  Tax Incidence — Who Actually Pays?

14 min · The wedge equation

A tax is an incentive — and elasticity is the response

A per-unit tax \(t\) raises what buyers pay to \(p_c\) and cuts what sellers keep to \(p_s\). Who moves?

Inelastic — weak response

Eggs \(-0.23\), cigarettes \(-0.4\), insulin \(\approx 0\).
Buyers cannot walk away → \(p_c\) rises by nearly all of \(t\).
Fat revenue, thin DWL.

Elastic — strong response

Luxury yachts \(-2\), one cereal brand, soda with substitutes.
Buyers leave → the tax lands on sellers.
Thin revenue, fat DWL.

Two halves of one story — and §3.5 turns it into a formula.

The sentence to land is that a tax is just a price change, so everything students already know about elasticity applies unchanged. Voice the trade-off explicitly: a government that wants REVENUE taxes inelastic things (cigarettes, fuel, salt historically); a government that wants BEHAVIOUR CHANGE taxes elastic things - but then collects little, which is the point. §3.5 formalises this as dp_c/dt = eS/(eS - eD), and the two columns here are just that formula's two extremes.

The tax wedge

The wedge

\(p_c\) = price buyer pays
\(p_s\) = price seller receives
\(p_c - p_s = t\)

Statutory incidence (whose paycheck the deduction shows up on) is economically irrelevant.

Equilibrium with tax

Statute on sellers:
\(Q^d(p_c) = Q^s(p_c - t)\)

One equation in one unknown \(p_c\) for each \(t\). At \(t = 0\): \(p_c = p_s = p^*\).

The comparative-statics question: how do \(p_c\) and \(p_s\) move as \(t\) rises? Total differentiate.

Try It — A \$5 tax on rideshare drivers

Problem. Rides: \(Q^d = 120 - 2p\), \(Q^s = 3p\) (\(Q\) in thousands/week, \(p\) in \$/ride). The city charges drivers \(t=\$5\) per ride. Find \(p_c\), \(p_s\), \(Q\), then CS, PS, tax revenue and DWL.

\(120-2p = 3p \Rightarrow p^*=\$24,\ Q^*=72\)
CS \(=\tfrac12(60-24)(72)=1{,}296\)   PS \(=\tfrac12(24)(72)=864\)

\(120-2p_c = 3(p_c-5) \Rightarrow \mathbf{{p_c=\$27}}\)
\(\mathbf{{p_s=\$22}}\), \(\mathbf{{Q=120-2(27)=66}}\)  riders +\$3, drivers −\$2

CS \(=\tfrac12(60-27)(66)=\mathbf{{1{,}089}}\)

Calculus Corner 3.2 — Deriving the incidence formula

Differentiate \(Q^d(p_c) = Q^s(p_c - t)\) w.r.t. \(t\). Both sides need the chain rule — \(p_c\) is itself a function of \(t\):

\[\frac{dQ^d}{dp_c}\cdot\frac{dp_c}{dt} = \frac{dQ^s}{dp_s}\left(\frac{dp_c}{dt} - 1\right)\]

Solve for \(dp_c/dt\), then multiply num/denom by \(p^*/Q^*\) to turn the derivatives into elasticities:

\[\boxed{\dfrac{dp_c}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}, \qquad \dfrac{dp_s}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_D}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}}\]

\(\varepsilon_S > 0\) ⇒ \(dp_c/dt > 0\) (consumer price rises)

\(\varepsilon_D < 0\) ⇒ \(dp_s/dt < 0\) (seller price falls)

For finite nonzero elasticities: \(0 < dp_c/dt < 1\). Pass-through is strictly between 0% and 100% in the competitive linear model.

First chain rule of the course. Math Refresher →

Reading the formula

\(\dfrac{dp_c}{dt}=\dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S-\varepsilon_D}\) — a ratio of one side's responsiveness to the total.

Demand inelastic

\(\varepsilon_D\to 0 \Rightarrow dp_c/dt\to 1\)
Buyers pay it all. Cigarettes, eggs, insulin.

Supply inelastic

\(\varepsilon_S\to 0 \Rightarrow dp_c/dt\to 0\)
Sellers eat it. Land, port slots, sports stars.

The side that cannot walk away bears the tax.

The denominator is total responsiveness across both sides; the numerator is the seller's share of it. So the formula is just "how much of the combined flexibility sits on the other side". Land is the cleanest inelastic-supply case - you cannot make more of it, so a tax on land value falls entirely on landowners, which is the whole Georgist argument. Sanity rule worth repeating from Heads Up 3.1: shares must land in [0,1]; anything outside means a sign slip.

Walkthrough 3.3 — Eggs: \(2/3\) price, \(1/3\) quantity

Before any tax: the elasticity-share rule is about who absorbs a shock — any shift in supply or demand, not just a tax. Here the shock is bird flu.

Problem. US eggs — \(Q^d = 200-5p\), \(Q^s = 50+10p\)  (\(Q\) in M dozens/wk, \(p\) in \$/dozen); \(p^*=\$10\), \(Q^*=150\). HPAI cull shifts supply in by \(\Delta\alpha=-30\).

(a) Elasticities at \(p^*=10\)

\(\varepsilon_D = (-5)\dfrac{10}{150} = \mathbf{-\tfrac{1}{3}}\) · \(\varepsilon_S = (10)\dfrac{10}{150} = \mathbf{+\tfrac{2}{3}}\)
\(|\varepsilon_S|/|\varepsilon_D| = 2\)

(b) The split

Price share \(=\dfrac{2/3}{2/3+1/3} = \mathbf{\tfrac{2}{3}}\)
Quantity share \(= \mathbf{\tfrac{1}{3}}\)

(c) Ch 2 agrees

\(\Delta p = +2\), \(\Delta Q = -10\) — the same \(2\!:\!1\) split.

The more elastic side escapes — shock or tax, the rule does not care which. Supply is twice as elastic here, so demand absorbs twice the share.

First numerical workout of the incidence idea, and deliberately on a NON-tax shock - the HPAI cull from Chs 1 and 2 - so students see the formula is about elasticities, not about taxes. Say the lead-in rather than showing it. Part (c) is the payoff: Ch 2 solved this by re-solving the whole system and got dp = +2, dQ = -10; the elasticity route gets the same 2:1 split in one line. Same answer two ways - the §3.5 elasticity formula and Ch 2's solve-then-differentiate route are the same mathematics wearing different clothes. Sanity check to voice: supply here is twice as elastic as demand, so demand should eat twice the burden, and it does.

Walkthrough 3.4 — The Logan rideshare fee

Problem. On 1 July 2025 Massport raised the per-trip rideshare fee at Logan from \(\$3.25\) to \(\$5.50\): a unit tax of \(t=\$2.25\) a ride, billed to the trip. For airport rideshare take \(\varepsilon_D=-0.6\), \(\varepsilon_S=+2.0\).  (a) Rider’s share and \(\Delta p_c\).  (b) Driver’s share and \(\Delta p_s\).  (c) Swap the elasticities — what changes?

(a) & (b) The split

\(\dfrac{dp_c}{dt}=\dfrac{2.0}{2.0+0.6}=\mathbf{0.769}\)
\(\Delta p_c = \mathbf{+\$1.73}\), \(\Delta p_s = \mathbf{-\$0.52}\)

(c) Now swap them

\(\varepsilon_D=-2.0\), \(\varepsilon_S=+0.6\) → \(\dfrac{0.6}{2.6}=\mathbf{0.231}\)
Rider \(\mathbf{+\$0.52}\), driver \(\mathbf{-\$1.73}\). The shares flip.

Whoever can walk away, walks away. Nothing changed about who hands over the money — only the two elasticities.

The given \(\varepsilon\)’s are the estimated ones, so (a) is the real case: riders are the inelastic side and pay about \(\$1.73\) of every \(\$2.25\).

\(\varepsilon_D=-0.57\) for UberX: Cohen et al. (2016, NBER w22627). \(\varepsilon_S\): driver supply highly elastic — Hall, Horton & Knoepfle.

Local, current, and a clean unit tax students have actually paid. Read the setup out: the fee went three twenty-five to five fifty on 1 July 2025, so t is two twenty-five a ride, and the two elasticities are GIVEN the way they are in every incidence question - the work is applying the formula, not guessing the parameters. Each bar now appears WITH its own case, so nobody sees the answer to (c) while you are still doing (a) and (b). Stress that the given numbers are anchored, not invented: Cohen and co-authors used Uber surge pricing as a regression discontinuity across four US markets and got about minus zero point five seven for UberX, and Hall, Horton and Knoepfle find driver supply highly elastic because there is no hours restriction and essentially free entry. Part (c) is the whole walkthrough - swap the two numbers and the burden reverses with no change to who hands over the money. Anyone still thinking "the driver pays, it is billed to the trip" cannot hold that afterwards. The airport nuance worth voicing: parking, the Silver Line and a friend at the curb are real substitutes, so you might expect demand to be elastic here - the estimates say otherwise, and that gap between intuition and evidence is the useful moment. Hands to section 3.6, where Massport wanting seven fifty instead of five fifty is exactly the t-squared question.

Heads Up 3.3 — Statutory incidence is irrelevant

Whether the statute puts the tax on the buyer or the seller, the same \(dp_c/dt = \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)\) emerges from total differentiation. Mathematical identity, not coincidence.

What is conventional

The sign on \(\varepsilon_D\). If you let the absolute value slip and write \(\varepsilon_S + |\varepsilon_D|\) in the denominator, the formula still gives the right answer. Mix the two conventions and you'll compute negative shares.

Poll #2: Burden split

Luxury yacht market: \(\varepsilon_D = -2.0\) (rich folks have substitutes), \(\varepsilon_S = 0.5\). 10% excise tax. Whose burden is bigger?

A) Buyers (rich folks pay)
B) Sellers (yacht builders eat it)
C) Equal split
D) Depends on the statute

60 sec to vote

Answer: B — Sellers eat it

Consumer share \(= 0.5 / (0.5 + 2.0) = 0.20\). Sellers bear 80%. The 1990 U.S. luxury tax on yachts and small aircraft was repealed in 1993 partly because boatbuilders absorbed it — jobs, not yachts, were lost.

Trap (D): Statutory irrelevance — the formula doesn't see whose paycheck the deduction shows up on. It sees behavior.

§3.6  Deadweight Loss as a Welfare Integral

10 min · The triangle in dollars

Why the integral collapses to a triangle

Both curves are straight lines, so the gap between them is a straight line too — and the area under a straight line is a triangle.

\[\int_{Q_t}^{Q^*}\!\big[p^d-p^s\big]dq=\tfrac12\,t\,\Delta Q\]

The gap is \(\mathbf{t}\) at \(Q_t\) and \(\mathbf{0}\) at \(Q^*\) — height \(t\), base \(\Delta Q\).

Curved demand? The triangle is still a good approximation — Harberger, 1964.

The point is to retire the integral, not to practise it: for linear supply and demand the triangle is exact, so students should always reach for geometry. Endpoints are the argument - at the taxed quantity the vertical gap between inverse demand and inverse supply is exactly t, and at the untaxed equilibrium it is zero, so a linear function running from t down to 0 over a base of delta-Q has area one-half t delta-Q. Harberger's 1964 result is the footnote: for nonlinear curves a second-order Taylor expansion preserves the triangle to first approximation, which is why the formula survives well outside the linear case.

Walkthrough 3.5 — A \$3 egg tax: DWL two ways

Problem. US eggs — \(Q^d = 200-5p\), \(Q^s = 50+10p\)  (\(Q\) in M dozens/wk, \(p\) in \$/dozen). Seller-side tax \(t=\$3\) a dozen.

(a) Post-tax equilibrium

\(200-5p_c = 50+10(p_c-3)\)
\(\mathbf{p_c=\$12}\), \(\mathbf{p_s=\$9}\), \(\mathbf{Q=140}\)

(b) Triangle

\(\tfrac12 \cdot t \cdot \Delta Q = \tfrac12(3)(10) = \mathbf{\$15}\)M/wk

(c) Integrate the wedge

\(\displaystyle\int_{140}^{150}\!\left(45-\tfrac{3q}{10}\right)dq = \mathbf{\$15}\)M/wk

Both routes give \$15M. For a straight-line market the triangle is the integral — so use the triangle.

The point of doing it twice is to retire the integral, not to teach it: on linear curves the triangle is exact, so students should reach for geometry every time. Numbers worth voicing rather than showing - DWL of \$15M is only about 3.6% of the tax revenue collected here, which is what "a small tax on inelastic demand" looks like. Sanity check: the wedge is \$3 and quantity falls by 10, so half the rectangle is the loss. If a student asks why it is a triangle and not a rectangle, that is the difference between transfer (revenue, a rectangle) and destruction (DWL, the triangle) - Heads Up 3.4 next makes the t-squared point off the same figure.

§3.7  Pass-Through Regimes

8 min · The 0–100% benchmark and what breaks it

Three pass-through regimes

\(0 < dp_c/dt < 1\)

Sub-100%

Linear competitive baseline. Both finite elasticities.

\(dp_c/dt = 1\)

Full 100%

Perfectly elastic supply (\(\varepsilon_S \to \infty\)). Small open economy on world market.

\(dp_c/dt > 1\)

Over-shifting

Cannot arise in competitive linear model. Fingerprint of market power.

Pass-through is a testable empirical quantity. When the data show \(> 100\%\), Ch 11 (monopoly) gives the formula that explains why.

Theory & Data 3.2 — Philly vs Boulder soda

Philadelphia (1.5¢/oz, 2017): pass-through \(\approx \mathbf{97\%}\) (Seiler-Tuchman-Yao 2021)

Boulder (2¢/oz, 2017): \(\approx \mathbf{50\%}\) (scanner) to \(\mathbf{75\%}\) (audit) (Cawley et al. 2021)

Same theory, different numbers. Some is measurement (data type matters); some is structural (Philly chain-grocery vs Boulder mix).

Poll #3: When does pass-through exceed 100%?

2025 tariff data show some categories with pass-through estimates above 1.0. What does this most likely indicate?

A) Measurement error in the data
B) Market power on the seller side
C) The competitive linear model is wrong
D) Both B and C

60 sec to vote

Answer: D — over-shifting needs market power

In competition the wedge splits between the two sides — so pass-through is capped at 100%.

With market power price is a markup over cost. Raise the cost and the markup rises with it — so the price can move by more than the tax.

Over-shifting is a fingerprint of market power, not a puzzle about taxes.

Ch 11 derives the monopolist's pass-through and the exact curvature condition.

The trap is thinking over-shifting is impossible because the tax is only t. It is possible, but only outside the competitive model - a firm charging markup x MC passes on the cost AND re-marks it up, so with convex enough demand the price rises by more than t. Concrete hook if wanted: several 2025 tariff categories showed retail price increases exceeding the tariff, which is diagnostic of concentrated sellers rather than of the tariff itself. Flag that this is the first time in the course the competitive assumption visibly fails, and that Chs 11-12 are where it gets relaxed properly.

Key Takeaways

Elasticity is a point, not a slope. \(\varepsilon=(dQ/dp)(p/Q)\) — it varies along a straight line.

Revenue peaks at \(\varepsilon_D=-1\). Inelastic → raise price. Elastic → cut it. A profit-maximiser with \(MC>0\) stops earlier, still elastic.

Whoever can walk away, walks away. Who writes the cheque is irrelevant.

DWL grows with \(t^{2}\). Double the tax, quadruple the damage.

Ch 2's grocery manager could say which way price would move. You can now say by how much, and who pays for it.

Four takeaways, one per objective. The price-ceiling line is gone with §3.8-3.9. Pass-through is worth a spoken addition if the poll went well: in competitive linear markets it sits in [0,1], and anything outside that range is a fingerprint of market power, which is where Ch 11 picks up.

§3.4  Income & Cross-Price Elasticities

Next meeting's opener — not today

8 min · Two more elasticities, same template · moved off this meeting so tax incidence gets the time it needs

PARKED (Sept 2026 review). These two slides used to sit between the revenue section and tax incidence, where they arrived rushed and pushed incidence into the last eight minutes of class. They now open the NEXT meeting, which is a natural seam - own-price elasticity is finished, and income and cross-price are the same partial-derivative template with a different variable on the bottom. Do not present them today. If the room is somehow ahead of schedule, the better use of the time is the Logan exercise, not these.

Income elasticity

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} = \dfrac{\partial Q^d_i}{\partial I} \cdot \dfrac{I}{Q^d_i}\)  —  the sign classifies the good, the size ranks it

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} > 0\) — Normal

• Necessity: \(0 < \varepsilon < 1\) (food)
• Luxury: \(\varepsilon > 1\) (restaurant meals)

Restaurant meals, \(\varepsilon_I \approx 1.0\) — right on the luxury edge.

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} < 0\) — Inferior

Quantity falls as income rises — buyers substitute toward something better.

MBTA Orange Line — as income rises, riders switch to cars.

U.S. food: \(\varepsilon_I \approx 0.1\)–\(0.3\). Bus travel can flip from inferior to normal at different income levels.

Same partial-derivative template as own-price elasticity, just with respect to income. The split to hammer: the SIGN says normal or inferior, the SIZE says necessity or luxury - two different questions off one number. Photos are the Principles anchors: fine dining for the luxury edge, and the Orange Line as the local Boston example students actually ride. Bus travel is the honest complication - it is inferior over some income ranges and normal over others, so the label belongs to a range, not to the good forever.

Cross-price elasticity

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} = \dfrac{\partial Q^d_i}{\partial p_j} \cdot \dfrac{p_j}{Q^d_i}\)  —  does \(i\) sell more or less when \(j\) gets pricier?

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} > 0\) — Substitutes

\(j\) gets pricier, buyers move to \(i\). Tea/coffee, iPhone/Galaxy, ride-hail/rental.

Raise the price of one console, buyers shift to the other.

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} < 0\) — Complements

\(j\) gets pricier, demand for \(i\) falls too. Printer/ink, console/games.

Console and cartridge — price up on one, demand down on both.

\(\varepsilon_{i,j}=0\) — unrelated goods, no cross-relationship at all.  •  Antitrust use: a large positive \(\varepsilon_{i,j}\) puts two products in the same relevant market for merger analysis.

The sign rule is the whole lesson: positive means substitutes, negative means complements, zero means unrelated. The console pair is worth dwelling on because both photos are the same console - what changes is what it is sitting next to, which is the point: the relationship lives between the two goods, not inside either one. Antitrust is the live application - a large positive cross-price elasticity is how regulators argue two products compete in the same market, and it comes back in Ch 12.
