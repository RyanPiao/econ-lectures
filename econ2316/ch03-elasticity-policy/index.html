Math Refresher — What does a ratio mean?

A ratio is just a comparison of two numbers: how big is \(A\) relative to \(B\)? Write it \(A/B\) (read “\(A\) to \(B\)” or “\(A\) per \(B\)”).

The idea

\(\dfrac{A}{B} = 2\)

“\(A\) is twice \(B\).”

\(\dfrac{A}{B} = 0.5\)

“\(A\) is half of \(B\).”

\(\dfrac{A}{B} = 1\)

“\(A\) and \(B\) are equal.”

Apple \$1, water bottle \$2: ratio \(= 2/1 = 2\) — water is 2× the apple.

MBTA fare hike (July 2025)

New subway fare \(p_{\text{new}} = \$2.90\)
Old subway fare \(p_{\text{old}} = \$2.40\)

Ratio \(\dfrac{p_{\text{new}}}{p_{\text{old}}} = \dfrac{2.90}{2.40} \approx \mathbf{1.208}\)

“The new fare is 120.8% of the old fare” — or equivalently, “\$1.21 of new per \$1.00 of old.”

Order matters. \(2.90/2.40 = 1.208\) is “new per old.” Flipping it — \(2.40/2.90 \approx 0.828\) — says the old fare was 82.8% of the new. Same data, different ratio, different statement.

Anchor: students need to read ratios as comparisons, not as raw numbers. The 1.208 is meaningful only because we know what's on top and bottom. This sets up elasticity, which is a ratio of two percentage changes — order matters there too.

Math Refresher — Percentage change

\(\displaystyle \%\Delta = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100\%\)

MBTA — continued

Numerator: \(p_{\text{new}} - p_{\text{old}} = 2.90 - 2.40 = \mathbf{\$0.50}\)
Denominator: \(p_{\text{old}} = \mathbf{\$2.40}\)

\(\%\Delta p = \dfrac{0.50}{2.40} \approx 0.2083 = \mathbf{+20.83\%}\)
(a price increase)

Why “old” in the denominator?

We want the change relative to the starting point — \$0.50 from \$2.40 is meaningfully different from \$0.50 from \$50.

Sign convention

\(\%\Delta > 0\) rise · \(\%\Delta < 0\) fall · \(\%\Delta = 0\) no change

Building block of elasticity: \(\;\varepsilon_D = \dfrac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta p}\) — two percentage changes divided by each other.

Connect explicitly: the MBTA fare ratio (slide before) was the building block; the percentage change formula here is the operational version. When we write elasticity as %ΔQ / %Δp, each numerator and denominator is a (new − old)/old calculation. Real-world: MBTA published ridership data shows weekday subway boardings dropped from ~510K to ~500K after the hike — students can compute the implied arc elasticity at home.

Why bother promoting elasticity to a derivative?

Midpoint (Principles)

An average over a price segment:

"How elastic between \(p_1\) and \(p_2\)?"

Point (Micro Theory)

A derivative at one price:

"How elastic right here, right now?"

The tax-incidence formula in §3.5 needs a point elasticity at the equilibrium — the midpoint can't deliver that. Same intuition, sharper math.

Same good, same price change — different elasticity depending on the time horizon. Substitutes take time to develop.

Elasticities — Terminology

\(\displaystyle E^D = \frac{\%\Delta Q^D}{\%\Delta P}\)

  • Inelastic: \(0 < |E^D| < 1\)
  • Unit elastic: \(|E^D| = 1\)
  • Elastic: \(|E^D| > 1\)
  • Perfectly elastic: \(|E^D| = \infty\)
  • Perfectly inelastic: \(|E^D| = 0\)

Important: Elasticities do not have units attached.

  • Allows comparison across goods and markets.
  • Same classifications apply to supply (\(E^S\)).

Review · Principles of Microeconomics

Elasticities and Linear Demand & Supply

We often assume demand and supply are linear, so knowing how to calculate elasticity along a linear curve is important. (Using \(d\) instead of \(\Delta\) — same idea, calculus notation.)

\(\displaystyle E^D = \frac{\%\,dQ^D}{\%\,dP} \;\;=\;\; \frac{dQ/Q}{dP/P}\)

Along an inverse linear demand/supply, the ratio \(dQ/dP = 1/\text{slope}\). Rewrite the formula:

\(\displaystyle E = \frac{dQ/Q}{dP/P} = \frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\text{slope}}}_{\substack{\text{constant along}\\\text{the curve}}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{P}{Q}}_{\substack{\text{falls as you move}\\\text{down the curve}}}\)

  • As you move down a linear demand curve, demand becomes less elastic (more inelastic).
  • Eventually perfectly inelastic at the horizontal (Q) axis.
  • And perfectly elastic at the vertical (P) axis.

Figure 6.1 — Walk along a linear demand: \(E\) varies as \(P/Q\) changes

Bridge slide between Principles (\(\Delta\)) and Micro Theory (\(d\)). Same arithmetic, calculus notation. The key insight: along a linear demand curve, slope is constant but elasticity falls as you move down — because \(P/Q\) falls. This sets up §3.2 directly.

Review · Principles of Microeconomics

Extreme Elasticities

(a) Perfectly inelastic

Perfectly Inelastic Demand & Supply

  • Quantity demanded/supplied does not change in response to price.
  • Example: life-saving drugs (near-perfectly inelastic demand).
  • When is \(E = 0\)? When the slope of demand/supply is infinite (vertical).

(b) Perfectly elastic

Perfectly Elastic Demand & Supply

  • Quantity is infinitely responsive to miniscule price changes.
  • Example: commodity crops (near-perfectly elastic demand to one farmer).
  • When is \(E = \infty\)? When the slope of demand/supply is zero (horizontal).
These are limiting cases — useful benchmarks for §3.5 tax incidence (perfectly inelastic supply: sellers eat the entire tax; perfectly elastic supply: buyers eat the entire tax). And for §3.7 pass-through: perfectly elastic supply gives 100% pass-through.

Review · Principles of Microeconomics

Income Elasticity of Demand

Ratio of the percentage change in quantity demanded to the percentage change in income:

\(\displaystyle E_I = \frac{\%\,dQ^D}{\%\,dI} = \frac{dQ/Q}{dI/I}\)

The sign of \(E_I\) classifies the good:

\(E_I > 0\) — Normal good

  • \(0 < E_I < 1\): necessity (food)
  • \(E_I > 1\): luxury (fine dining)

Restaurant meals \(\;E_I \approx 1.0\) (luxury edge).

\(E_I < 0\) — Inferior good

  • Quantity demanded falls as income rises.
  • Consumers substitute toward higher-quality alternatives.

MBTA Orange Line: as income rises, riders substitute to cars.

Same partial-derivative template as own-price elasticity, just with respect to income. The sign-vs-magnitude split (sign = normal/inferior, magnitude = necessity/luxury) is exactly the §3.4 lesson. MBTA Orange Line is the local Boston example students will recognize.

Review · Principles of Microeconomics

Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand

Ratio of the percentage change in one good's quantity demanded (\(X\)) to the percentage change in the price of another good (\(Y\)):

\(\displaystyle E^D_{XY} = \frac{\%\,dQ^D_X}{\%\,dP_Y} = \frac{dQ_X/Q_X}{dP_Y/P_Y}\)

The sign of \(E^D_{XY}\) reveals the relationship between the two goods:

\(E^D_{XY} < 0\) — Complements

Consumption of good \(X\) decreases with an increase in the price of related good \(Y\), and vice versa.

Switch console + Switch 2 cartridge — raising the price of one cuts demand for the other.

\(E^D_{XY} > 0\) — Substitutes

Consumption of good \(X\) increases with an increase in the price of related good \(Y\), and vice versa.

Switch + Xbox — raising the price of one shifts buyers toward the other.

Cross-price elasticity is the formal antitrust tool for defining "relevant market" — if two products have a large positive \(E^D_{XY}\), they belong in the same market. The sign rule (positive = substitutes, negative = complements, zero = unrelated) is the §3.4 takeaway.

§3.1  From Midpoint to Point Elasticity

Promoting elasticity from a percent-change to a derivative

The point-elasticity formula

\[\varepsilon_D = \lim_{\Delta p \to 0} \frac{\Delta Q/Q}{\Delta p/p} = \frac{dQ}{dp} \cdot \frac{p}{Q}.\]

Two pieces

\(dQ/dp\) is the slope. \(p/Q\) rescales it into percentage units — so the answer doesn't depend on whether \(p\) is in dollars or cents.

Sign matters

Law of demand: \(dQ/dp < 0\), so \(\varepsilon_D < 0\). Micro Theory keeps the sign. No absolute-value hiding.

Same vocabulary: \(|\varepsilon|>1\) elastic · \(|\varepsilon|<1\) inelastic · \(|\varepsilon|=1\) unit elastic · \(0\) perfectly inelastic · \(\infty\) perfectly elastic.

A first case: locked egg market

From Ch 2, demand \(Q^d = 200 - 5p\), supply \(Q^s = 50 + 10p\), pre-shock \(p^* = 10\), \(Q^* = 150\).

\(\varepsilon_D = (-5) \cdot (10/150) = -\tfrac{1}{3}\)

\(\varepsilon_S = (10) \cdot (10/150) = +\tfrac{2}{3}\)

The ratio \(|\varepsilon_S|/|\varepsilon_D| = 2\) does most of the work in §3.5. Inelastic demand + moderately inelastic supply is the staple-food story.

Heads Up 3.1 — Our \(\varepsilon_D\) is negative

Principles often quoted \(|\varepsilon_D|\) as positive numbers. Micro Theory keeps the sign. Mix conventions and the incidence formula in §3.5 will produce negative pass-through shares — a guaranteed clue you've sign-flipped.

Translating from Principles of Micro

Principles of Micro's “elasticity 0.5” is our \(\varepsilon_D = -0.5\).
Principles of Micro's “elasticity 1.2” is our \(\varepsilon_D = -1.2\).

Sanity rule

Pass-through and burden shares should land in \([0,1]\). If you see a negative or \(>1\) result, check the sign on \(\varepsilon_D\) first.

Arc elasticity — the approximation

When you have only two scanner observations and no functional form, you can't take a derivative. You can still estimate elasticity from the data using the midpoint (arc) formula.

\(\varepsilon^{\text{arc}}_D = \dfrac{(Q_2 - Q_1)/[(Q_1+Q_2)/2]}{(p_2 - p_1)/[(p_1+p_2)/2]}\)

It's a finite-difference approximation to the point elasticity, evaluated somewhere between the two observations — close to the point at the midpoint, less accurate over large segments.

Rule of thumb

• You have a calibrated function \(Q^d(p)\) → use the point elasticity (a derivative).
• You have two FRED data points with no functional form → use the arc elasticity (a finite difference).

The Walkthroughs in this chapter use the point formula (we have the calibrated demand). The end-of-chapter data problems use the arc formula (FRED gives observations, not parameters).

Make this distinction explicit: arc vs point is a research-design question, not a definition question. Both converge in the limit as the interval shrinks.

Application — The Value of Innovation

Consumer and producer surplus are how we measure the value a new product creates — e.g., a solar-powered car. The CS triangle is the welfare gain to buyers.

Thought experiment. Hold \(P^*\) and \(Q^*\) fixed. Draw two demand curves with different slopes, both through E. Which generates more CS?

1. Steep demand — inelastic

\(|\varepsilon_D| < 1\). Some inframarginal buyers will pay far above \(P^*\) ⇒ tall CS triangle.

2. Shallow demand — elastic

\(|\varepsilon_D| > 1\). Substitutes are easy; buyers' WTP clusters near \(P^*\) ⇒ short CS triangle.

Role of elasticity. At the same \((P^*, Q^*)\), more inelastic demand ⇒ more CS. Elasticity is the geometric translator between “steepness” and “welfare.”

Role of innovation

New products serving buyers with highly varied WTP (insulin pumps, iPhones, EVs) face steep demand ⇒ the CS triangle is large ⇒ the market creates big welfare gains.

Figure 3.3 — Consumer Surplus & Elasticity of Demand

4 fragments: (1) steep demand + tall CS + inelastic tag; (2) shallow demand + short CS + elastic tag; (3) key result on elasticity-as-translator; (4) innovation implication. Speak: "same P*, Q* — but the steeper curve hides inframarginal buyers willing to pay MUCH more. Their excess WTP is consumer surplus." Pedagogical link: when we measure the welfare gain of a new technology, we INTEGRATE under the new demand curve above the new price — i.e., the CS triangle. Innovations targeting buyers with highly varied valuations (insulin, smartphones, EVs) generate big welfare gains because the upper part of demand is steep.

Calculus Corner 3.1 — Elasticity = slope of \(\ln Q\) vs \(\ln p\)

\(\varepsilon_D = \dfrac{d\ln Q}{d\ln p}\)

Chain-rule check

\(\dfrac{d\ln Q}{d\ln p} = \dfrac{1}{Q}\cdot\dfrac{dQ}{dp}\cdot p = \dfrac{dQ}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{p}{Q}\). ✓

Why empiricists love this

Run a regression of \(\ln Q\) on \(\ln p\) — the coefficient is the elasticity. Gas demand: \(\hat\beta \approx -0.2\) (Hughes-Knittel-Sperling 2008).

§3.2–3.3  Linear Demand Zones & Revenue

14 min · Where on the curve are you?

Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve

For \(Q^d = a - bp\): slope \(=-b\) (constant), but \(\varepsilon_D = -bp/(a-bp)\) varies.

\(p \to 0\): \(\varepsilon_D \to 0\) (perfectly inelastic at \(Q\)-intercept)

\(p \to a/b\): \(\varepsilon_D \to -\infty\) (perfectly elastic at \(p\)-intercept)

\(p = a/(2b)\): \(\varepsilon_D = -1\) (unit elastic, midpoint)

The midpoint isn't a coincidence — it's the revenue-max price on a linear demand curve. We derive this in §3.3.

Heads Up 3.2 — Slope is not elasticity

Same slope, two demand curves, very different elasticities at the same price.

\(Q = 200 - 5p\) at \(p = 5\):
slope \(-5\), \(\varepsilon_D = (-5)(5/175) \approx -0.143\)

\(Q = 40 - 5p\) at \(p = 5\):
same slope \(-5\), \(\varepsilon_D = (-5)(5/15) \approx -1.667\)

Same slope. Elasticities differ by a factor of 12. The \(p/Q\) ratio is doing all the work.

Revenue and the unit-elasticity rule

\(\displaystyle \frac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p)\,[\,1 + \varepsilon_D\,]\)

Press ↓ to see the derivation · the sign of \(dR/dp\) tracks where you sit on the demand curve:

Inelastic

\(\varepsilon_D > -1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp > 0\).
Raise the price.

Unit elastic

\(\varepsilon_D = -1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp = 0\).
Revenue max.

Elastic

\(\varepsilon_D < -1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp < 0\).
Lower the price.

Laffer logic for excise taxes: revenue rises with the rate only until the market hits unit elasticity.

Hit down-arrow to walk through the differentiation step-by-step. The three-zone chart here is the punchline; the derivation explains why.

Deriving \(dR/dp = Q^d(p)\,[1 + \varepsilon_D]\)

Setup. Total revenue is price times quantity demanded: \(\;R(p) = p \cdot Q^d(p)\;\) — a product of two functions of \(p\).

Step 1 — Product rule

\(\displaystyle \frac{dR}{dp} = \frac{d}{dp}\big[p \cdot Q^d(p)\big] = Q^d(p) + p \cdot \frac{dQ^d}{dp}\)

Step 2 — Factor out \(Q^d(p)\)

\(\displaystyle = Q^d(p)\left[\,1 + \frac{dQ^d}{dp} \cdot \frac{p}{Q^d(p)}\,\right]\)

Step 3 — Recognize the elasticity

The bracketed second term is exactly the point elasticity of demand:
\(\;\;\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{p}{Q^d(p)} = \varepsilon_D\)

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\;\frac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p)\,[\,1 + \varepsilon_D\,]\;}\)

Two observations: (i) \(Q^d(p) > 0\) on the interior, so the sign of \(dR/dp\) equals the sign of \(1 + \varepsilon_D\). (ii) That sign depends entirely on where on the demand curve you sit — \(|\varepsilon_D|\) less than 1, equal to 1, or greater than 1.

Use down-arrow fragments to reveal each algebraic step. Step 1 product rule. Step 2 factor Q^d(p) out — note the multiply/divide trick (multiply the second term by Q^d/Q^d to introduce the p/Q^d factor). Step 3 recognize the elasticity. Final boxed result agrees with the previous slide.

Walkthrough 3.2 — The revenue-max NYC toll

Problem. \(Q^d(P) = 300{,}000 - 5{,}000\,P\).  (a) Find \(P^*\) that maximizes daily revenue.  (b) Compare with the actual \(\$9\) toll and scheduled \(\$12\).  (c) Check \(\varepsilon_D = -1\) at \(P^*\).

(a) Maximize revenue

\(R(P) = 300{,}000\,P - 5{,}000\,P^2\)
\(R'(P) = 300{,}000 - 10{,}000\,P = 0\)
\(\Rightarrow \mathbf{P^* = \$30}\), \(\mathbf{Q^* = 150{,}000}\), \(\mathbf{R^* = \$4.5\text{M/day}}\). SOC: \(R'' < 0\). ✓

(b) Compare actual tolls

\(P=\$9\): \(R = \mathbf{\$2.295\text{M/day}}\) · \(P=\$12\): \(R = \mathbf{\$2.88\text{M/day}}\). \$9 << \$30 → deep in inelastic zone.

(c) Unit elasticity at \(P^*\)

\(\varepsilon_D = (-5{,}000)\cdot\dfrac{30}{150{,}000} = \mathbf{-1}\). ✓ Midpoint of linear demand \(\equiv\) revenue-max.

Takeaway. \(P^* = \$30\) is the revenue-max toll; the \$9 toll sits deep in the inelastic zone, so each step up the phase-in raises revenue. But revenue-max \(\ne\) welfare-max — the cordon exists to correct an externality (Ch 17).

§3.4  Income & Cross-Price Elasticities

8 min · Two more elasticities, same template

Income elasticity

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} = \dfrac{\partial Q^d_i}{\partial I} \cdot \dfrac{I}{Q^d_i}\)

Normal

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} > 0\). Subdivisions:
• Necessity: \(0 < \varepsilon < 1\) (food)
• Luxury: \(\varepsilon > 1\) (restaurant meals)

Inferior

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} < 0\). Bus travel for low-income commuters: as income rises, substitute into cars.

People Respond to Incentives — The Cobra Effect

Key concept — Incentive

Anything that motivates a person to take a particular action — rewards (positive) or penalties (negative).

British colonial India: the government offered a bounty per dead cobra to reduce the snake population.

Phase 1. It works! People hunt cobras for the reward.

Phase 2. Entrepreneurs start breeding cobras to collect the bounty.

Phase 3. Government cancels the program. Breeders release their snakes — cobra population higher than before.

Lesson. Incentives are powerful — people respond. Designing them requires thinking several steps ahead about how behavior will adapt.

From Principles of Micro ch 1 §incentives. Walk through 3 phases — pause at each fragment. Modern analogs: Wells Fargo fake accounts (sales incentive gamed), publish-or-perish in academia (citation-stuffing), surge pricing creating a wedge between rider and driver incentives. The point: elasticity (next slides) is the QUANTITATIVE measure of "how much do people respond" — incentive theory is the qualitative motivation behind it.

Apply — How a Tax Reshapes Consumer Incentives

A per-unit tax \(t\) is an incentive change: it raises the price buyers pay from \(p^*\) to \(p_c > p^*\). How big is the response? — that's exactly what elasticity measures.

Inelastic demand — weak response

  • Eggs (\(\varepsilon_D \approx -0.23\)), cigarettes (\(-0.4\)), insulin (near 0).
  • Buyers can't easily walk away → tax raises \(p_c\) by nearly the full \(t\).
  • Small \(\Delta Q\) → tax-revenue rectangle is fat, DWL triangle is thin.
Cigarette example: \(\varepsilon_D = -0.5\), \(\varepsilon_S = 1.5\), \(t = \$2\). Buyers absorb \(\mathbf{75\%}\) → \(\Delta p_c = +\$1.50\).

Elastic demand — strong response

  • Luxury yachts (\(\varepsilon_D \approx -2\)), specific cereal brands, soda with substitutes.
  • Buyers walk away easily → tax shifts mostly to sellers / collapses the market.
  • Large \(\Delta Q\) → revenue rectangle is thin, DWL triangle is fat.
1990 U.S. luxury yacht tax: elastic demand → sales collapsed, boatbuilders absorbed it (not the rich). Repealed 1993.

Two halves of the same story. §3.5 formalizes this as the incidence formula \(dp_c/dt = \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)\): the side with more inelastic behavior bears more of the tax — because that side can't respond by walking away.

Bridge from incentives (Cobra Effect: people respond) to taxes (§3.5: HOW MUCH people respond is elasticity). Inelastic side bears more. Examples: cigarettes (inelastic — buyers eat it), yacht tax 1990 (elastic — sellers ate it; repealed). Plant the seed for the §3.5 incidence formula on the next slides.

§3.5  Tax Incidence by Total Differentiation

14 min · The wedge equation

The tax wedge

The wedge

\(p_c\) = price buyer pays
\(p_s\) = price seller receives
\(p_c - p_s = t\)

Statutory incidence (whose paycheck the deduction shows up on) is economically irrelevant.

Equilibrium with tax

Statute on sellers:
\(Q^d(p_c) = Q^s(p_c - t)\)

One equation in one unknown \(p_c\) for each \(t\). At \(t = 0\): \(p_c = p_s = p^*\).

The comparative-statics question: how do \(p_c\) and \(p_s\) move as \(t\) rises? Total differentiate.

Calculus Corner 3.2 — Deriving the incidence formula

Differentiate \(Q^d(p_c) = Q^s(p_c - t)\) w.r.t. \(t\):

\[\frac{dQ^d}{dp_c}\cdot\frac{dp_c}{dt} = \frac{dQ^s}{dp_s}\left(\frac{dp_c}{dt} - 1\right)\]

Solve for \(dp_c/dt\), then multiply num/denom by \(p^*/Q^*\) to convert slopes → elasticities:

\[\boxed{\dfrac{dp_c}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}, \qquad \dfrac{dp_s}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_D}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}}\]

\(\varepsilon_S > 0\) ⇒ \(dp_c/dt > 0\) (consumer price rises)

\(\varepsilon_D < 0\) ⇒ \(dp_s/dt < 0\) (seller price falls)

For finite nonzero elasticities: \(0 < dp_c/dt < 1\). Pass-through is strictly between 0% and 100% in the competitive linear model.

Reading the formula

\(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D\) = total responsiveness on both sides. The ratio is the fraction of market responsiveness on the supply side — the consumer's share of the wedge.

Demand inelastic (\(\varepsilon_D \to 0\)): \(dp_c/dt \to 1\). Cigarettes, eggs, insulin.

Supply inelastic (\(\varepsilon_S \to 0\)): \(dp_c/dt \to 0\). Land, sports stars, port slots.

The side that can't easily walk away bears the tax.

Walkthrough 3.4 — Tax incidence on a cigarette excise

Problem. Short-run \(\varepsilon_D = -0.5\), \(\varepsilon_S = 1.5\); state imposes \(t = \$2.00\) per pack (statute on distributors).  (a) Consumer share of the burden.  (b) Producer share + \(\Delta p_c\), \(\Delta p_s\).  (c) Swap the elasticities — what changes?

(a) Consumer share

\(\dfrac{dp_c}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}\)
\(= \dfrac{1.5}{1.5-(-0.5)} = \dfrac{1.5}{2.0}\)
\(= \mathbf{0.75}\) (75% on buyers)

(b) Producer share + \(\Delta p\)

\(|dp_s/dt| = \mathbf{0.25}\) (25% on sellers)
\(\Delta p_c = +0.75 \cdot 2 = \mathbf{+\$1.50}\)
\(\Delta p_s = -0.25 \cdot 2 = \mathbf{-\$0.50}\)
Revenue/pack: full \(\$2.00\). \(\Delta p_c + |\Delta p_s| = t\). ✓

(c) Swap: \(\varepsilon_D = -1.5\), \(\varepsilon_S = 0.5\)

Consumer share \(= 0.5/2.0 = \mathbf{0.25}\)
Producer share \(= \mathbf{0.75}\)
Shares flip. The formula tracks behavior, not the statute.

Takeaway. Inelastic demand ⇒ buyers bear most. With cigarettes' very small |\(\varepsilon_D\)|, an excise is politically effective as revenue (buyers can't walk away) and regressive as policy (smoking incidence is higher in low-income households). Application 3.2 (next slide) shows why state rates diverge by 30×.

Heads Up 3.3 — Statutory incidence is irrelevant

Whether the statute puts the tax on the buyer or the seller, the same \(dp_c/dt = \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)\) emerges from total differentiation. Mathematical identity, not coincidence.

What is conventional

The sign on \(\varepsilon_D\). If you let the absolute value slip and write \(\varepsilon_S + |\varepsilon_D|\) in the denominator, the formula still gives the right answer. Mix the two conventions and you'll compute negative shares.

§3.6  Deadweight Loss as a Welfare Integral

10 min · The triangle in dollars

DWL = the integral of the wedge

\[\text{DWL} = \int_{Q_t}^{Q^*}\big[\,p^d(q) - p^s(q)\,\big]\,dq\]

Geometry

Area between inverse demand and inverse supply, between post-tax \(Q_t\) and pre-tax \(Q^*\).

Economics

Value of trades that didn't happen — not transferred, just gone.

Linear special case: \[\text{DWL} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot t \cdot \Delta Q\] — the Harberger triangle (1964 AER).

Calculus Corner 3.3 — Integral → triangle for linear S&D

Claim: \(\displaystyle\int_{Q_t}^{Q^*}[p^d(q) - p^s(q)]\,dq = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot t \cdot \Delta Q\) — exactly, not as approximation, for linear demand and supply.

The two endpoints

At \(q = Q_t\): \(p^d(Q_t) - p^s(Q_t) = p_c - p_s = \mathbf{t}\)
At \(q = Q^*\): \(p^* - p^* = \mathbf{0}\)

Why the triangle is exact

\(p^d(q)\) and \(p^s(q)\) are linear in \(q\), so their difference is linear too. A linear function taking value \(t\) at one endpoint and \(0\) at the other integrates to \(\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot t \cdot \Delta Q\) — the area of a right triangle.

Harberger 1964: for nonlinear S&D, a second-order Taylor expansion preserves the triangle as the leading term as \(t \to 0\). For large wedges or sharply curved demand, integrate directly.

Sidebar — Arnold Harberger (b. 1924) and "The Measurement of Waste"

Emeritus professor, U. Chicago. His 1964 AER paper "The Measurement of Waste" turned the welfare-loss triangle from a blackboard cartoon into a measurable dollar quantity.

Method: Taylor-expand the surplus integral around the pre-tax equilibrium → \(\text{DWL} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}t\Delta Q\). First applied to U.S. corporate-income taxation; generalized immediately to any wedge: tax, subsidy, tariff, price control.

Before 1964

“Taxes are inefficient.” Qualitative; rhetorical.

After 1964

Welfare loss is a number in dollars, comparable across policies. The term Harberger triangle becomes universal.

Forward reference: Frank Ramsey (1927) took the same logic the other way — the inverse-elasticity rule for optimal commodity taxation. Ch 17 picks this up.

§3.7  Pass-Through Regimes

8 min · The 0–100% benchmark and what breaks it

Three pass-through regimes

\(0 < dp_c/dt < 1\)

Sub-100%

Linear competitive baseline. Both finite elasticities.

\(dp_c/dt = 1\)

Full 100%

Perfectly elastic supply (\(\varepsilon_S \to \infty\)). Small open economy on world market.

\(dp_c/dt > 1\)

Over-shifting

Cannot arise in competitive linear model. Fingerprint of market power.

§3.8–3.9  Price Ceilings & Floors

15 min · Same integrals, opposite sides

Welfare under a binding ceiling \(\bar p < p^*\)

Quantity traded \(Q_s = (\bar p - B)/b\) — supply binds.

Assume efficient rationing (highest-WTP buyers get the units):

\(\text{CS}_{\text{ceil}} = \int_0^{Q_s}[p^d(q) - \bar p]\,dq\)
\(\text{PS}_{\text{ceil}} = \int_0^{Q_s}[\bar p - p^s(q)]\,dq\)

Three effects: transfer from sellers to inframarginal buyers + shortage + DWL on units that no longer trade.

Heads Up 3.5 — Min wage in monopsony can raise employment

The competitive welfare story assumes both sides are price-takers. In a monopsony (single large employer in a small geography), a binding minimum wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage raises both employment and wages.

Why this matters here: the Berkeley null-employment finding for California fast-food may reflect monopsony power in local labor markets — not a rejection of the welfare framework.

Ch 19 derives this formally as the labor-market mirror of monopoly (Ch 11).

Poll #4: Minimum-wage trade-off

Which conclusion follows most directly from the elasticity framework alone (no monopsony)?

A) Raising the minimum wage always reduces welfare
B) DWL is larger when labor demand is more elastic
C) Workers always benefit from a higher minimum wage
D) Employment effects are independent of elasticities

60 sec to vote

Answer: B — DWL grows with \(|\varepsilon_{L,d}|\)

The integral grows with \(\Delta L\), and \(\Delta L\) grows with the elasticity of demand. Trap (A): ignores the transfer to hired workers. Trap (C): the unemployed don't benefit. Trap (D): elasticities are the drivers in the formula.

Key Takeaways

  1. Elasticity is a point concept. \(\varepsilon = (dQ/dp)(p/Q)\). On linear demand, slope is constant but elasticity varies.
  2. Revenue is maximized at \(\varepsilon_D = -1\). Inelastic zone: raise the price. Elastic: lower it.
  3. Tax incidence: \(dp_c/dt = \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)\). Statutory split is irrelevant; the inelastic side bears more.
  4. DWL = the integral. Linear case: Harberger triangle \(\tfrac{1}{2}t\Delta Q\), grows with \(t^2\).
  5. Pass-through in competitive linear markets is in \([0,1]\). Outside that range ⇒ market power.
  6. Price ceilings & floors use the same integrals: transfer + DWL + shortage/unemployment.
