\[ \varepsilon_D = \frac{\text{quantity reaction}}{\text{price reaction}} = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta p} \]

§3.1  From Midpoint to Point Elasticity

Promoting elasticity from a percent-change to a derivative

The point-elasticity formula

\[\varepsilon_D = \frac{dQ}{dp} \cdot \frac{p}{Q}.\]

Two pieces

\(dQ/dp\) is the quantity response — units per \$1. We draw \(p\) up the vertical axis, so this is the reciprocal of the slope you see, not the slope itself. \(p/Q\) rescales it into percentage units.

Sign matters

Law of demand: \(dQ/dp < 0\), so \(\varepsilon_D < 0\). Micro Theory keeps the sign. No absolute-value hiding.

Same vocabulary: \(|\varepsilon|>1\) elastic · \(|\varepsilon|<1\) inelastic · \(|\varepsilon|=1\) unit elastic · \(0\) perfectly inelastic · \(\infty\) perfectly elastic.

Units discipline. \(\varepsilon_D\) is unit-free — but \(dQ/dp\) and \(Q\) are not. Every demand function in this chapter is printed with its units, and you should read the numbers out in full: “two hundred forty thousand entries a day,” never “240.” Write \(240\) where the curve means \(240{,}000\) and \(\varepsilon=-0.25\) becomes \(-250\) — off by a factor of a thousand, with nothing in the algebra to catch it.

Farewell to the Midpoint Method

You spent a chapter of Principles on this. Two minutes to retire it — and to say exactly what replaces it.

What Principles did

\[\varepsilon^{\text{arc}}_D=\frac{(Q_2-Q_1)\big/\overline{Q}}{(p_2-p_1)\big/\overline{p}}, \quad \overline{Q}=\frac{Q_1+Q_2}{2}\]

Its formal name is arc elasticity. Dividing by the midpoint rather than the starting value is what makes the answer come out the same going up as coming down — the one real reason to teach it.

It is a finite-difference approximation to the point elasticity somewhere between the two observations. Shrink the interval and it becomes the derivative.

Why one derivative replaces it

Hand us a calibrated curve \(Q^d(p)\) and there is nothing to average. Differentiate once, evaluate anywhere: \(\varepsilon_D=\dfrac{dQ}{dp}\dfrac{p}{Q}\). One line, exact, and it works when demand is not a straight line — which arc cannot do at all.

Where arc still earns its keep: two raw scanner observations and no model. No function, nothing to differentiate — average the endpoints. That is the only case.

For the record, on the toll demand below: arc across \(\$9\to\$12\) is \(-0.212\), point at \(\$12\) is \(-0.250\). Same slope \(-5{,}000\) both times — the only difference is which \((p,Q)\) you rescale by. That is the whole story, and it is why we stop computing the first one.

From here on: point elasticity only. If you have a function, differentiate it. That sentence is most of the reason this course is taught with calculus.

Walkthrough 3.1 — Point Elasticity at the \$12 Toll

Problem. NYC congestion pricing: \(Q^d(P)=300{,}000-5{,}000P\)  —  \(Q\) in entries per day, \(P\) in dollars per entry. Find \(\varepsilon_D\) at \(P=\$12\).

Three steps — every elasticity in this course

1. Differentiate

\(\dfrac{dQ^d}{dP}=\mathbf{-5{,}000}\) entries per day, per \(\$1\) of toll — constant, because demand is linear.

2. Quantity at that price

\(Q^d(12)=300{,}000-5{,}000(12)=\mathbf{240{,}000}\) entries per day — two hundred forty thousand, not \(240\).

3. Rescale by \(P/Q\)

\(\varepsilon_D=-5{,}000\cdot\dfrac{12}{240{,}000}=\mathbf{-0.250}\)

Work it first. Three minutes on your own, then we take the answer from the room — before the reveal.

3 min, then poll the room

\(|\varepsilon_D|=0.25<1\): inelastic. Commuters cannot easily stop driving into Manhattan, which is why the toll raises steady revenue and why traffic fell only modestly.

Two traps. Units: \(240\) instead of \(240{,}000\) gives \(\varepsilon=-250\) — if your answer is not between \(0\) and a few, you dropped a thousand. Pairing: the elasticity at \(\$12\) must use \(Q(12)\). Pairing \(\$12\) with \(Q(9)=255{,}000\) mixes two different points on the curve.

§3.2–3.3  Linear Demand Zones & Revenue

14 min · Where on the curve are you?

Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve

Nothing new needed — just the point-elasticity formula, read carefully.

\(\varepsilon_D=\underbrace{\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}}_{\text{fixed}}\cdot\underbrace{\dfrac{p}{Q^d}}_{\text{moves}}\)

For \(Q^d=a-bp\), \(dQ^d/dp=-b\). That is a constant — it is the same at every price on the curve.

So the only thing that changes as you walk along the line is the \((p,Q)\) pair:

\(\varepsilon_D=\dfrac{-bp}{a-bp}\)

\(p \to 0\): \(p/Q\to0\), so \(\varepsilon_D \to 0\) — perfectly inelastic at the \(Q\)-intercept

\(p \to a/b\): \(Q\to0\), so \(\varepsilon_D \to -\infty\) — perfectly elastic at the \(p\)-intercept

\(p = a/(2b)\): \(\varepsilon_D = -1\) (unit elastic, midpoint) — the revenue-maximising price, derived in §3.3. Not the profit-maximising one.

Figure 3.2 — Walk down the curve: \(dQ/dp\) never moves, \(\varepsilon_D\) does

Drag the price: the curve never bends, yet \(\varepsilon_D\) runs from \(0\) to \(-\infty\).

SAY: On a straight line the slope never changes. Only P over Q moves, so elasticity runs from zero to minus infinity.

BACKGROUND:

  • Do not introduce anything new here - this whole slide is the point-elasticity formula read carefully.
  • Epsilon is a product of two factors; on a straight line the first one is nailed down at -b, so every bit of the variation has to come from p over Q.
  • Walk the slider from the bottom of the curve to the top and narrate it: at the quantity intercept p is nearly zero so p/Q is nearly zero and demand is perfectly inelastic; at the price intercept Q is nearly zero so p/Q blows up and demand is perfectly elastic; exactly halfway the two effects balance and epsilon is -1.
  • That midpoint is the revenue-maximising price, which is section 3.3 two slides on.
  • Heads Up 3.2 next is the same lesson from the other side - there the price is held fixed and the curve changes.

TIME: ~2 min. Walk the slider from bottom to top.

Revenue and the unit-elasticity rule

Revenue is price times quantity — a product, so differentiate it as one. Everything on this slide is about a revenue-maximiser.

1. Product rule on \(R(p)=p\cdot Q^d(p)\):
\(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p) + p\,\dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\)

2. Factor out \(Q^d\):
\(= Q^d(p)\left[\,1 + \dfrac{dQ^d}{dp}\dfrac{p}{Q^d}\,\right]\)

3. That bracket is \(\varepsilon_D\):

\(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p)\,[\,1+\varepsilon_D\,]\)

\(Q^d>0\) always — so the bracket alone decides the sign.

So where you sit decides what to do

Inelastic

\(\varepsilon_D>-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp>0\) — raise the price

Unit elastic

\(\varepsilon_D=-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp=0\) — revenue peaks

Elastic

\(\varepsilon_D<-1\) ⇒ \(dR/dp<0\) — lower the price

Laffer logic for an excise tax: revenue climbs with the rate only until the market turns elastic — past that, a higher rate collects less.

Say it precisely

A revenue-maximiser stops at \(\varepsilon_D=-1\). A profit-maximiser with positive marginal cost stops earlier, in the elastic region \(|\varepsilon_D|>1\), where marginal revenue is still positive and can equal \(MC\). Unit elasticity is the profit rule only in the special case \(MC=0\). Chs 9 and 11 do the profit version properly.

SAY: Revenue rises with price while demand is inelastic, falls once it is elastic, and peaks at unit elastic.

BACKGROUND:

  • Merged from two slides that ran in the wrong order - the old pair showed the result first and then said "press down for the derivation".
  • Derive, then read the regimes off it.
  • Eight steps, but they are two runs of four: the derivation (1-4), then the three zones with their heading (5-7) and the tax note (8).
  • Move briskly through the derivation.
  • The payoff is step 4: Q is always positive, so the bracket alone carries the sign, which is why the whole rule reduces to where epsilon sits relative to -1.
  • Tie back to Poll #1 two slides earlier - that was the unit-elastic point on this same curve, and this is why it is also the revenue-maximising price.
  • A lost less-than sign in the old derivation slide rendered as "|eps| 1"; fixed here.
  • Fragment 9 is the key correction and it belongs in the OPENING sentence of the slide, not eighty minutes later: say "a revenue-maximiser will stop at unit elastic" every time, because the unqualified version is the one students write down. \$30 is what a revenue-maximiser would charge, and it lands exactly on epsilon = -1.
  • The MTA is NOT a revenue-maximiser: it set \$9 to hit a revenue target, a traffic target and a political limit at once (Ch 1 Poll #2), so \$9 is a policy price, not a market equilibrium.
  • A private firm with positive marginal cost stops in the elastic zone, where |epsilon| is above 1.
  • If you want one line for why: MR = 0 at unit elasticity, and no firm paying a positive MC wants MR = 0.

Elasticities — Terminology

\(\displaystyle E^D = \frac{\%\Delta Q^D}{\%\Delta P}\)

  • Inelastic: \(0 < |E^D| < 1\)
  • Unit elastic: \(|E^D| = 1\)
  • Elastic: \(|E^D| > 1\)
  • Perfectly elastic: \(|E^D| = \infty\)
  • Perfectly inelastic: \(|E^D| = 0\)

Important: Elasticities do not have units attached.

  • Allows comparison across goods and markets.
  • Same classifications apply to supply (\(E^S\)).

Review · Principles of Microeconomics

Application — Innovation, Substitutes and Market Power

Consumer and producer surplus are how we measure the value a new product creates — e.g., a solar-powered car. The CS triangle is the welfare gain to buyers.

Thought experiment. Hold \(P^*\) and \(Q^*\) fixed. Draw two demand curves with different slopes, both through E. Which generates more CS?

1. Steep demand — inelastic

\(|\varepsilon_D| < 1\). Some inframarginal buyers will pay far above \(P^*\) ⇒ tall CS triangle. The profile of a brand-new product with few substitutes.

2. Shallow demand — elastic

\(|\varepsilon_D| > 1\). Substitutes are easy; buyers' WTP clusters near \(P^*\) ⇒ short CS triangle. The profile of a mature market.

Figure 3.3 — Consumer Surplus & Elasticity of Demand

Blue = a new innovative product (few substitutes ⇒ steep). Red = a mature market (many substitutes ⇒ flat). As rivals arrive, demand flattens blue→red and CS shrinks.

Role of elasticity. At the same \((P^*, Q^*)\), more inelastic demand ⇒ more CS. Elasticity is the geometric translator between “steepness” and “welfare.”

Role of innovation

New products whose buyers have highly varied WTP (insulin pumps, iPhones, EVs) face steep demand ⇒ a large CS triangle ⇒ big welfare gains.

Heads up — the word runs the other way

A dozen firms make eyeglasses, so the demand any one faces is elastic — nudge price up and buyers walk. Patent the contact lens and the substitutes are gone, so the demand you face becomes LESS elastic, not more. That is what market power is (Chs 9, 11–12).

SAY: A patent removes substitutes, so the demand you face gets LESS elastic. That is market power.

BACKGROUND:

  • 4 fragments: (1) steep demand + tall CS + 'D_steep (inelastic)' label at bottom-right;
  • (2) shallow demand + short CS + 'D_shallow (elastic)' label;
  • (3) Role of elasticity (full-width banner under the grid);
  • (4) Role of innovation (full-width banner).
  • Speak: "same P*, Q* — but the steeper curve hides inframarginal buyers willing to pay MUCH more.
  • Their excess WTP is consumer surplus." Pedagogical link: when we measure the welfare gain of a new technology, we INTEGRATE under the new demand curve above the new price — i.e., the CS triangle.
  • Fragment 5 is the key correction, and it is worth the extra thirty seconds because it is load-bearing for Chs 9 and 11-12: a patent makes the demand you face LESS elastic, which is precisely where market power comes from.
  • The inverted version ("now consumers are more elastic, so you have more market power") is an easy slip because every other sentence in the argument is right - the whole error is the one word.
  • Say the correction out loud at the top of the meeting as well, not only here; a student who wrote the backwards version down will not un-write it from a slide fix.
  • Sanity anchor to offer: perfectly elastic demand means you have NO pricing discretion at all, so more elastic can never mean more power.

Income elasticity

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} = \dfrac{\partial Q^d_i}{\partial I} \cdot \dfrac{I}{Q^d_i}\)  —  the sign classifies the good, the size ranks it

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} > 0\) — Normal

• Necessity: \(0 < \varepsilon < 1\) (food)
• Luxury: \(\varepsilon > 1\) (restaurant meals)

Restaurant meals, \(\varepsilon_I \approx 1.0\) — right on the luxury edge.

\(\varepsilon_{I,i} < 0\) — Inferior

Quantity falls as income rises — buyers substitute toward something better.

MBTA Orange Line — as income rises, riders switch to cars.

U.S. food: \(\varepsilon_I \approx 0.1\)–\(0.3\). Bus travel can flip from inferior to normal at different income levels.

SAY: The sign says normal or inferior; the size says necessity or luxury.

BACKGROUND:

  • Same partial-derivative template as own-price elasticity, just with respect to income.
  • The split to hammer: the SIGN says normal or inferior, the SIZE says necessity or luxury - two different questions off one number.
  • Photos are the Principles anchors: fine dining for the luxury edge, and the Orange Line as the local Boston example students actually ride.
  • Bus travel is the honest complication - it is inferior over some income ranges and normal over others, so the label belongs to a range, not to the good forever.

Cross-price elasticity

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} = \dfrac{\partial Q^d_i}{\partial p_j} \cdot \dfrac{p_j}{Q^d_i}\)  —  does \(i\) sell more or less when \(j\) gets pricier?

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} > 0\) — Substitutes

\(j\) gets pricier, buyers move to \(i\). Tea/coffee, iPhone/Galaxy, ride-hail/rental.

Raise the price of one console, buyers shift to the other.

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} < 0\) — Complements

\(j\) gets pricier, demand for \(i\) falls too. Printer/ink, console/games.

Console and cartridge — price up on one, demand down on both.

\(\varepsilon_{i,j}=0\) — unrelated goods, no cross-relationship at all.  •  Antitrust use: a large positive \(\varepsilon_{i,j}\) puts two products in the same relevant market for merger analysis.

SAY: Positive means substitutes, negative means complements, zero means unrelated.

BACKGROUND:

  • The sign rule is the whole lesson: positive means substitutes, negative means complements, zero means unrelated.
  • The console pair is worth dwelling on because both photos are the same console - what changes is what it is sitting next to, which is the point: the relationship lives between the two goods, not inside either one.
  • Antitrust is the live application - a large positive cross-price elasticity is how regulators argue two products compete in the same market, and it comes back in Ch 12.

§3.5  Tax Incidence — Who Actually Pays?

14 min · The wedge equation

SAY: Who writes the check to the government does not decide who pays the tax. ⏸ 3 s.

TIME: 20 s: read the title and move on.

A tax is an incentive — and elasticity is the response

A per-unit tax \(t\) raises what buyers pay to \(p_c\) and cuts what sellers keep to \(p_s\). Who moves?

Inelastic — weak response

Eggs \(-0.23\), cigarettes \(-0.4\), insulin \(\approx 0\).
Buyers cannot walk away → \(p_c\) rises by nearly all of \(t\).
Fat revenue, thin DWL.

Elastic — strong response

Luxury yachts \(-2\), one cereal brand, soda with substitutes.
Buyers leave → the tax lands on sellers.
Thin revenue, fat DWL.

Two halves of one story — and §3.5 turns it into a formula.

SAY: A tax is just a price change, so everything you know about elasticity applies: the side that cannot walk away pays. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY:

  • “We pay more of the tax because we are more sensitive.” (Backwards: the LESS elastic side pays more.)
  • “Taxing an elastic good raises more money.” (It raises less, because buyers leave.)

BACKGROUND:

  • The sentence to land is that a tax is just a price change, so everything students already know about elasticity applies unchanged.
  • Voice the trade-off explicitly: a government that wants REVENUE taxes inelastic things (cigarettes, fuel, salt historically); a government that wants BEHAVIOUR CHANGE taxes elastic things - but then collects little, which is the point. §3.5 formalises this as dpc/dt = eS/(eS - eD), and the two columns here are just that formula's two extremes.

IF ASKED:

  • “Then why tax soda at all?” → There the goal is behaviour change, not revenue, and collecting little is the point.
  • “Anything else” → Good question. I'll confirm and open next class with it.

The tax wedge

The wedge

\(p_c\) = price buyer pays
\(p_s\) = price seller receives
\(p_c - p_s = t\)

Statutory incidence (whose paycheck the deduction shows up on) is economically irrelevant.

Equilibrium with tax

Statute on sellers:
\(Q^d(p_c) = Q^s(p_c - t)\)

One equation in one unknown \(p_c\) for each \(t\). At \(t = 0\): \(p_c = p_s = p^*\).

The comparative-statics question: how do \(p_c\) and \(p_s\) move as \(t\) rises? Total differentiate.

Calculus Corner 3.2 — Deriving the incidence formula

Differentiate \(Q^d(p_c) = Q^s(p_c - t)\) w.r.t. \(t\). Both sides need the chain rule — \(p_c\) is itself a function of \(t\):

\[\frac{dQ^d}{dp_c}\cdot\frac{dp_c}{dt} = \frac{dQ^s}{dp_s}\left(\frac{dp_c}{dt} - 1\right)\]

Solve for \(dp_c/dt\), then multiply num/denom by \(p^*/Q^*\) to turn the derivatives into elasticities:

\[\boxed{\dfrac{dp_c}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}, \qquad \dfrac{dp_s}{dt} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_D}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D}}\]

\(\varepsilon_S > 0\) ⇒ \(dp_c/dt > 0\) (consumer price rises)

\(\varepsilon_D < 0\) ⇒ \(dp_s/dt < 0\) (seller price falls)

For finite nonzero elasticities: \(0 < dp_c/dt < 1\). Pass-through is strictly between 0% and 100% in the competitive linear model.

First chain rule of the course. Math Refresher →

Reading the formula

\(\dfrac{dp_c}{dt}=\dfrac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S-\varepsilon_D}\) — a ratio of one side's responsiveness to the total.

Demand inelastic

\(\varepsilon_D\to 0 \Rightarrow dp_c/dt\to 1\)
Buyers pay it all. Cigarettes, eggs, insulin.

Supply inelastic

\(\varepsilon_S\to 0 \Rightarrow dp_c/dt\to 0\)
Sellers eat it. Land, port slots, sports stars.

The side that cannot walk away bears the tax.

SAY: The less elastic side bears more of the tax. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: “We pay more of the tax because we are more sensitive.” (Backwards. Eggs: buyers −1/3, sellers +2/3, so buyers pay 2/3.)

BACKGROUND:

  • The denominator is total responsiveness across both sides; the numerator is the seller's share of it.
  • So the formula is just "how much of the combined flexibility sits on the other side".
  • Land is the cleanest inelastic-supply case - you cannot make more of it, so a tax on land value falls entirely on landowners, which is the whole Georgist argument.
  • Sanity rule worth repeating from Heads Up 3.1: shares must land in [0,1]; anything outside means a sign slip.

IF ASKED:

  • “What if my share comes out above 1?” → A sign slip: εD is negative, so εS − εD adds two positives. Shares land in [0, 1].
  • “Who pays a tax on land?” → The landowners, all of it: nobody can make more land.
  • “Anything else” → Good question. I'll confirm and open next class with it.

Walkthrough 3.3 — the rule works without a tax

Supply is twice as elastic as demand.

• sellers bring back \(10\times 2 = \mathbf{20}\)
10 + 20 = 30. The quantity piece is the buyers' piece.

Each piece is its own elasticity's share:
quantity \(= \dfrac{|\varepsilon_D|}{|\varepsilon_D|+\varepsilon_S} = \dfrac{1/3}{1} = \mathbf{\tfrac13}\) of 30 \(= 10\)  ·  price-recovered \(= \mathbf{\tfrac23}\) of 30 \(= 20\).
Ch 2 re-solved the system and got the same \(\Delta p=+\$2\), \(\Delta Q=-10\).

Careful — this split is not the tax split. Here it is price vs quantity. On the next slide it is buyer vs seller.

Heads Up 3.3 — Statutory incidence is irrelevant

Whether the statute puts the tax on the buyer or the seller, the same \(dp_c/dt = \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)\) emerges from total differentiation. Mathematical identity, not coincidence.

The sign on \(\varepsilon_D\). If you let the absolute value slip and write \(\varepsilon_S + |\varepsilon_D|\) in the denominator, the formula still gives the right answer. Mix the two conventions and you'll compute negative shares.

§3.6  Deadweight Loss as a Welfare Integral

10 min · The triangle in dollars

Why the integral collapses to a triangle

Both curves are straight lines, so the gap between them is a straight line too — and the area under a straight line is a triangle.

\[\int_{Q_t}^{Q^*}\!\big[p^d-p^s\big]dq=\tfrac12\,t\,\Delta Q\]

The gap is \(\mathbf{t}\) at \(Q_t\) and \(\mathbf{0}\) at \(Q^*\) — height \(t\), base \(\Delta Q\).

Curved demand? The triangle is still a good approximation — Harberger, 1964.

SAY: With straight lines the gap runs from t down to zero, so deadweight loss is a triangle: one half t times delta-Q.

BACKGROUND:

  • The point is to retire the integral, not to practise it: for linear supply and demand the triangle is exact, so students should always reach for geometry.
  • Endpoints are the argument - at the taxed quantity the vertical gap between inverse demand and inverse supply is exactly t, and at the untaxed equilibrium it is zero, so a linear function running from t down to 0 over a base of delta-Q has area one-half t delta-Q.
  • Harberger's 1964 result is the footnote: for nonlinear curves a second-order Taylor expansion preserves the triangle to first approximation, which is why the formula survives well outside the linear case.

TIME: ~1.5 min.

§3.7  Pass-Through Regimes

8 min · The 0–100% benchmark and what breaks it

Three pass-through regimes

\(0 < dp_c/dt < 1\)

Sub-100%

Linear competitive baseline. Both finite elasticities.

\(dp_c/dt = 1\)

Full 100%

Perfectly elastic supply (\(\varepsilon_S \to \infty\)). Small open economy on world market.

\(dp_c/dt > 1\)

Over-shifting

Cannot arise in competitive linear model. Fingerprint of market power.

Pass-through is a testable empirical quantity. When the data show \(> 100\%\), Ch 11 (monopoly) gives the formula that explains why.

Theory & Data 3.2 — why two soda taxes gave two answers

Philadelphia 1.5¢/oz ≈ 97% Chain groceries that price a whole city at once. Almost the entire tax reached the shelf.
Boulder 2¢/oz ≈ 50% to 75% Smaller market, easy to shop outside it — and the two numbers come from two different data sources.

The number is not the theory. Pass-through is \(\varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S-\varepsilon_D)\) in both cities. What differs is the market — and what you measured it with.

Boulder's 50% is scanner data; 75% is a store audit. Same tax, same year, different instrument. When you read an estimate, ask what produced it.

Philadelphia: Seiler, Tuchman & Yao (2021). Boulder: Cawley et al. (2021).

SAY: Pass-through is the same formula in both cities. The numbers differ because the markets differ — and because the two Boulder numbers come from two different data sources. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: One of these studies is wrong.  |  Philadelphia proves pass-through is 100%.

BACKGROUND:

  • Laid out as a comparison, because four sentences of prose hide what is being compared.
  • Rows now line up city, tax, estimate, mechanism, and the two Boulder figures are explicitly labelled as two instruments rather than a contradiction.

  • “Why would chains pass more through?” → Zone pricing: one price for the whole city, so the tax cannot be absorbed store by store.
  • “Anything else” → Good question. I'll confirm and open next class with it.

Poll #3: a tax of \$1, a price rise of \$1.20

A city puts a \$1 per-unit tax on sellers. Retail prices rise by \$1.20. Which single explanation fits?

A) Demand is perfectly inelastic
B) Sellers have market power
C) The tax was really collected from buyers
D) Supply is perfectly elastic

Vote 60s

SAY: A one-dollar tax, a one-twenty price rise. Only one of these four can do that. ⏸ 5 s, then vote.

DON'T SAY: The answer before the count is in.  |  That over-shifting is impossible.

BACKGROUND:

  • The old version asked what pass-through above 1.0 "most likely indicates" with "both B and C" as the key, which let students pick the compound answer by pattern.
  • This version has one answer and makes A and D do real work: both are the limiting cases that give EXACTLY full pass-through, so a student who thinks "buyers can't escape, so they pay more than all of it" gets caught.

IF ASKED:

  • “Isn't A right? Buyers can't walk away.” → Perfectly inelastic demand gives exactly 100%, not more. That is the trap.
  • “Anything else” → Good question. I'll confirm and open next class with it.

TIME: 2 min with the reveal. Cut the whole of §3.7 if you are past minute 20.

Answer: B — over-shifting needs market power

Why B. A firm with market power charges a markup over cost. Raise its cost by \$1 and the markup is applied to the higher cost too — so the price can move by more than the tax.

Why not A or D. Both are the limiting cases of our formula: \(\varepsilon_D\to 0\) or \(\varepsilon_S\to\infty\) gives \(dp_c/dt = 1\) — exactly \$1, never \$1.20. In competition the wedge splits, so pass-through is capped at 100%.

It happens. The 2018 washing-machine tariff: pass-through estimated at 108–225%. Washers rose about \$86 — and dryers, which were never tariffed, rose about \$92, because the pair is priced together (Flaaen, Hortaçsu & Tintelnot, AER 2020).

European wine, 25% tariff of 2019–21: retail rose only 6.9%, yet consumers paid about \$1.59 a bottle against \$1.19 of tariff — each link in the chain marks up the higher cost (Flaaen, Hortaçsu, Tintelnot, Urdaneta & Xu).

Trap C. Statutory incidence again: who hands over the money cannot change the price by a cent. Heads Up 3.3.

Over-shifting is a fingerprint of market power, not a puzzle about taxes. It is the first time this term the competitive assumption visibly fails — Ch 11 derives the monopolist's pass-through properly.

SAY: Over-shifting is a fingerprint of market power, not a puzzle about taxes. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: The competitive model is wrong. (It is the right model for competitive markets; this is evidence you are not in one.)

IF ASKED:

  • “Have we seen this in real data?” → Several 2025 tariff categories showed retail rises above the tariff, which points at concentrated sellers rather than at the tariff. Read it off the slide, do not quote a figure from memory.
  • “Anything else” → Good question. I'll confirm and open next class with it.

Key Takeaways

Elasticity is a point, not a slope. \(\varepsilon=(dQ/dp)(p/Q)\) — it varies along a straight line.

Revenue peaks at \(\varepsilon_D=-1\). Inelastic → raise price. Elastic → cut it. A profit-maximiser with \(MC>0\) stops earlier, still elastic.

Whoever can walk away, walks away. Who writes the cheque is irrelevant.

DWL grows with \(t^{2}\). Double the tax, quadruple the damage.

Ch 2's grocery manager could say which way price would move. You can now say by how much, and who pays for it.

SAY: Elasticity is a point, revenue peaks at minus one, and whoever can walk away pays less of the tax.

BACKGROUND:

  • Four takeaways, one per objective.
  • The price-ceiling line is gone with §3.8-3.9.
  • Pass-through is worth a spoken addition if the poll went well: in competitive linear markets it sits in [0,1], and anything outside that range is a fingerprint of market power, which is where Ch 11 picks up.
