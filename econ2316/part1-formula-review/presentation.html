The calculus behind Chapters 1–3 — every key equation, in plain English

Dr. Richeng Piao • Northeastern University

First: the notation isn’t the enemy

Four symbols do almost all the work in Chapters 1–3. Here is what each one is really saying.

\( \dfrac{dC}{dq} \)

A derivative. Just a rate of change — the slope of a curve, “what the next unit does.”

\( \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial p} \)

A partial derivative. Same idea — but you change only this one variable and hold everything else still.

\( \displaystyle\int_0^{Q} \square \;\, dq \)

An integral. Add up infinitely many thin slices — it measures an area, or a running total.

\( \max f \ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ g \le 0 \)

An optimization. Find the biggest value of your goal while staying inside a limit you can’t break.

Every formula in this deck is built from these four moves. Learn the moves, and the economics reads itself.

Calculus tool 1 of 5

Derivatives = Marginal Thinking

A derivative is a slope. In economics, that slope is the “next-unit” effect.

Marginal means “the next unit” — and that is a slope

Derivatives

Ch 1 • §1.3
\[ \text{marginal} \;=\; \frac{d(\text{total})}{d(\text{quantity})} \]

In plain English

Principles compared things in clunky one-unit steps. Intermediate micro uses the derivative — the exact slope of the total curve at a point. Same idea, sharper tool: the marginal value is just how fast the total is changing right now.

Shrink a one-unit step until it is infinitely small, and the step becomes the tangent slope. That is the whole move.

Marginal cost — and its siblings

Derivatives

Ch 1 • §1.3
\[ MC(q) = \frac{dC(q)}{dq} \]

In plain English

Marginal cost is the slope of the total-cost curve — how many more dollars the next unit adds. The “\(d/dq\)” is not a new monster; it just means “rate of change.”

Swap in revenue or benefit and the template is identical: \( MR = dR/dq \), \( MB = dB/dq \). One idea, three names.

Calculus tool 2 of 5

Optimization = The Best Choice Under a Limit

Set the slope to zero — you are at the top of the hill.

The one problem the whole book solves

Optimization

Ch 1 • §1.1
\[ \max_{x \in X} \; f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g(x) \le 0 \]

In plain English

Pick the option that makes your goal \(f\) as big as possible, given a limit \(g\) you cannot break. Swap in the labels — utility and a budget, profit and a production limit, welfare and a pollution cap — and you have the entire book.

Every model from Chapter 4 onward is this one template, with the names changed.

Finding the top: first- and second-order conditions

Optimization

Ch 1 • §1.3

First-order condition

\[ f'(x^{*}) = 0 \]

Second-order condition (max)

\[ f''(x^{*}) \le 0 \]

In plain English

At the top of a smooth hill the ground is flat — so the best choice sits where the derivative equals zero (FOC). The second derivative just confirms you found a peak, not a valley: negative means the curve bends downward, so \(x^{*}\) is a maximum.

Before the next tools — the setup

The Algebra of a Market

Demand, supply, equilibrium — written as equations the calculus can act on.

Demand and supply, promoted to functions

Foundations

Ch 2 • §2.1
\[ Q^d(p) = a - bp \qquad\qquad Q^s(p) = c + dp \]

In plain English

A graph tells you a direction; a function gives you a number. Demand falls as price rises (the \(-b\) slope); supply rises with price (the \(+d\) slope). Writing them as functions is what lets every tool ahead actually compute.

This is not new economics — it is the supply-and-demand picture from Principles, rewritten so you can solve it.

Equilibrium: solved, not eyeballed

Foundations

Ch 2 • §2.2
\[ Q^d(p^{*}) = Q^s(p^{*}) \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p^{*} = \frac{a-c}{b+d}, \quad Q^{*} = \frac{ad+bc}{b+d} \]

In plain English

Equilibrium is where buyers’ and sellers’ plans match — set demand equal to supply and solve. The price comes out higher when buyers want more (bigger \(a\)) and lower when sellers offer more (bigger \(c\)).

Notice the \(b+d\) in the denominator. It returns in comparative statics and tax incidence — the same arithmetic, three times.

Calculus tool 3 of 5

Partial Derivatives & Elasticity

Change one thing, freeze the rest — then rescale the slope into percentages.

Movement along vs. shift — two different derivatives

Partial derivatives

Ch 2 • §2.5
\[ \frac{\partial Q^d}{\partial p} \]

Movement along the curve

\[ \frac{\partial Q^d}{\partial \alpha} \]

Shift of the whole curve

In plain English

The curved-d \((\partial)\) means “change just this one thing, freeze the rest.” Change price and you slide along the demand curve. Change a shifter \(\alpha\) — income, tastes — and the whole curve moves. Graphs blur these together; the notation forces you to say which you mean.

Elasticity — a slope rescaled into percentages

Elasticity

Ch 3 • §3.1
\[ \varepsilon_D = \frac{dQ}{dp}\cdot\frac{p}{Q} \]

Demand

\[ \varepsilon_S = \frac{dQ^s}{dp}\cdot\frac{p}{Q^s} \]

Supply

In plain English

Elasticity is the slope of demand (or supply), rescaled into percentages. It answers one question: if price rises 1%, how many % does quantity move? The \((p/Q)\) factor is just a unit-converter — so the answer does not depend on dollars-vs-cents or dozens-vs-units.

Reading the number — same bands for \(\varepsilon_D\) and \(\varepsilon_S\)

\(|\varepsilon| \gt 1\) — elastic. Quantity is very responsive: it moves more than 1% per 1% price change.

\(|\varepsilon| \lt 1\) — inelastic. Quantity barely responds: less than 1% per 1% price change.

\(|\varepsilon| = 1\) — unit elastic. Exactly 1% of quantity per 1% of price — the knife-edge.

The sign is automatic: \(\varepsilon_D \lt 0\) (demand slopes down) · \(\varepsilon_S \gt 0\) (supply slopes up).

Income and cross-price elasticity — same template

Elasticity

Ch 3 • §3.4
\[ \varepsilon_I = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{I}{Q} \]

Income elasticity

\[ \varepsilon_{i,j} = \frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial p_j}\cdot\frac{p_j}{Q_i} \]

Cross-price elasticity

In plain English

Exactly the same “slope \(\times\) ratio” template — now with partial derivatives, because income and other-good prices are extra variables you hold separate. For both, the sign is the whole payoff:

Income elasticity \(\varepsilon_I\) — the sign classifies the good

\(\varepsilon_I \gt 0\): normal good — demand rises with income (\(\gt 1\) luxury, \(0\)–\(1\) necessity).

\(\varepsilon_I \lt 0\): inferior good — demand falls as income rises.

Cross-price elasticity \(\varepsilon_{i,j}\) — the sign classifies the pair

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} \gt 0\): substitutes — a pricier \(j\) sends buyers to \(i\) (Coke and Pepsi).

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} \lt 0\): complements — a pricier \(j\) drags \(i\) down (printers and ink).

\(\varepsilon_{i,j} = 0\): the goods are unrelated.

Revenue and the unit-elasticity rule

Elasticity

Ch 3 • §3.3
\[ \frac{dR}{dp} = Q^d(p)\,\bigl[\,1 + \varepsilon_D\,\bigr] \]

In plain English

Take the derivative of revenue \((R = p \times Q)\) and elasticity falls right out. Revenue rises with price while demand is inelastic, and falls once demand is elastic — so revenue peaks exactly where \(\varepsilon_D = -1\).

This is the rule behind every “how high can we set the toll, the tax, the price?” question.

Calculus tool 4 of 5

Integrals = Areas of Value

Add up the gap, unit by unit — the integral measures welfare.

Consumer and producer surplus — value as an area

Integrals

Ch 2 • §2.3
\[ CS = \int_0^{Q^{*}}\!\bigl[\,p^d(q) - p^{*}\,\bigr]\,dq \]

Consumer surplus

\[ PS = \int_0^{Q^{*}}\!\bigl[\,p^{*} - p^s(q)\,\bigr]\,dq \]

Producer surplus

In plain English

Consumer surplus is the area between what buyers would have paid and what they actually paid; producer surplus is the mirror image for sellers. The \(\int\) just means “add up that gap over every unit sold.” For a straight-line demand it is literally the triangle from Principles — the integral also works when the curve bends.

Deadweight loss — the value a tax destroys

Integrals

Ch 3 • §3.6
\[ DWL = \int_{Q_t}^{Q^{*}}\!\bigl[\,p^d(q) - p^s(q)\,\bigr]\,dq \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,t\,\Delta Q \]

In plain English

A tax kills trades that would have made both sides better off. Deadweight loss adds up the value of those lost trades — the gap between what buyers would pay and what sellers would accept, over the missing quantity. For straight lines it collapses to a triangle: half of (the tax) times (the drop in quantity).

Because \(\Delta Q\) itself grows with the tax, DWL grows with \(t^{2}\) — double the tax, quadruple the loss.

Calculus tool 5 of 5

Total Differentiation = Chasing a Moving Equilibrium

When a parameter nudges the market, how far does the equilibrium slide?

Comparative statics — the equilibrium’s response

Total differentiation

Ch 2 • §2.4
\[ \frac{dp^{*}}{d\alpha} = \frac{\partial Q^s/\partial\alpha \;-\; \partial Q^d/\partial\alpha}{\partial Q^d/\partial p \;-\; \partial Q^s/\partial p} \]

linear demand & supply \(\;\Rightarrow\;\) \( \dfrac{dp^{*}}{d\alpha} = \dfrac{\phi - \psi}{b + d} \)

In plain English

Differentiate both sides of “demand \(=\) supply” and solve for how \(p^{*}\) moves when a parameter \(\alpha\) nudges the market. It tells you how far the equilibrium slides, and in which direction — without re-solving the whole system from scratch.

The denominator is always negative (demand slopes down, supply slopes up), so the direction of every shift is predictable.

Tax incidence — who actually pays?

Total differentiation

Ch 3 • §3.5
\[ \frac{dp_c}{dt} = \frac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D} \]

In plain English

This is comparative statics applied to a tax — and it is the cleanest result in Part I. Who actually bears a tax? The side that cannot walk away. The more inelastic your side of the market, the bigger the share of the tax you eat — no matter who legally writes the check.

Same machinery as the last slide: total-differentiate the market-clearing condition, this time with the tax \(t\) as the parameter.

Five tools. That is the whole toolkit.

Derivatives

A slope — the marginal, “next-unit” effect. \(MC = dC/dq\).

Optimization

Set the slope to zero — the best choice under a limit. \(\max f \text{ s.t. } g \le 0\).

Partials & elasticity

Change one thing, freeze the rest; rescale the slope into percentages. \(\varepsilon = \tfrac{dQ}{dp}\cdot\tfrac{p}{Q}\).

Integrals

Add up the gaps — surplus, welfare, deadweight loss. \(CS = \int[p^d - p^{*}]\,dq\).

Total differentiation

Track how the equilibrium moves when a parameter shifts. \(dp_c/dt = \varepsilon_S/(\varepsilon_S - \varepsilon_D)\).

Every chapter from 4 onward — consumer choice, production, market power, externalities — reuses exactly these five. The economics changes; the calculus does not.
