The budget constraint — Maya’s weekly food

The slope is the relative price: the market’s rate for trading ramen for pizza, not Maya’s taste.

Budget constraint: all combinations of two goods a consumer can afford, given income and prices.

Maya has \$16 a week. Ramen \$1 a pack, pizza \$4 a slice:

\(1 \cdot Q_R + 4 \cdot Q_P = 16\)

All ramen: 16 packs. All pizza: 4 slices.

Slope = relative price. Ramen across, pizza up: \(\text{slope} = -\dfrac{P_R}{P_P} = -\dfrac{1}{4}\). One more ramen costs ¼ slice; read the other way, one slice costs 4 ramen.

A price trade-off, not a preference trade-off. The market sets this rate; Maya cannot change it. Her own willingness to trade is her MRS (Ch 4). §5.2: the best bundle is where the two agree, \(\mathrm{MRS} = P_R/P_P\).

Scale everything, and nothing moves

Scale every price and income by the same factor and the budget set does not change.

Why: \(\alpha\) cancels

\(\alpha p_1 x_1 + \alpha p_2 x_2 = \alpha I \iff p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = I\)

Vocabulary

\(B(p, I)\) is homogeneous of degree zero in \((p, I)\), and demand inherits it: \(x_i^*(\alpha p, \alpha I) = x_i^*(p, I)\).

Why it matters

Pure inflation (every price and income up together) changes nothing real. It is also why any one price can be set to 1.

The problem

Riley spends her \$48 weekly fun budget on 🍣 sushi rolls (\(S\), \$8 each) and 🍡 mochi (\(M\), \$4 each). Her utility is Cobb-Douglas:

\[U(S, M) = \sqrt{S\,M} = S^{0.5} \, M^{0.5}\]

Marginal utilities (given):

\(MU_S = 0.5\,S^{-0.5} M^{0.5}\)  ·  \(MU_M = 0.5\,S^{0.5} M^{-0.5}\)

 What are Riley's utility-maximizing quantities \(S^*\) and \(M^*\)?

Step 1 — set MRS = price ratio

\(\mathrm{MRS}_{SM} = \dfrac{MU_S}{MU_M} = \dfrac{0.5\,S^{-0.5} M^{0.5}}{0.5\,S^{0.5} M^{-0.5}} = \dfrac{M}{S} = \dfrac{p_S}{p_M} = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2\)
  ⇒ \(M = 2S.\)

Step 2 — substitute into the budget

\(8S + 4M = 48 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; 8S + 4(2S) = 48 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; 16S = 48\)
  ⇒ \(S^{\!*} = 3\),  \(M^{\!*} = 6.\)

Formal consumer problem

The best affordable bundle exists, strict convexity makes it unique, and it defines Marshallian demand.

\[\max_{x_1, x_2 \geq 0} \; U(x_1, x_2) \quad \text{subject to} \quad p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \leq I.\]

Existence (Weierstrass)

\(U\) continuous on a compact budget set ⇒ the max exists. The compactness comes from the budget being a closed, bounded triangle.

Uniqueness

\(U\) strictly quasi-concave (preferences strictly convex) ⇒ the maximizer is unique. Recovered from Ch 4 axiom 4.

The output: Marshallian demand functions \(x_1^*(p_1, p_2, I)\) and \(x_2^*(p_1, p_2, I)\) — the central objects of consumer theory.

Weierstrass · compact · strictly quasi-concave · Marshallian demand

SAY: The best affordable bundle exists, strict convexity makes it unique, and it defines Marshallian demand. ⏸ 3 s. Everything after this computes those demand functions.

DON’T SAY:

  • “Existence needs convex preferences.” (Existence needs only continuous utility on a closed, bounded budget set (Weierstrass); convexity is for uniqueness.)
  • “Strictly convex preferences mean the utility function is convex.” (It means U is strictly quasi-concave: indifference curves bow toward the origin.)

BACKGROUND:

  • Existence by Weierstrass: a continuous function on a compact set attains its maximum; the budget set is a closed, bounded triangle.
  • Uniqueness by strict quasi-concavity, which is strict convexity of preferences (axiom 4 from Ch 4) in different language.
  • The output is the Marshallian demand function x_i*(p1, p2, I); every later chapter builds on it, and the rest of the chapter computes closed forms for it.

Tangency condition — slope form

At an interior optimum the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line: \(\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} = p_1/p_2\).

At an interior optimum, the highest indifference curve the consumer can reach is tangent to the budget line. Tangency means equal slopes:

Indifference-curve slope

\(-\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} \;=\; -MU_1/MU_2\)

from implicit differentiation of \(U(x_1, x_2) = \bar{U}\), Ch 4

Budget-line slope

\(-p_1/p_2\)

opportunity cost of \(x_1\) in units of \(x_2\)

\[\boxed{\;\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} \;=\; \dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2} \;=\; \dfrac{p_1}{p_2}.\;}\]

Geometric intuition. At a non-tangent point, the indifference curve crosses the budget line — slide along the budget line toward the steeper-IC side and the consumer reaches a higher curve. Tangency is the place where no such gain remains.

tangency condition

SAY: At an interior optimum the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line: MRS equals p-one over p-two. ⏸ 3 s. Your trade-off rate equals the market’s.

DON’T SAY:

  • “Tangency always gives the best bundle.” (Only with convex preferences and an interior optimum; with non-convex preferences a tangency can pick the worst bundle on the line.)
  • “MRS equals minus the price ratio.” (Both slopes are negative; the condition equates their absolute values, MRS = p1/p2.)

BACKGROUND:

  • The slope-form FOC is the geometric statement of the optimum. IC slope −MU1/MU2 comes from implicit differentiation of U(x1, x2) = Ū (Ch 4); budget slope −p1/p2 is the opportunity cost of x1 in units of x2.
  • Why it must hold: at a non-tangent point the budget line crosses the local IC, so moving along the line reaches a higher curve. At tangency every local move along the line reaches a lower curve.
  • Watch the sign: both slopes are negative; the FOC equates absolute values (or both signs flip together). HU3 in the chapter makes this point.
  • Deep Dive framing (about 11:06): the MRS is the voice in your head; at the optimum it agrees with the market’s price tag.

IF ASKED:

  • “Which way do you slide at a non-tangent point?” → If the curve is steeper than the line (MRS > p1/p2), good 1 is worth more to you than it costs: buy more good 1.

\(\lambda\) is the marginal utility of income

\(\lambda\) is the marginal utility of income: the extra utils from one more dollar, in utils per dollar.

If you handed the consumer one more dollar of income, how many additional utils would they extract from it (when re-optimizing)?

\[\boxed{\;\dfrac{\partial v(p, I)}{\partial I} = \lambda^*.\;}\]

This is the envelope theorem — stated here, proved in Math Appendix A.6, and used again in Ch 8 on the cost side. \(v(p, I)\) is the indirect utility function: the value of \(U\) at the optimum, as a function of \(p\) and \(I\).

The Lagrangian

The Lagrangian’s first-order conditions give \(MU_1/p_1 = MU_2/p_2 = \lambda\) and the budget constraint.

\[\mathcal{L}(x_1, x_2, \lambda) \;=\; U(x_1, x_2) \;+\; \lambda \big( I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2 \big).\]

Sign convention: constraint written as \(I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2 \geq 0\) — "you can spend up to \(I\)"; \(\lambda \geq 0\).

FOC 1

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial x_1 = MU_1 - \lambda p_1 = 0\)

FOC 2

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial x_2 = MU_2 - \lambda p_2 = 0\)

FOC \(\lambda\)

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \lambda = I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2 = 0\)

From FOC 1 and FOC 2: \(\;MU_1/p_1 = \lambda = MU_2/p_2\). The equimarginal principle is the FOC of the Lagrangian.

Lagrangian · Lagrange multiplier · first-order condition (FOC)

SAY: The Lagrangian’s first-order conditions give MU-one over p-one equals MU-two over p-two equals lambda, and the budget constraint. ⏸ 3 s. The equimarginal principle is the FOC.

DON’T SAY:

  • “The lambda term is a penalty for going over budget.” (At the optimum the bracket is zero; lambda prices the constraint, it does not punish.)
  • “Lambda can come out negative.” (With the constraint written I − p1x1 − p2x2 ≥ 0, λ ≥ 0: more income cannot hurt.)

BACKGROUND:

  • Three FOCs deliver everything: two stationarity equations plus the constraint itself, which is just ∂L/∂λ = 0; treat that one as bookkeeping.
  • The two stationarity equations recombine into the equimarginal principle from the Alex slides.
  • The whole apparatus is just calculus on a sum.
  • The Deep Dive calls the second term ’a penalty’ (about 18:51); the deck’s misconception box on the next slide says otherwise, and the deck wins.

IF ASKED:

  • “Why does ∂L/∂λ give the budget?” → The derivative of λ(I − p1x1 − p2x2) in λ is the bracket; setting it to zero spends all income.

Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1736–1813)

Giuseppe Lodovico Lagrangia — born in Turin, succeeded Euler at the Berlin Academy in 1766, at 30. Mécanique Analytique (1788) is the founding text of analytical mechanics.

The constrained-mechanics move

A particle constrained to a surface; a pendulum at fixed length. Introduce an auxiliary variable — the multiplier — that encodes the force the constraint exerts. The math: identical to \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial x_i = MU_i - \lambda p_i = 0\).

Hicks & Allen (1934) — tracing back to Slutsky (1915) — noticed the parallel. A consumer constrained to a budget line is mathematically identical to a particle constrained to a surface. Lagrange's "constraint force" became the economist's "marginal utility of income."

From the Mécanique preface

“No diagrams will be found in this work.”

A deliberate rejection of Newton's and Euler's geometric style: mechanics is a branch of pure analysis.

Calculus Corner CC2 — the general Cobb-Douglas closed form

In general \(x_1^* = \alpha I/p_1\) and \(x_2^* = (1-\alpha)I/p_2\): share times income, divided by own price.

Same two moves as the worked case — but keep \(\alpha\) symbolic. For \(U = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}\), \(\alpha \in (0,1)\):

Step 1 — FOC ratio (kills \(\lambda\))

\[\dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2} = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\dfrac{x_2}{x_1} = \dfrac{p_1}{p_2} \;\Rightarrow\; p_2 x_2 = \dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,p_1 x_1.\]

Step 2 — substitute into the budget

\[p_1 x_1 + \dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}p_1 x_1 = \dfrac{1}{\alpha}\,p_1 x_1 = I \;\Rightarrow\; p_1 x_1 = \alpha I.\]

\[\boxed{\;x_1^* = \dfrac{\alpha I}{p_1}, \qquad x_2^* = \dfrac{(1-\alpha) I}{p_2}.\;}\]

and \(\lambda^* = \alpha^{\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha}\,p_1^{-\alpha}\,p_2^{-(1-\alpha)}\)

When Jordan's income rises, the number of shows doesn't budge — the extra just spreads across everything else.

\(U(x_1, x_2) = 40\sqrt{x_1} + x_2\), \(p_1 = 10\), \(p_2 = 1\), \(I = 100\).

FOCs: \(\tfrac{40}{2\sqrt{x_1}} = 10\lambda\), \(1 = \lambda\), \(10 x_1 + x_2 = 100\).

From FOC2: \(\lambda^* = 1\). From FOC1: \(\tfrac{20}{\sqrt{x_1^*}} = 10 \Rightarrow \sqrt{x_1^*} = 2 \Rightarrow x_1^* = 4\). Budget: \(x_2^* = 100 - 40 = 60\).

\[\boxed{\;x_1^* = 4, \;\; x_2^* = 60, \;\; \lambda^* = 1.\;}\]

Crucially: \(x_1^*\) does not depend on \(I\). Double income to \$200 and \(x_1^*\) is still 4; \(x_2^*\) jumps to 160 — every extra dollar flows to other goods.

Why this matters. Quasilinear utility makes good \(x_2\) the numeraire that absorbs all income changes. This is the workhorse for partial-equilibrium welfare analysis (consumer surplus calculations in Ch 6 / Ch 11).

Three ways tangency fails — the diagnostic

Check each corner: if its good’s \(MU/p\) is at least the other’s there, that corner is optimal.

Case 1: Linear preferences

\(U = ax_1 + bx_2\): constant MRS \(= a/b\). Compare to \(p_1/p_2\) — the corner with higher \(MU/p\) wins. W5.4.

Case 2: Non-negativity binds

Unconstrained tangency would push \(x_i < 0\) (infeasible). Optimum slides to the corner where \(x_i = 0\). HW Problem 2.

Case 3: Non-differentiable \(U\)

\(U = \min(\cdot)\): kink at the ridge \(ax_1 = bx_2\). FOCs don't apply — use ridge + budget. W5.3.

Two-good corner diagnostic. Check the candidate corners \((I/p_1, 0)\) and \((0, I/p_2)\):

  • At \((I/p_1, 0)\): if \(MU_1/p_1 \geq MU_2/p_2\) there, that corner is the optimum.
  • Symmetric at \((0, I/p_2)\).
  • If neither corner satisfies its diagnostic, look for an interior tangency.

Key takeaways

  1. The Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L} = U + \lambda(I - p \cdot x)\) is the unifying tool for constrained consumer choice. Three FOCs: two stationarity + one budget.
  2. Two equivalent forms of the interior optimum: slope (\(\mathrm{MRS} = p_1/p_2\)) and ratio (\(MU_i/p_i = \lambda\)). Slope cleaner geometrically, ratio cleaner algebraically and generalizes to N goods.
  3. \(\lambda^*\) is the marginal utility of income — utils per dollar, the shadow price of the budget.
  4. Four canonical utility families, four closed-form demands: Cobb-Douglas (constant shares), perfect complements (ride the ridge), perfect substitutes (corner), quasilinear (zero income effect on the non-numeraire).
  5. Real budget sets bend. The Lagrangian adapts piecewise: solve FOCs on each smooth segment, evaluate at the kink, compare. Empirical bunching is muted by frictions.
