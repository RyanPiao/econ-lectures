The Consumer's Income and the Budget Constraint

The budget constraint is the curve of every consumption bundle the consumer can afford when spending all of their income. Plotted alongside indifference curves to find the optimum.

Step 1 — Two-good income identity

\[\text{Income} \;=\; P_X Q_X + P_Y Q_Y\]

Step 2 — Solve for \(Q_Y\) (slope-intercept)

\[Q_Y \;=\; \dfrac{\text{Income}}{P_Y} \;-\; \dfrac{P_X}{P_Y} \, Q_X\]

\[Q_B \;=\; 5 \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2} \, Q_L\]

The budget set, formally

\[B(p_1, p_2, I) = \{(x_1, x_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2_{+} \mid p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \leq I\}\]

The set of bundles the consumer can afford — a triangle in two-good space, bounded by the non-negativity orthant and the budget line.

Boundary — the budget constraint

\[p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = I\]

Anatomy

  • \(x_1\)-intercept: \(I/p_1\) — spend everything on good 1
  • \(x_2\)-intercept: \(I/p_2\) — spend everything on good 2
  • Slope: \(-p_1/p_2\) — opportunity cost of good 1 in units of good 2
After Maya's concrete setup (slide 4) and the three-panel comparative-statics (slide 7), we now lift to the formal set-theoretic definition. Two pieces of vocabulary to land: (1) the inequality version (the set) vs the equality version (the boundary), and (2) the slope as opportunity cost in units of good 2. From here we step into the per-case detail (price rotation, income shift, homogeneity) on the next three slides.

Factors that affect the budget constraint's position

The slope and position of the budget constraint are functions of two factors: income and relative prices.

1. Change in income

Shifts the budget constraint by changing the intercepts (both \(I/p_1\) and \(I/p_2\) scale by the same factor). Slope unchanged.

2. Change in the price of one good

Pivots the budget constraint by changing the slope (\(-p_1/p_2\)) and the affected good's intercept; the other intercept stays put.

(a) Doubling of the price of lattes  |  (b) Doubling of the price of burritos  |  (c) Reduction in income by \(1/2\)

Comparative static: price rotation

\(p_1\) rises with \(p_2\) and \(I\) held fixed:

  • \(x_1\)-intercept \(I/p_1\) falls — constraint pivots inward along the \(x_1\) axis.
  • \(x_2\)-intercept \(I/p_2\) unchanged — pivot point is on the \(x_2\) axis.
  • Slope \(-p_1/p_2\) becomes more negative — constraint is steeper.

Price rotation = same income, different relative prices ⇒ the menu of feasible trade-offs changes.

Figure 5.2 — \(p_1\) rises ⇒ pivot inward along \(x_1\) axis.

The pivot is around the unaffected intercept. Symmetric story when p2 rises (pivot around x1 intercept). The slide previews substitution: a price change rearranges relative prices and the consumer will reallocate — we'll formalize that in Ch 6 with the Slutsky decomposition.

Comparative static: income parallel shift

\(I\) rises with both prices held fixed:

  • Both intercepts rise proportionally.
  • Slope \(-p_1/p_2\) unchanged — no change in relative prices.
  • Outward parallel shift — the consumer can afford strictly more of every good.

Income shift = same relative prices, more money ⇒ pure income effect, no substitution effect.

Figure 5.3 — \(I\) rises ⇒ parallel outward shift, same slope.

Price rotation vs income shift is the foundational distinction the Slutsky decomposition will exploit in Ch 6. A price change does both rotation and shift (the substitution effect lives in the rotation, the income effect lives in the implied shift on real income); the Slutsky equation is the algebra that separates them.

Equiproportional scaling — nothing happens

Scale \((p_1, p_2, I)\) by any factor \(\alpha > 0\). Then the new constraint is

\[\alpha p_1 x_1 + \alpha p_2 x_2 = \alpha I \;\;\iff\;\; p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = I.\]

Both sides divide by \(\alpha\). The budget set is identical. Doubling everything is the same as doubling nothing.

Vocabulary

\(B(p, I)\) is homogeneous of degree zero in \((p, I)\). Demand inherits the property: \(x_i^*(\alpha p, \alpha I) = x_i^*(p, I)\).

Why this matters. Pure inflation — all prices and wages move proportionally — leaves real consumption unchanged in this model. The Phillips-curve story is about relative price stickiness, not the homogeneity property itself.

Homogeneity of degree zero is the most-used algebraic fact in the rest of the course. It is why we can always normalize one price to 1 in worked examples without loss of generality. It also delivers the canonical macro point: in the absence of nominal rigidities, only relative prices matter. Engineering note: if you ever derive a "demand function" that is NOT homog-deg-zero in (p, I), you have a sign error somewhere — check first.

Lin's Claude API budget: where the kink comes from

Setup

Lin (econ-CS double major) has \$30/week between energy drinks \(E\) at \$5 and Claude API tokens \(T\) (millions). Tier pricing: \$3/M for the first 5M tokens (Standard sync), then \$1.50/M beyond 5M (Batch API, 24h async).

Step 1 — Standard segment \((T \leq 5)\)

All tokens at standard rate. Spend \(5E + 3T = 30\):

\[T = 10 - \tfrac{5}{3}E \;\;\text{slope} = -\tfrac{5}{3}\]

Each energy drink forgone ⇒ \(5/3\!\approx\!1.67\) M tokens.

Step 2 — Batch segment \((T > 5)\)

First 5M at \$3/M, rest at \$1.50/M. Spend \(5E + 15 + 1.5(T-5) = 30\):

\[T = 15 - \tfrac{10}{3}E \;\;\text{slope} = -\tfrac{10}{3}\]

Each energy drink forgone ⇒ \(10/3\!\approx\!3.33\) M tokens (Batch).

Step 3 — The kink

The two segments meet at \(E = 3,\; T = 5\) — exactly where Lin has spent \$15 on tokens (5M at standard rate). Above the kink, each energy drink forgone now buys twice as many tokens. Budget line kinks outward.

Universal pattern. Volume discounts turn the relative price into a piecewise-constant function. Same structure: AWS S3 tiers · Costco bulk eggs · electricity tier-2 rates · Spotify family vs solo.

Poll #1: Equiproportional price + income change

Suppose income doubles from \(I\) to \(2I\), and \(p_1\) doubles from \(p_1\) to \(2 p_1\), while \(p_2\) stays the same. What happens to the budget set?

A) Both intercepts shift outward
B) Pure rotation, no shift
C) Identical budget set as before
D) \(x_1\)-intercept unchanged, \(x_2\)-intercept doubles

60 sec to vote

Press 1 to start a 60-sec timer. Tests the trap that "everything's bigger so the set grows" intuition (A). Right answer is D — only \(p_1\) and \(I\) scaled together, leaving \(I/p_1\) unchanged and \(I/p_2 \rightarrow 2 I/p_2\). Down arrow reveals the answer slide.

Answer: D — \(x_1\) unchanged, \(x_2\) doubles

\[\frac{I'}{p_1'} = \frac{2I}{2p_1} = \frac{I}{p_1}, \qquad \frac{I'}{p_2'} = \frac{2I}{p_2} = 2 \cdot \frac{I}{p_2}.\]

The trap is C — equiproportional scaling leaves the budget set unchanged, but here only good 1's price scaled with income; good 2's relative price fell. Slope changes from \(-p_1/p_2\) to \(-2 p_1 / p_2\) — steeper. The set does grow, but only on the good-2 axis.

Most students pick A (everything got bigger). The careful answer is D. Use this to anchor the homogeneity-degree-zero distinction: equiproportional in all three vs. proportional in only some. The trap is identical to the one in Ch 3 elasticity (mixing up percentage changes).

§5.2  The Consumer's Problem and the Tangency Condition

15 min · Two ways to write one optimum: slope form and ratio form

What will the consumer consume?

Chapter 4 gave us utility and indifference curves — how the consumer ranks bundles. §5.1 gave us the budget constraint — which bundles are feasible. Combining these gets us to choice.

The consumer's optimization problem

Maximize utility, subject to income and market prices.

Two equivalent characterizations of the optimum

  • Slope form: \(\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} = p_1/p_2\) — indifference curve tangent to budget line.
  • Ratio form: \(MU_1/p_1 = MU_2/p_2 = \lambda\) — equimarginal across goods.

We will see both characterizations are the FOC of the same Lagrangian (§5.3). For now, build the intuition table-first.

What if MU per dollar differs across goods?

Take a starting bundle on the budget line but not at the optimum. The rational consumer reallocates — and the Law of Diminishing MU drives the system back to equality.

Step 1 — Off-optimum bundle B: \((G=0.5,\; U=\tfrac{11}{3})\)

🎮  \(\dfrac{MU_G}{p_G} = \dfrac{180}{80} = \mathbf{2.25}\)

🍔  \(\dfrac{MU_U}{p_U} = \dfrac{41.25}{30} \approx \mathbf{1.375}\)

2.25 > 1.375  →  Games gives more "bang per buck"  →  shift spending toward G.

Step 2 — Reallocate; LDMU pulls both ratios toward each other

Buy more G (0.5 → 1):  \(MU_G\) falls 180 → 140  (LDMU)
⇒ \(MU_G/p_G\) drops 2.25 → 1.75

Buy less U (\(\tfrac{11}{3}\) → \(\tfrac{7}{3}\)):  \(MU_U\) rises 41.25 → 52.5
⇒ \(MU_U/p_U\) rises 1.375 → 1.75

Step 3 — Convergence at Point C: \((G=1,\; U=\tfrac{7}{3})\)

\(\dfrac{MU_G}{p_G} = \dfrac{140}{80} = \mathbf{1.75}\)  \(=\)  \(\dfrac{MU_U}{p_U} = \dfrac{52.5}{30} = \mathbf{1.75}\)  ✓  EQUAL — no more reallocation gains.

This is the "why does the optimum land where MU/$ values match?" answer. Pick a bundle ON the budget line but off-optimum (Bundle B: G=0.5, U=11/3, cost = 40 + 110 = 150 ✓). At B, Games gives 2.25 per dollar vs Uber Eats at 1.375 — clear disequilibrium. The rational consumer's move: shift toward G. Two LDMU effects work simultaneously: more G drives MU_G down (180→140), less U drives MU_U up (41.25→52.5, because fewer units means each remaining unit is more valuable at the margin). Both ratios converge to 1.75 at Point C. Pedagogy: emphasize that the LDMU is what GUARANTEES convergence — without it, a small advantage in MU/$ would never close. This sets up the formal Equimarginal Principle on the next slide as the FIXED POINT of this reallocation process.

The Equimarginal Principle — the rule for choosing

\[\boxed{\;\dfrac{MU_G}{p_G} \;=\; \dfrac{MU_U}{p_U}.\;}\]

Plain English

Allocate spending so the last dollar spent on each good buys the same amount of utility. If it didn't, you could swap a dollar to the better good and rise — you are not yet at the top.

Tie to Alex (W5.1)

At Point C (\(G=1,\; U=\tfrac{7}{3}\)):  \(\tfrac{140}{80} = \tfrac{52.5}{30} = \mathbf{1.75}\).  Same optimum we found by enumerating bundles in the utility table.

Reminder:  MU is the derivative \(dU/dQ\); the MU/$ table uses \(\Delta TU/\Delta Q\) on 5 sample bundles. The Lagrangian gives the exact derivative-based optimum without sampling.

Real-world application: cloud storage plan choice. Students often hear "cheaper per GB is better" — that's the price-per-unit fallacy. The correct rule is MU per dollar (the equimarginal principle), which depends on YOUR USAGE PATTERN. For a typical college student using ~45 GB for photos/iMessage/iCloud backup: iCloud+ 50 GB at $0.99/mo gives MU/$ ≈ 101 (the 50 GB nearly all gets used). Other real-world examples to cite: family-size cereal box vs single-serve (more cereal per $ but goes stale), unlimited gym membership vs pay-per-visit (lower per-visit cost only if you visit often enough), all-you-can-eat vs à la carte (cheaper per item but you stop being hungry well before the buffet runs out). The pedagogical anchor: MU drops sharply once you've covered essentials, and equimarginal says STOP allocating to a good once its MU/$ falls below what alternatives offer.

The problem

Riley spends her \$48 weekly fun budget on 🍣 sushi rolls (\(S\), \$8 each) and 🍡 mochi (\(M\), \$4 each). Her utility is Cobb-Douglas:

\[U(S, M) = \sqrt{S\,M} = S^{0.5} \, M^{0.5}\]

Marginal utilities (given):

\(MU_S = 0.5\,S^{-0.5} M^{0.5}\)  ·  \(MU_M = 0.5\,S^{0.5} M^{-0.5}\)

 What are Riley's utility-maximizing quantities \(S^*\) and \(M^*\)?

Step 1 — set MRS = price ratio

\(\mathrm{MRS}_{SM} = \dfrac{MU_S}{MU_M} = \dfrac{0.5\,S^{-0.5} M^{0.5}}{0.5\,S^{0.5} M^{-0.5}} = \dfrac{M}{S} = \dfrac{p_S}{p_M} = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2\)
  ⇒ \(M = 2S.\)

  ⇒ \(S^{\!*} = 3\),  \(M^{\!*} = 6.\)

Formal consumer problem

\[\max_{x_1, x_2 \geq 0} \; U(x_1, x_2) \quad \text{subject to} \quad p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \leq I.\]

Existence (Weierstrass)

\(U\) continuous on a compact budget set ⇒ the max exists. The compactness comes from the budget being a closed, bounded triangle.

Uniqueness

\(U\) strictly quasi-concave (preferences strictly convex) ⇒ the maximizer is unique. Recovered from Ch 4 axiom 4.

The output: Marshallian demand functions \(x_1^*(p_1, p_2, I)\) and \(x_2^*(p_1, p_2, I)\) — the central objects of consumer theory.

Two technical conditions land here. Existence by Weierstrass (continuous function on compact set attains its max). Uniqueness by strict quasi-concavity (which is just strict convexity of preferences, axiom 4 from Ch 4 in different language). The output we want is the Marshallian demand function — every later chapter builds on it. We will spend the rest of today computing closed forms for it.

Tangency condition — slope form

At an interior optimum, the highest indifference curve the consumer can reach is tangent to the budget line. Tangency means equal slopes:

Indifference-curve slope

\(-\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} \;=\; -MU_1/MU_2\)

from implicit differentiation of \(U(x_1, x_2) = \bar{U}\), Ch 4

Budget-line slope

\(-p_1/p_2\)

opportunity cost of \(x_1\) in units of \(x_2\)

\[\boxed{\;\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} \;=\; \dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2} \;=\; \dfrac{p_1}{p_2}.\;}\]

Geometric intuition. At a non-tangent point, the indifference curve crosses the budget line — slide along the budget line toward the steeper-IC side and the consumer reaches a higher curve. Tangency is the place where no such gain remains.

The slope-form FOC is the geometric way to state the optimum. The intuition for *why* it must hold: any non-tangent point sits on a budget line that crosses the local IC, so the consumer can do better by moving. At tangency, every local move along the budget line takes the consumer to a lower IC. Watch out: the sign. Both slopes are negative; the FOC equates the absolute values (or both signs flip together). HU3 from the chapter notes makes this point.

Tangency condition — slope form, drawn

Read it as a flow

(1) the indifference curve sets the consumer's trade-off, slope \(-MU_1/MU_2\) (the MRS).  (2) the budget line sets the market's trade-off, slope \(-p_1/p_2\).  (3) the best affordable bundle is where the two are tangent.

\[\mathrm{MRS} = \dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2} = \dfrac{p_1}{p_2}\]

At any non-tangent point the indifference curve crosses the budget line — slide along the line toward the steeper-IC side and reach a higher curve. Tangency is where no such gain is left.

Equimarginal principle — ratio form

Rewrite the same FOC:

\[\dfrac{MU_1}{p_1} \;=\; \dfrac{MU_2}{p_2} \;=\; \lambda.\]

Marginal utility per dollar is the same for every good. If it weren't — say \(MU_1/p_1 > MU_2/p_2\) — the consumer would shift a penny from good 2 to good 1 and gain.

Generalizes to \(N\) goods: \(\;MU_i/p_i = \lambda\;\) for every \(i\). The slope form needs \(\binom{N}{2}\) pairwise equations; the ratio form needs just \(N\) parallel ones sharing the same \(\lambda\).

What \(\lambda\) means: the shared value is the shadow value of an extra \$1 of income — how much utility one more dollar buys at the optimum. The same \(\lambda\) falls out of the Lagrangian in §5.3; we unpack it next slide.

Figure 5.8 — equimarginal across three goods, common shadow value \(\lambda\).

Slope form vs ratio form: HU3 from the chapter notes. Both are algebraic faces of the same FOC. Use whichever is shorter for the utility function at hand: ratio form for Cobb-Douglas (product cancels), slope form for clean geometric arguments. The ratio form's big advantage is generalization to N goods — we'll see it in Walkthrough W5.8 (three-good Cobb-Douglas). This slide now also introduces what λ means (the shadow value of income), previewing the full interpretation on the next slide.

\(\lambda\) is the marginal utility of income

If you handed the consumer one more dollar of income, how many additional utils would they extract from it (when re-optimizing)?

\[\boxed{\;\dfrac{\partial v(p, I)}{\partial I} = \lambda^*.\;}\]

Formal statement — the envelope theorem. Full proof: Math Appendix A.6. \(v(p, I)\) is the indirect utility function (the value of \(U\) at the optimum, as a function of \(p\) and \(I\)). Appendix Walkthrough W5.A1 will verify this numerically for Cobb-Douglas.

HU2 — Units matter.

\(\lambda\) is in utils per dollar, not utils, not dollars. Walkthrough W5.2 will produce \(\lambda^* \approx 0.10095\) utils/\$; Walkthrough W5.5 will produce \(\lambda^* = 12\) "dollars per discretionary hour" because the utility weights were chosen to make utils dollar-equivalent.

Why \(\lambda\) is interesting. It is the shadow price of the constraint — the value to the consumer of relaxing the budget by \$1. In production theory (Ch 7), the same multiplier becomes the firm's shadow value of a relaxed resource constraint.

Land three things here. (1) The number \(\lambda\) has economic meaning — it is the rate at which utility rises with income, formally via the envelope theorem. (2) The units — utils per dollar. Students consistently get this wrong; HU2 is in the chapter precisely because of how often this trips students up. (3) The same object reappears in producer theory as the shadow price of a resource — the Lagrangian is a unified piece of machinery.

§5.3  The Lagrangian Method

15 min · The auxiliary variable that does all the work

The Lagrangian

\[\mathcal{L}(x_1, x_2, \lambda) \;=\; U(x_1, x_2) \;+\; \lambda \big( I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2 \big).\]

Sign convention: constraint written as \(I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2 \geq 0\) — "you can spend up to \(I\)"; \(\lambda \geq 0\).

FOC 1

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial x_1 = MU_1 - \lambda p_1 = 0\)

FOC 2

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial x_2 = MU_2 - \lambda p_2 = 0\)

FOC \(\lambda\)

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \lambda = I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2 = 0\)

From FOC 1 and FOC 2: \(\;MU_1/p_1 = \lambda = MU_2/p_2\). The equimarginal principle is the FOC of the Lagrangian.

Three FOCs deliver everything: two stationarity equations plus the constraint itself (which is just \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \lambda = 0\)). The FOC on \(\lambda\) re-derives the budget constraint — treat it as bookkeeping. The two stationarity equations recombine into the equimarginal principle from slide 15. The whole apparatus is just calculus on a sum.

Visualizing the Lagrangian optimum

Figure 5.1 — budget line, indifference curves, tangency point, and \(\lambda^*\).

Cobb-Douglas \(U = x_1^{1/3} x_2^{2/3}\), \(p_1 = 4,\ p_2 = 6,\ I = 60\): tangency at \((5,\, 20/3)\), \(\lambda^* \approx 0.10095\). Every number is derived in closed form in Walkthrough W5.2.

Misconception alert. The Lagrangian is not “utility minus a penalty for breaking the budget.” It is the device that locks in both the equimarginal condition (\(MU_i/p_i\) equal across goods) and the budget constraint at once — solving it just means writing down the three FOCs.

Here \(\lambda^*\) is the multiplier on the budget constraint — the same shadow value of income we met earlier (utils per extra \$1). That is why it rides on \(I - p_1 x_1 - p_2 x_2\): it prices how much relaxing the budget is worth.

Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1736–1813)

Giuseppe Lodovico Lagrangia — born in Turin, succeeded Euler at the Berlin Academy by his late twenties. Mécanique Analytique (1788) is the founding text of analytical mechanics.

The constrained-mechanics move

A particle constrained to a surface; a pendulum at fixed length. Introduce an auxiliary variable — the multiplier — that encodes the force the constraint exerts. The math: identical to \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial x_i = MU_i - \lambda p_i = 0\).

Hicks & Allen (1934) — tracing back to Slutsky (1915) — noticed the parallel. A consumer constrained to a budget line is mathematically identical to a particle constrained to a surface. Lagrange's "constraint force" became the economist's "marginal utility of income."

From the Mécanique preface

“No diagrams will be found in this work.”

A deliberate rejection of Newton's and Euler's geometric style: mechanics is a branch of pure analysis.

Historical context that lands the unification point. Pendulums and consumer budgets share the same mathematical structure — one constraint, one Lagrangian, one shadow price. The Hicks-Allen 1934 paper (Slutsky 1915 in Italian) is when economics formally imported the apparatus. The "no diagrams" preface quote is a great hook for the geometric-vs-algebraic intuition discussion.

Worked example — Alex's budget via the Lagrangian

Goal: take the same Alex budget problem on games and Uber Eats (budget \$150, \(p_G = \$80\), \(p_U = \$30\)) and recover the optimum \((G^{\!*} = 1,\, U^{\!*} = \tfrac{7}{3})\) analytically — no enumerating bundles.

Setup — Cobb-Douglas with exponents calibrated to Alex's expenditure shares (\(\$80/\$150 = \tfrac{8}{15}\) on G, \(\$70/\$150 = \tfrac{7}{15}\) on U):   \(U(G,\, U) = G^{8/15} \cdot U^{7/15}.\)

Step 1 — write the Lagrangian

\[\mathcal{L} = G^{8/15} U^{7/15} + \lambda(150 - 80G - 30U)\]

Step 2 — three first-order conditions

\(\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial G} = \tfrac{8}{15}\, G^{-7/15} U^{7/15} - 80\lambda = 0\)
\(\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial U} = \tfrac{7}{15}\, G^{8/15} U^{-8/15} - 30\lambda = 0\)
\(\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = 150 - 80G - 30U = 0\)

Step 3 — divide FOCG by FOCU

\(\dfrac{MU_G}{MU_U} = \dfrac{p_G}{p_U}\)  ⇒  \(\tfrac{8}{7} \cdot \dfrac{U}{G} = \tfrac{8}{3}\)  ⇒  \(\boxed{\,U = \tfrac{7}{3} G\,}\)

Step 4 — substitute into the budget

\(80G + 30\!\left(\tfrac{7}{3}G\right) = 150\)
\(\;\;80G + 70G = 150\)
\(\;\;150G = 150\)

Step 5 — recover the optimum

\(G^{\!*} = 1,\;\; U^{\!*} = \tfrac{7}{3}\)  ✓

Same bundle we found earlier by table-enumeration, dollar-by-dollar buying, and the tangency picture — now derived analytically.

\(\lambda^{\!*}\): from FOCU, \(\lambda^* = \tfrac{MU_U}{30} = \tfrac{1}{30}\cdot\tfrac{7}{15}\,(\tfrac{3}{7})^{8/15} \approx 0.0095\) utils/\$ — the shadow value of \$1 of income at the optimum (the "marginal utility of income" idea from earlier).

Capstone Lagrangian worked example for the chapter — recover Alex's budget-allocation optimum analytically using the same numbers we ran earlier ($150 budget, games $80, Uber Eats $30). Cobb-Douglas exponents (8/15, 7/15) are calibrated so the optimum lands EXACTLY at (G=1, U=7/3), matching the bundle we found by enumeration; the exponents correspond to expenditure shares ($80/$150 = 8/15 on games, $70/$150 = 7/15 on Uber Eats). Step 1 — Divide FOC1 by FOC2

Calculus Corner CC2 — the general Cobb-Douglas closed form

Same two moves as the worked case — but keep \(\alpha\) symbolic. For \(U = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}\), \(\alpha \in (0,1)\):

Step 1 — FOC ratio (kills \(\lambda\))

\[\dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2} = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\dfrac{x_2}{x_1} = \dfrac{p_1}{p_2} \;\Rightarrow\; p_2 x_2 = \dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,p_1 x_1.\]

Step 2 — substitute into the budget

\[p_1 x_1 + \dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}p_1 x_1 = \dfrac{1}{\alpha}\,p_1 x_1 = I \;\Rightarrow\; p_1 x_1 = \alpha I.\]

\[\boxed{\;x_1^* = \dfrac{\alpha I}{p_1}, \qquad x_2^* = \dfrac{(1-\alpha) I}{p_2}.\;}\]

and \(\lambda^* = \alpha^{\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha}\,p_1^{-\alpha}\,p_2^{-(1-\alpha)}\)

Constant shares. \(\dfrac{p_1 x_1^*}{I} = \alpha\) and \(\dfrac{p_2 x_2^*}{I} = 1-\alpha\) — identically, whatever the prices or income. Spending shares equal the exponents — the signature Cobb-Douglas prediction, and the easiest empirical test (→ TD1 / the CEX data).

What if Maya's wage rises 20%?

Suppose Maya gets a raise: \$18/hr → \$24/hr. How does her time allocation change?

Re-solve: the work FOC

New utility piece: \(96 \ln(24 h)\). The work FOC:

\(\dfrac{\partial U}{\partial h} = \dfrac{96 \cdot 24}{24\,h} = \dfrac{96}{h}\)

The wage cancels. The 24 in the numerator and the 24 inside the log cancel exactly. Result: \(\lambda^* = 12,\, h^* = 8,\, \ell^* = 61,\, s^* = 28\) — nothing changes.

Why? Two effects cancel

  • Substitution effect: each hour of work now buys more → work more
  • Income effect: Maya is effectively richer → take more leisure

In log-utility, these cancel exactly. Net change: zero. This is the signature of unit elasticity of substitution.

Is this realistic? No — real Frisch elasticities are around 0.3–0.5 (substitution dominates, people work a bit more when wages rise). Log utility is a useful baseline; Ch 6 swaps it for a more flexible form to match the data.

Walkthrough 5.A1 — Indirect utility and the envelope check FYI

Plain English:  \(v(p, I)\) is the highest utility a consumer can reach given prices \(p\) and income \(I\), after they've already optimized. It's the "best you can do" number at those prices and that income.

For Cobb-Douglas \(U = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}\), plug the CC2 demands \(x_1^* = \alpha I/p_1\), \(x_2^* = (1-\alpha) I/p_2\) back into \(U\):

\[U(x_1^*, x_2^*) = \left(\dfrac{\alpha I}{p_1}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{(1-\alpha) I}{p_2}\right)^{1-\alpha}\]

collect the constants; the income terms merge, \(I^{\alpha} I^{1-\alpha} = I\)  ⇒

\[v(p_1, p_2, I) = \alpha^\alpha (1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha} \cdot \dfrac{I}{p_1^\alpha p_2^{1-\alpha}}\]

\(v\) is linear in \(I\) for Cobb-Douglas — the coefficient on \(I\) is \(\partial v/\partial I\).

At \(\alpha = 1/3\), \(p_1 = 4\), \(p_2 = 6\):

\(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial I} = \dfrac{\sqrt[3]{6}}{18} \approx 0.10095\)

\[\boxed{\;\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial I} = \lambda^* \approx 0.10095\;}\]

Matches \(\lambda^*\) from W5.2 to 4 decimals — the envelope theorem verified.

Why we need \(v(p, I)\) for next chapter (income & substitution effects)

Ch 6 asks: when a price changes, how much of the demand change is "I now prefer the cheaper good more" (substitution effect) vs "I'm richer/poorer in real terms" (income effect)? To separate them you need to know what utility level the consumer was at before, and how much income would keep them at that same utility under the new prices — that's exactly what \(v(p, I)\) lets you compute. The Slutsky decomposition we'll do next chapter cannot be written without indirect utility.

Indirect utility walkthrough — what v(p, I) is and why we need it for Ch 6. Plain-English version at the top: v is "the highest utility a consumer can reach at those prices and that income." Two-column math: LEFT now SHOWS the substitution (previously it just asserted the result) — plug the CC2 demands x_1*=αI/p_1, x_2*=(1-α)I/p_2 into U = x_1^α x_2^(1-α), giving (αI/p_1)^α ((1-α)I/p_2)^(1-α); collect the constants and note the income terms merge (I^α I^(1-α) = I) to land v(p, I) = α^α (1-α)^(1-α) · I / (p_1^α p_2^(1-α)); then shows v is linear in I. RIGHT computes ∂v/∂I numerically at the W5.2 numbers and matches it to λ* from the Lagrangian — envelope theorem verified to 4 decimal places. Bottom callout connects to Ch 6 — Slutsky decomposes a price change into substitution effect (preferring the cheaper good more) and income effect (feeling richer/poorer). Both effects require knowing the original utility level and what income would maintain it under new prices — and that's literally what v(p, I) computes. Without indirect utility, the Slutsky equation can't be stated, much less solved. Old "envelope buys you" misconception is replaced by this more forward-looking bridge to Ch 6.

Roy's identity — get demand back out of \(v(p, I)\)

Plain English:  if you know the value function \(v(p, I)\) — say you estimated it from welfare data — you can pull the demand functions straight back out by differentiating. No need to re-solve the Lagrangian.

\[\boxed{\;x_i^*(p, I) \;=\; -\,\dfrac{\partial v/\partial p_i}{\partial v/\partial I}.\;}\]

Check it on Cobb-Douglas

From W5.A1, \(v = \alpha^{\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha}\, I\, p_1^{-\alpha} p_2^{-(1-\alpha)}\). Two derivatives:

\[\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial p_1} = -\,\dfrac{\alpha\, v}{p_1}, \qquad \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial I} = \dfrac{v}{I}.\]

(\(v\) carries \(p_1^{-\alpha}\), so differentiating pulls down \(-\alpha/p_1\); \(v\) is linear in \(I\), so \(\partial v/\partial I = v/I\).)

\[-\dfrac{\partial v/\partial p_1}{\partial v/\partial I} = -\dfrac{-\alpha v/p_1}{v/I} = \dfrac{\alpha I}{p_1} = x_1^{*} \;\checkmark\]

Exactly the closed form from CC2 — recovered without re-solving.  You'll use this in the AI Lab. The formal proof (and its producer-side dual, Shephard's lemma) come in Ch 6.

Roy's identity as a down-arrow sub-slide off W5.A1 — added so students have the slide-side support for the AI Lab (previously it lived only in the book's Ch 5 and was just name-dropped on the Ch 6 forward-bridge slide). Statement: x_i* = -(∂v/∂p_i)/(∂v/∂I). Intuition first: v is the value function; if you know it, demand falls out by differentiation — no re-optimizing. Then the two-line Cobb-Douglas check using the v already derived on W5.A1: v carries p_1^(-α) so ∂v/∂p_1 = -αv/p_1; v is linear in I so ∂v/∂I = v/I; the ratio gives αI/p_1 = x_1*, matching CC2 exactly. Emphasize the minus sign (it cancels the negative from ∂v/∂p_1 — demand is positive). Full proof + Shephard's lemma deferred to Ch 6, consistent with the book. Pacing: ~2 min — state it, then let the class do the two derivatives before revealing the ratio.

§5.5–5.6  Corners, Kinks, and Applications

20 min · What happens when tangency fails — and where the kinks live in real policy

Three ways tangency fails — the diagnostic

Case 1: Linear preferences

\(U = ax_1 + bx_2\): constant MRS \(= a/b\). Compare to \(p_1/p_2\) — the corner with higher \(MU/p\) wins. W5.4.

Case 2: Non-negativity binds

Unconstrained tangency would push \(x_i < 0\) (infeasible). Optimum slides to the corner where \(x_i = 0\). Problem 9.

Case 3: Non-differentiable \(U\)

\(U = \min(\cdot)\): kink at the ridge \(ax_1 = bx_2\). FOCs don't apply — use ridge + budget. W5.3.

Two-good corner diagnostic. Check the candidate corners \((I/p_1, 0)\) and \((0, I/p_2)\):

  • At \((I/p_1, 0)\): if \(MU_1/p_1 \geq MU_2/p_2\) there, that corner is the optimum.
  • Symmetric at \((0, I/p_2)\).
  • If neither corner satisfies its diagnostic, look for an interior tangency.
Three cases, one diagnostic. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions are the formal generalization to N goods with inequality constraints; the two-good corner check is what's tested. CC3 in the chapter notes has the full statement. Used directly in Problems 3, 9, 10, 11.

TD2 — Do people actually pick the best plan?

The Lagrangian model says: the rational consumer surveys all options and picks the highest-utility one. Do real people do this when choosing health insurance?

What the data say

When ACA marketplace customers re-enroll each year, most stick with last year's plan — even when a cheaper plan with the same benefits is right there. Plan choices are sticky, not freshly re-optimized (Handel, Kolstad & Spinnewijn, 2019).

The kicker

In Dutch health-insurance data, the top 5% of decision-makers get 3× the value out of the same set of plans as the bottom 5% — same prices, same options, very different outcomes. People with more education and quantitative training make better choices.

Takeaway: The Lagrangian shows what the optimum would be — a benchmark. Real choices are dragged off the benchmark by inertia, complexity, and defaults. The gap between model and reality is itself something policy can target (auto-enrollment in better plans, simpler comparison tools).

Key takeaways

  1. The Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L} = U + \lambda(I - p \cdot x)\) is the unifying tool for constrained consumer choice. Three FOCs: two stationarity + one budget.
  2. Two equivalent forms of the interior optimum: slope (\(\mathrm{MRS} = p_1/p_2\)) and ratio (\(MU_i/p_i = \lambda\)). Slope cleaner geometrically, ratio cleaner algebraically and generalizes to N goods.
  3. \(\lambda^*\) is the marginal utility of income — utils per dollar, the shadow price of the budget. Envelope theorem: \(\partial v / \partial I = \lambda^*\).
  4. Four canonical utility families, four closed-form demands: Cobb-Douglas (constant shares), perfect complements (ride the ridge), perfect substitutes (corner), quasilinear (zero income effect on the non-numeraire).
  5. Real budget sets bend. The Lagrangian adapts piecewise: solve FOCs on each smooth segment, evaluate at the kink, compare. Empirical bunching is muted by frictions.
