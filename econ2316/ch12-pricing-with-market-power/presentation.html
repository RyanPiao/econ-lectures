From One Price to Many

Ch 11 — single price

  • One price for all buyers: \(MR = MC\)
  • Markup \(L = (P-MC)/P = -1/\varepsilon_D\)
  • Captures only the rectangle below \(P_M\)
  • Leaves consumer surplus and deadweight loss on the table

Ch 12 — many prices

  • Different prices to different buyers
  • Six tools: 1st / 2nd / 3rd-degree, two-part tariffs, bundling, platforms
  • Converts that left-behind CS into producer surplus
  • DWL almost always falls — sometimes to zero

One assumption relaxes. Chapter 11 forced the monopolist to post a single price. Drop that — let the firm separate buyers it can tell apart, or get them to sort themselves — and the surplus a uniform price couldn't reach becomes capturable. The common goal of all six tools: convert consumer surplus into producer surplus. The welfare effect on consumers is ambiguous; the effect on the firm's profit never is.

The Basics of Pricing Strategy

Which pricing tool a firm can use ultimately depends on the information it has about its customers.

1 · Knows demand before the sale

  • Direct PD — price by observable customer traits.
  • Knows each buyer's whole demand curve → first-degree (perfect).
  • Less detail → segment by group → third-degree.

2 · Learns demand only after the sale

  • Indirect / second-degree — offer a menu, buyers self-select.
  • Bundle products into a package.
  • Block pricing & two-part tariffs.

3 · Customers share the same demand

  • Still profitable: block pricing and two-part tariffs capture extra surplus.

More information → finer discrimination. Full info reaches first-degree; a group label gives third-degree; no way to tell buyers apart falls back on menus, bundles, and two-part tariffs. The rest of the chapter works through each tool.

Pigou's Three Degrees — Ordered by What the Firm Must Know

Arthur Cecil Pigou, The Economics of Welfare (1920). Still the working taxonomy a century later.

First-degree (perfect)

Charge each buyer their exact reservation price. Captures all CS; output equals competitive. Needs: know every buyer's WTP.

Second-degree (menu)

Offer a menu; buyers self-select. The firm can't see types but designs choices so types reveal themselves. Needs: know the distribution.

Third-degree (group)

Segment by an observable label (student, weekend) and apply the Ch 11 rule per group: \(L_i = -1/\varepsilon_{D,i}\). Needs: observe a label that correlates with WTP.

Descending informational demand: know each buyer → know the distribution → observe a group label. Each degree extracts less than the one above but is more achievable. Real firms mix all three — Spotify uses group labels (Student), a menu (Audiobooks vs Premium), and edges toward personalization.

The organizing slide for §12.2–12.4. The single axis is informational requirement: first-degree is most demanding (and the upper bound on extraction), third-degree is least demanding (and the most common). Stress that the three are not rival theories — they are a menu the firm draws from depending on what it can observe and verify. We do one section each.

The Catch: No Arbitrage

Every PD scheme must block arbitrage — resale from the low-price segment to the high-price one. If a student bought at \$6.99 and resold to a non-student at \$11, the Student tier collapses.

Spotify: SheerID + same-address checks

Airlines: non-transferable named tickets

Pharma: serialization + 340B audit regime

Theme parks: date-stamped, ID/biometric entry

Heads Up 12.1 — PD needs market power and no arbitrage

Not every price difference is price discrimination. PD requires (a) market power (so \(P > MC\) is sustainable) and (b) no arbitrage between segments.

Two competitive sellers at different prices is price dispersion (search costs), not PD. And a monopolist who cannot stop resale is forced back to a single price — however different its buyers' WTPs.

Without an anti-arbitrage mechanism, PD collapses into a single price. The verification technology — ID checks, contracts, DRM, geography — is what makes the whole chapter possible.

The enabling condition for everything that follows. Ch 11's Lerner index assumed a single uniform price; Ch 12 assumes the firm additionally has tools to keep segments apart. Heads Up 12.1 heads off the most common student error: calling any price gap “discrimination.” Price dispersion (competitive, search-driven) is not PD. The four anti-arbitrage examples show it's an engineering problem solved differently by industry.

§12.2

First-Degree (Perfect) PD

The upper bound: capture every dollar, restore efficiency — \(Q_{PD} = Q_C\), \(CS = 0\)

First-Degree — Each Buyer’s Exact Willingness to Pay

The firm captures every dollar of consumer surplus. Nearly impossible — but the data economy is closing the gap.

What it takes — all three, or it collapses

1. Market power. Demand must slope down. A price-taker has no price to discriminate with.

2. No arbitrage. The cheap buyer must not be able to resell to the dear one (§12.1).

3. Know each buyer's WTP. The hard one — and the reason first-degree stays a bound.

It’s direct PD — with complete information about customers, the firm charges each buyer exactly their willingness to pay. So what happens to consumer surplus? Under perfect PD it is driven to zero: every dollar of surplus is converted into producer surplus.

What If the Firm Could Read Every Mind?

A monopolist who knows each buyer's exact reservation sells one unit to everyone whose WTP exceeds \(MC\), charging each their reservation:

Output expands to the competitive level \(Q_{PD} = Q_C\) — no deadweight loss. But the firm takes every dollar below demand and above \(MC\): \(CS = 0\), and producer surplus equals total surplus.

Perfect PD is the only form of market power that is allocatively efficient — the efficiency loss of monopoly comes from the single price, not from the markup itself. It is also the hardest to achieve: it needs each buyer's WTP.

The trap — monopoly is not the inefficiency

Most students answer that monopoly causes deadweight loss. It doesn't. The single price does.

Remove the single price and the inefficiency vanishes while the market power remains: output returns to \(Q_C\), \(DWL = 0\), and the entire surplus simply changes hands. Efficient and maximally extractive at the same time.

Walkthrough 12.1 — Perfect PD on Linear Demand

Problem. Demand \(P^d(Q) = 100 - Q\), constant \(MC = 20\). Compare (a) competitive, (b) uniform monopoly, (c) perfect PD — tally CS, PS, and DWL in each.

Set up & solve

(a) Competitive \(P=MC\): \(Q_C = 80\), \(P_C = \$20\). \(CS = \tfrac12(80)(80) = \$3{,}200\), \(PS = 0\), \(DWL = 0\).

(b) Uniform monopoly \(MR = 100 - 2Q = 20\): \(Q_M = 40\), \(P_M = \$60\). \(\pi = (40)(40) = \$1{,}600\), \(CS = \$800\), \(DWL = \$800\).

Perfect PD & check

(c) Perfect PD: every unit with \(P^d > MC\) trades, so \(Q_{PD} = 80\). Profit is the area under demand above \(MC\):

\(\pi_{PD} = \displaystyle\int_0^{80}(80 - q)\,dq = 6400 - 3200 = \$3{,}200\)

\(CS_{PD} = 0\), \(DWL = 0\). Profit equals competitive total surplus — the firm captured everything.

Takeaway. Surplus split moves \(0/\$3{,}200\) (competition) → \(\$1{,}600/\$800\) (uniform monopoly) → \(\$3{,}200/0\) (perfect PD), firm/consumer. Perfect PD is the upper bound: efficient and fully extractive. Real firms fall short for one reason — information.

Six step-reveals — the benchmark every later PD calculation measures against, and the exact calibration of the interactive widget (k=1 row is line (b); k→∞ is line (c)). Hammer the contrast: competition and perfect PD have the same output (80) and the same total surplus (\$3,200); they differ only in who gets it. Uniform monopoly is the inefficient one in the middle. The reason real firms can't reach (c) is informational, which motivates §12.3 (menus) and §12.4 (group labels).

An Attempt at First-Degree PD — Amazon, 2000

Could a seller read each shopper’s willingness to pay online — and quietly charge each a different price? In September 2000, Amazon tried.

Shoppers who deleted their cookies (so Amazon no longer recognized them as regulars) saw lower prices. One customer watched a DVD fall from $26.24 → $22.74.

Close, but not textbook first-degree — only an attempt. Amazon never observed anyone’s WTP: a cookie is a crude proxy for “repeat customer,” which is really third-degree on two groups. And since clearing cookies was free, buyers could sort themselves into the low price — the no-arbitrage condition failed.

The digital version of the car-dealer example — but press the "attempt" hard. Amazon's 2000 "price test" charged identifiable repeat customers more; clearing cookies (becoming an unknown shopper) revealed a lower price. The DVD anecdote ($26.24 vs $22.74) is the canonical story. Why this is NOT first-degree: (1) first-degree needs the seller to know each buyer's own WTP and charge exactly that, extracting all CS — Amazon knew only "cookie present / absent," a two-group observable, i.e. third-degree with a behavioral label; (2) the no-arbitrage condition failed, because deleting a cookie costs nothing, so any buyer could self-select into the low price (that self-sorting is second-degree logic, and it is what makes the scheme unravel); (3) Amazon's own account was that prices were varied randomly to estimate elasticity, not tailored to individuals. Good exam distinction: direction of travel = first-degree, mechanism actually available = third-degree on a crude proxy. Sets up the backlash next.

The Aftermath

The backlash was immediate — customers felt cheated, and the story made national news.

“We have never tested and we never will test prices based on customer demographics.”

— Jeff Bezos, 2000

Amazon called it “random” testing and refunded shoppers who paid more. The lesson: open first-degree PD on identifiable customers is a PR & legal landmine.

The reputational cost of visible personalized pricing. Bezos's flat denial + refunds. Even though firms have always price-discriminated, doing it openly on named customers triggers a fairness backlash (the Internet Policy Review survey: most people call online PD "unfair and unacceptable").

Amazon’s Strategy Today

After the backlash, Amazon and most e-tailers avoid true first-degree PD. Two tools do the work instead:

1. Dynamic pricing (looks second-degree) — the price moves for everyone with demand, rivals, time of day, and inventory. Not personalized — just a hyper-fast algorithm.

2. Personalized marketing — your history triggers a coupon or recommendation aimed at you. Same effect as a personal price — but framed as a discount for you, never a surcharge.

Amazon Personalize — your data drives what you see (and the deal you get)

The two legal, low-backlash substitutes for first-degree PD. Dynamic pricing = same price to all, changing fast (Uber surge is the extreme). Personalized marketing = the coupon/recommendation engine (Amazon Personalize) that targets YOU — economically a personalized price, but "framed" as a gift so it feels fair.

In-Store: Third-Degree That Edges Toward First

Grouping (third-degree): charge identifiable groups different prices.

Prime members get the discount; non-Prime pay the listed price.

Targeting → a “group of one”: merge your online data (browsing, Prime Video, income proxies) with your offline grocery data.

Amazon learns where you’re inelastic (brand-name detergent at full price) vs elastic (organic chicken only on sale).

Whole Foods (Amazon-owned) is the bridge from third- to first-degree. Prime vs non-Prime = explicit group pricing. But the real prize is the "group of one": fusing your Amazon online profile with your in-store grocery purchases to estimate item-by-item elasticity — the data needed for near-perfect PD.

The Payoff — Personalized Coupons

Amazon won’t change the register price by shopper (the 2000 lesson). It uses your data for behavior-based third-degree PD later:

Inelastic you — no coupon for the brand-name detergent you always buy. Why discount what you’ll pay full price for?

Elastic you — a targeted “20% off organic chicken” lands in your app, timed to your next trip.

Personalized coupons ≈ personalized prices — quietly approaching first-degree, capturing your surplus over time.

Framing. A “coupon for you” feels like a gift; a “surcharge for you” feels like theft — though the economics is identical. That asymmetry is the whole reason this arc ends in coupons and not in personal prices.

The mechanism that turns data into surplus extraction. Coupons are individualized (only the elastic buyer gets the discount), so the firm captures the inelastic buyer's full WTP and still makes the marginal sale to the elastic buyer — the textbook goal of first-degree PD, delivered through "discounts" that feel like a perk. §12.3

Second-Degree PD

Versioning & screening: offer a menu, let buyers reveal themselves

Can't Tell You Apart? Make You Choose

The firm knows the distribution of types but can't observe any individual's type — and can't just ask (everyone would claim the cheap type). Its only tool: a menu designed so each type's own best choice reveals it. Pricing becomes mechanism design.

Individual rationality (IR): each type's chosen package gives non-negative surplus, so it participates. “Is this worth buying at all?”

Incentive compatibility (IC): each type prefers its assigned package to any other on the menu. “Would I rather have the one meant for somebody else?”

Maximize profit subject to IR + IC for all types — the screening menu (Mussa-Rosen 1978; Maskin-Riley 1984). Two classic forms: versioning (quality tiers) and quantity discounts.

Fig 12.4 — two quality tiers; the high type self-selects up.

The figure is the quality axis, worked later in Walkthrough 12.2. Walkthrough 12.3 runs the identical logic on the quantity axis. One idea, two axes — and every example between here and there is one or the other.

The conceptual heart of §12.3. The firm faces an information problem and answers it with menu design, not interrogation. IR and IC are the two constraints students must be able to state and apply. The deep result (next two Walkthroughs): “no distortion at the top, distortion at the bottom” — the high type buys the efficient package but keeps an information rent; the low type's IR binds. Versioning (quality) and quantity discounts are the same logic on different axes. This is the most abstract slide in the section, so anchor it before the algebra arrives. The firm is not solving a pricing problem, it is solving a LYING problem: it would happily charge each type its own WTP, but it cannot ask, because everyone answers "I am the cheap type." So it gives up on asking and builds a menu where telling the truth is each type's own best move. IR and IC are just the two questions every buyer asks of a menu — is this worth buying at all, and would I rather have the package meant for somebody else. Say those in plain words before you write them as inequalities. Second-Degree — Build a Menu, Let Buyers Sort Themselves

The firm can’t tell who you are — so it designs options and lets you reveal your type by choosing.

Versioning. Spotify: Individual $12.99 → Duo $18.99 → Family $21.99, same catalogue, tiers gated by account count.

Quantity discounts. Costco 50-bag box at $0.45/bag vs $0.50 at the corner store.

Two-part tariffs. Gym: $300/yr + $10/class. Buffet: $20 in, $0/plate.

Bundling. $15 CD (12 songs) → iTunes $0.99 unbundled → Spotify re-bundles 100M songs at $12.99.

Netflix tiers: $6.99 ad-supported vs $22.99 premium — a $16 gap for the same library. Cinephiles self-select up; budget households (higher elasticity) self-select into ads. ~70M ad-tier users by 2025.

It’s indirect PD — used when the firm has market power and can prevent resale, customers have different demand curves, but the firm can’t identify a buyer’s type before purchase. Its tool: post a menu of pricing options and let customers self-select.

The defining feature: SELF-SELECTION. The seller doesn't observe your type; the menu makes you reveal it. Netflix is the cleanest modern case and ties back to elasticity: the ad tier captures the elastic segment without dropping price for everyone. Bundling's CD→iTunes→Spotify arc is a great 25-year story.

Versioning

Versioning — a pricing strategy in which a firm offers different product options designed to attract different types of consumers.

Common example: air travel — the same plane sells leisure and business fares, fenced apart by advance-purchase and stay rules.

Versioning does not require a cost difference — the marginal costs of the versions need not be equal.

The only requirement: the markup must be higher for the segment with the less elastic demand — business travelers (inelastic) carry the higher markup; leisure travelers (elastic, price-sensitive) pay less.

Versioning overview (a second-degree self-selection tool). Definition: offer different product options to attract different types. Air travel is the canonical case — restricted leisure fares vs flexible business fares on the same flight. Two teaching points: (1) versioning does NOT require the versions to cost different amounts to produce; (2) the binding condition is the inverse-elasticity logic — the less-elastic segment (business) carries the higher markup. Leads into the versioning examples that follow (Netflix tiers, the Spotify Duo-vs-Family incentive-compatibility check, then damaged goods).

Versioning — Buyers Self-Select

Offer a menu of versions and let each buyer voluntarily pick the one best for them — revealing their type. The firm prices by the version chosen, not by who you are. To make buyers split, firms widen the gap between tiers — sometimes intentionally limiting the cheaper one (the “damaged-goods” idea: stripped Basic Economy, a deliberately crippled chip).

Spotify is textbook versioning: Individual ($12.99), Duo ($18.99 / 2 accounts), Family ($21.99 / up to 6 accounts). Households self-select by size, and the cheaper tiers are deliberately capped (one account, same-address rules) so higher-value households pay more.

This is the mechanism behind all second-degree PD: incentive-compatible menus. The counter-intuitive result — firms sometimes spend money to make a product WORSE (damaged goods: disabled chip features, stripped Basic Economy) — because too small a quality gap lets high-WTP buyers buy the cheap version. Degrading the low version protects the high-margin sale. Spotify's Individual/Duo/Family menu is the same logic — versioning by household size, with the cheaper tiers capped.

Self-Sorting Captures the Entire Demand Curve

Highly price-inelastic. Cinephiles & high-income households want 4K HDR — they pay $22.99+/mo and refuse to watch ads.
Highly price-elastic. Budget households & students — captured by the $6.99 ad-supported tier (~70M monthly active users by 2024).

To fund $17B+/yr of content like Stranger Things, Netflix uses the identical core product to monetize the bottom of the curve without sacrificing the margins at the top.

The payoff of versioning: one product, a menu of prices, and the tiers let buyers self-sort along the demand curve. The inelastic top (cinephiles) is monetized at $22.99; the elastic tail (students, budget households) is captured at $6.99 with ads — revenue Netflix would otherwise leave on the table. Ties straight back to elasticity: charge the inelastic segment more, the elastic segment less, without a single price cut for everyone.

The “Stripped” Economy — Damaged Goods

The flip side of versioning: a firm will deliberately make the cheap version worse — spending nothing, or even money, to degrade it — so high-WTP buyers refuse to trade down and pay for the premium tier.

Then. 1840s railways ran roofless third-class cars. Dupuit (1849): “not to hurt the poor, but to frighten the rich.”

Now. Ever-tighter “stripped” economy seats and Basic Economy keep the cheap tier just painful enough to upsell.

Now, in software — Spotify Free

Same catalogue as Premium. What Free removes:

ads between tracks · no offline downloads · no playing songs in any order · no lossless audio · no group session · no queue control

Restoring every one of those costs Spotify \$0.

Software makes Dupuit's point unanswerable. Ripping a roof off a carriage at least saved timber; letting you play a song in the order you like has a marginal cost of exactly zero. So the limit cannot be about cost — it can only be there to protect the Premium fare.

Moved to CLOSE the versioning block rather than interrupt it, because damaged goods is the punchline of versioning, not an aside on the way to it: students should first see a menu screen correctly (Duo vs Family), then see one break, and only then learn that firms will actively degrade the cheap tier to keep the screen working. The three panels are one argument at three technology levels. Dupuit 1849 is the founding observation and still the clearest sentence anyone has written about PD — the third-class carriage had no roof in order to frighten the rich into first class, not to punish the poor. Modern hardware version: Basic Economy and the recurring ultra-dense seat proposals, where the airline spends real money to make the cheap seat worse. The Spotify panel is the strongest of the three and worth the most time, because it closes the escape route students reach for. When you show the railway they will say the roofless car was CHEAPER TO BUILD, and they are right, which muddies the argument — degradation and cost-saving are confounded. Software separates them perfectly. Ad-free playback, offline downloads, playing a song in the order you chose, lossless audio: the marginal cost of granting every one of these to a free user is zero, and in the case of ads Spotify actually gives up revenue by NOT showing them to Premium users. So there is no cost story left. The only remaining explanation for the limits is the one the theory predicts: they exist to stop a high-WTP listener from being satisfied by the free tier. Ask the class directly — what would happen to Premium subscriptions if Free allowed offline downloads tomorrow? They will get there themselves. Tie back explicitly: this is why the gap between Duo and Family is not just a price gap but a FEATURE gap (account count, same-address rule, Kids access). Widening the quality distance between tiers is how a firm relaxes the incentive-compatibility constraint it was fighting two slides ago.

Problem. A studio ships one game in two editions: Standard, and Deluxe (skins, season pass, early access). The extras are digital, so \(MC = 0\) for either edition. Two equally common buyers, types unobservable. What menu \((P_{Std}, P_{Dlx})\) maximizes profit?

Willingness to payStandardDeluxe
Casual gamer\$40\$50
Superfan\$60\$110

The casual gamer barely values the extras (\$40→\$50). The superfan wants them badly (\$60→\$110). That gap is what makes screening possible.

1. Price the Standard. Ask: what is the most the casual gamer will pay? \$40 — take all of it. \(P_{Std} = \$40\). (the low type's IR)

2. Price the Deluxe. Careful — the superfan could just buy the Standard and pocket \$60 − \$40 = \$20. So the Deluxe must leave them at least \$20: \(110 - P_{Dlx} \geq 20\), so \(P_{Dlx} = \$90\). (the high type's IC)

3. Profit. \$40 + \$90 = \$130 per pair of buyers.

4. Compare to one edition. Best single-edition play is Deluxe at \$110, superfan only — \$110. (Deluxe at \$50 to both earns just \$100.) The menu wins by \$20.

The \$20 is the information rent. Perfect PD would take \$40 + \$110 = \$150. The menu gets \$130, because to stop the superfan buying down the studio must let them keep \$20. That is the standing price of not being able to tell your customers apart — and the superfan is paid it for volunteering the truth.

Quantity Discounts

Firms often use quantity discounts to price-discriminate — a lower per-unit price for buyers who purchase larger quantities. It is the workhorse of second-degree PD.

It is indirect PD: the firm posts the menu and each consumer group self-selects the package meant for it — the price offered to a group must be the one that group chooses.

It rides on incentive compatibility (IC): the deal aimed at one type must be the one that type actually prefers — no buyer profitably grabs another type's package.

The catch: discount too steeply on the large quantity and you cannibalize — the low-demand buyer abandons the small package and grabs the bulk deal meant for the high-demand buyer. IC breaks. We are about to watch this happen on purpose.

Concept overview for quantity discounts (second-degree PD). Three ideas: (1) it is indirect PD — the firm can't assign prices, it posts a menu and lets buyers self-select; (2) the menu must be incentive-compatible — each type prefers its own package; (3) the design tension — a bulk discount that is too generous cannibalizes the high-margin small-package sale because the low-demand buyer defects to the bulk deal. The next slides work the Lay's chips example geometrically.

§12.4 — the central tool

Third-Degree (Group) PD

The Ch 11 inverse-elasticity rule, applied per segment: \(L_i = -\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{D,i}}\)

Segmenting — Third-Degree PD

Follow the information. First-degree needed every buyer’s own WTP — unobtainable. Second-degree needed only the distribution of types. Third-degree needs least of all: one observable label that correlates with elasticity. Less information, less extraction — and far easier to actually run.

When to use it

1. Market power + can prevent resale.

2. Customers have different demand curves.

3. The firm can identify groups (not individuals) with different price sensitivities before purchase.

Third-degree extracts more surplus than a single-price monopolist — but less than first-degree (it sorts groups, not individuals).

Each group’s markup then follows the per-segment inverse-elasticity rule \(L_i = -1/\varepsilon_{D,i}\). The next four slides are the labels firms actually use.

Segmenting / third-degree PD overview, merged from the two 1116 'Direct Price Discrimination' slides. Key points: it is DIRECT PD on GROUPS (not individuals) using identifiable attributes; the three conditions (market power + no resale, heterogeneous demand, group-identifiable-before-purchase); the surplus ranking (more than single-price monopoly, less than first-degree); and the per-segment inverse-elasticity rule (CalculusCorner 12.1) that follows. The opening line is deliberately about INFORMATION rather than a definition: first-degree needs each buyer's WTP, second-degree needs the distribution of types, third-degree needs only one observable label. That ordering IS Pigou's taxonomy and it is the chapter's spine, so restate it here rather than defining "groups not individuals" for a third time -- pd-third and the student-discount slide both used to repeat it, and no longer do. The examples were also pulled from this slide because the four slides that follow ARE the examples.

Third-Degree — Different Price to an Observable Group

Spotify. \$12.99 Individual vs \$6.99 Student. Students have higher elasticity; a .edu check prevents arbitrage. Charge them full price and you lose them entirely.

The logic is always elasticity: charge the inelastic group more, the elastic group less. Segment by an observable trait — age, student status, location, timing — and stop resale.

Third-degree. The seller observes your group and assigns the price. You cannot opt in.

Second-degree. The seller posts a menu and you pick. Your choice reveals your type.

Same elasticity logic driving both — the difference is who does the sorting. This is the most-tested distinction in the chapter.

The 2nd-vs-3rd distinction is the single most-tested classification (Poll 2 next). Third = seller observes a verifiable group trait. Second = seller offers a menu, buyer self-selects. The elasticity logic is the engine of both. Same molecule across countries (Ozempic) is also third-degree — tie back.

Third-Degree — the Student Discount

A segment is only worth defining if you can verify it. The .edu check is doing the real work here — it is the arbitrage fence. No fence, no segment: one resold login and the \$6.99 tier becomes everyone’s price.

Third-Degree — Student Platforms (UNiDAYS)

Platforms like UNiDAYS gate deals (HP, Microsoft 365) behind a verified student ID. The long-run twist: once students learn these tools, they keep using them at work — becoming more inelastic later, when they'll pay full price.

UNiDAYS/Student Beans are pure third-degree segmentation by a verifiable trait (enrollment). The strategic kicker is dynamic: discounting students today builds habit and lock-in, lowering their elasticity tomorrow — so the "loss" on student pricing is really an investment in future inelastic demand. Ties to switching costs earlier in the deck.

Third-Degree — Movie Tickets (by Age)

Movie theaters charge different ticket prices for children, adults, and seniors — age is the observable trait, and the ticket is checked at the door (no resale).

Age-based pricing is the textbook third-degree example: kids and seniors have lower WTP / higher elasticity (more substitutes for their time), so a discount fills seats that would otherwise sit empty — at near-zero marginal cost — without cutting the adult price. The age check at the door is the arbitrage barrier.

Third-Degree — Airline Fares

Airlines charge varying fares to leisure (elastic) vs business (inelastic) travelers.

  • Prices rise as the departure date nears (late buyers = business).
  • Lower fares on non-peak days & with advance booking (leisure).
  • The ticket must be used by the purchaser — arbitrage blocked.
Airlines run the most sophisticated third-degree (and intertemporal) PD on earth: fences like advance-purchase requirements, Saturday-night stays, and non-refundable/non-transferable tickets sort business (inelastic, book late, won't stay the weekend) from leisure (elastic, flexible). Same seat, very different fare — and the named, non-transferable ticket stops resale.

How Much Should Each Segment Be Charged?

A segmenting monopolist treats each segment as its own market, so the Lerner index sets the optimal markup per segment:

\(\dfrac{P - MC}{P} = -\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_D}\)

Solve it segment by segment. At the Disneyland optimum:

Travelers: \(\varepsilon_D = -1.83\)

Locals: \(\varepsilon_D = -2.43\)

Two steps, no algebra tricks. Take the markup, then back out the price with \(P = \dfrac{MC}{1 - L}\):

Travelers: \(L_T = \tfrac{1}{1.83} = 0.545 \Rightarrow P_T = \tfrac{100}{0.455} = \$220\)

Locals: \(L_L = \tfrac{1}{2.43} = 0.412 \Rightarrow P_L = \tfrac{100}{0.588} = \$170\)

Elasticity alone pins both prices — \$220 and \$170, the same answer as the \(MR = MC\) solve. Travelers pay ~30% more, and \(MC\) was identical for both.

Calculus Corner 12.1 — Inverse-Elasticity Rule, Per Segment

With \(K\) non-arbitrageable segments and constant \(MC\), maximize

\[ \pi = \sum_i (P_i - MC)\,Q_i(P_i) \]

Each segment's FOC is independent of the others:

\[ Q_i + (P_i - MC)\,Q_i' = 0 \]

Multiply by \(P_i/Q_i\) and rearrange:

\[ L_i = \frac{P_i - MC}{P_i} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{D,i}} \]

Equivalently \(P_i = \dfrac{MC}{1 + 1/\varepsilon_{D,i}}\). The rule from Ch 11 simply fires \(K\) times in parallel — once per segment.

The more-elastic segment gets the smaller markup. Students pay less because their demand is more elastic; business travelers pay more because theirs is not.

The only difference from Ch 11: Ch 11 had one segment, Ch 12 has \(K\). Same engine, run per group — segments don't interact because there's no arbitrage between them.

Calculus Corner 12.1 — sidebarred per the book's voice-protection rule. The key structural fact: because segments can't arbitrage, the cross-partials vanish and the firm solves \(K\) independent Ch 11 problems. The payoff is the same inverse-elasticity rule per segment. The pedagogical headline never changes from Ch 11 — markup is set by elasticity, not cost — only now it's a vector of markups. Walkthroughs 12.4–12.5 plug in Spotify segment numbers.

Heads Up 12.3 — PD welfare is ambiguous

PD does not always reduce welfare. If PD lets the firm serve a market it would otherwise abandon, total surplus rises (output expands). If it only redistributes CS→PS, surplus is unchanged. If it cuts output (rare), surplus falls.

The Pink Tax: Price Discrimination — or the Evidence?

Hypothesis 1 — Price Discrimination. Firms charge women higher markups because their demand is less price-elastic (lower willingness to switch brands).

Hypothesis 2 — Cost Difference. Recent studies on goods (e.g. shampoo) challenge the PD story:

  • Moshary et al.: comparing the same brand with the same leading ingredients, the gap vanished or reversed — women's versions were cheaper ~60% of the time.
  • Barnes & Brounstein: econometrics find women's demand is slightly more elastic than men's — the opposite of what PD requires.

Conclusion (goods): the evidence points to different production costs (pricier ingredients/packaging), not gender-based price discrimination.

Caveat: for services like haircuts the cause is still unsettled — could be higher cost or discrimination.

The lesson: a visible price gap is not proof of discrimination — you need evidence on costs and elasticities.

The empirical turn. H1 is the popular claim (PD via lower female elasticity). H2 + evidence: Moshary and coauthors find no systematic premium once brand and leading ingredients are held fixed -- women's items are often cheaper. Barnes & Brounstein estimate women's demand as slightly MORE elastic, which under PD would push female prices DOWN, not up. So for packaged goods the gap is mostly cost/composition, not discrimination; services (haircuts) stay ambiguous because cost and WTP are hard to separate. Punchline ties back to "you can't read PD off the price tag."

Two-Part Tariff

A two-part tariff is a pricing strategy in which the payment has two components: a fixed fee \(F\) plus a per-unit price \(p\)  —  the customer pays \(F + p \times Q\).

The fixed fee gets you in the door; the per-unit price is charged on use:

Video game systems
fixed fee = console · per-unit = each game
Zipcar
fixed fee = annual membership · per-unit = hourly rental
Costco
fixed fee = annual membership · per-unit = each item
Amazon Prime
fixed fee = yearly charge · per-unit = any purchase
Overview of the two-part tariff: total payment = fixed fee F + per-unit price p times quantity. The four examples make the structure concrete -- a one-time/recurring access fee plus a usage charge. Costco is the sharpest of the four and worth a sentence: the membership is \$65 a year for Gold Star (\$130 Executive), and the warehouse runs famously thin margins on the goods themselves, so the fee is not an add-on to the profit, it substantially IS the profit. That is the two-part tariff in its purest observable form -- push p toward MC, then collect the surplus through F -- and it is the same Costco whose 50-pack opened the quantity-discount run earlier. (The old third card used a club cover charge plus per-drink pricing. Costco replaces it: cleaner economics, better callback, and no assumption about who in the room is 21.) The economics (next slides): set p near marginal cost so the buyer consumes efficiently, then use F to capture the consumer surplus. Three worked cases follow (gym, buffet, car wash), then Application 12.2 Disney.

Two-Part Tariffs — the Gym

Part 1: Membership = $300 / year
Part 2: each class = MC = $10 / class

The annual fee captures consumer surplus up front; the per-class price = marginal cost, so members keep using the gym efficiently instead of being priced out.

Two-part tariff = a fixed entry fee + a per-unit price. Set the per-unit price at MC so the buyer consumes the efficient quantity, then use the membership fee to scoop up the consumer surplus that efficient consumption generates. The gym membership + per-class (or included-class) structure is the textbook case.

Two-Part Tariffs — the Buffet

Part 1: entry = $30 / person
Part 2: each plate = MC = $0 / plate

Once the trays are cooked, unsold food is thrown out anyway — so the marginal cost of one more plate ≈ $0. The buffet charges $0 per plate and captures all the surplus in the entry fee.

The buffet is the MC≈0 special case of the two-part tariff. Because an extra plate from already-prepared food costs the restaurant almost nothing, charging per plate would only deter eating and shrink the experience customers are paying for. So Part 2 = $0 and the entire margin lives in the door price.

Application 12.2 — Disney's FastPass → Lightning Lane

A two-part tariff charges a fixed entry fee \(F\) (independent of use) plus a per-unit price \(p\). Disney is the canonical case — and it sharpened the structure in real time.

Until 2021, admission was the only price; in-park rides were free at the margin. Then Disney unbundled queue priority into paid Lightning Lane (\$10–\$35/ride). Now there are two prices: gate admission as \(F\) (\$169–\$209/day; up to the \$1,629 Incredi-Pass) and per-attraction Lightning Lane as \(p\).

Per-capita Parks revenue rose materially after the switch — and Universal, SeaWorld, and Six Flags copied it. Costco, Amazon Prime, AWS, and gyms use the same template.

Disney's gate fee \(F=\$169\)/day sweeps up the whole surplus triangle (rides at \(MC\approx\$0\)); post-2021 Lightning Lane adds p = $10–35/ride.

Disney is the cleanest two-part tariff story because it ran the natural experiment: from 2021 it converted free FastPass into priced Lightning Lane, turning a one-price product into an \(F + p\) structure, and per-cap revenue rose. The insight (Oi 1971, “A Disneyland Dilemma”): with homogeneous consumers a two-part tariff replicates first-degree PD — without reading minds. The entry fee is the surplus-extraction instrument; the per-unit price can sit near MC. CC 12.2 next proves \(p^*=MC\), \(F^*=CS(MC)\).

Calculus Corner 12.2 — The Two-Part Tariff Lagrangian

Identical consumers, demand \(q(p)\), \(CS(p) = \int_p^{p_{\max}} q(t)\,dt\). Maximize profit per consumer subject to participation:

\[ \mathcal{L} = F + (p - MC)\,q(p) + \lambda\,[CS(p) - F] \]

FOC in \(F\): \(1 - \lambda = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda = 1\) — participation binds. FOC in \(p\), using \(\partial CS/\partial p = -q\):

\[ q + (p - MC)\,q' - q = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (p - MC)\,q' = 0 \]

Since \(q' < 0\), the only solution is \(p = MC\).

\( p^{\ast} = MC, \qquad F^{\ast} = CS(MC) \)

Profit per consumer \(= CS(MC)\) — the entire competitive surplus. Output equals the competitive level, so there's no DWL; the entry fee converts what would have been CS into a lump-sum extraction.

This is why Disney charges high gate admission, Costco high membership, AWS large up-front commitments: \(F\) is the surplus-capture instrument; pricing usage near MC maximizes the surplus there is to capture.

Calculus Corner 12.2 — the beautiful trick. With homogeneous consumers, set per-unit price AT marginal cost (so the efficient quantity trades, zero DWL) and set the entry fee to the resulting consumer surplus (so the firm captures all of it). The Lagrangian makes it two clean FOCs: \(\lambda=1\) (participation binds) and \(p=MC\). The result \(p^*=MC,\ F^*=CS(MC)\) replicates first-degree PD. The intuition for why \(p>MC\) is suboptimal: raising \(p\) above MC shrinks the surplus pie faster than it adds per-unit margin, lowering the \(F\) you can charge. Heterogeneous consumers break this — W12.7.

Walkthrough 12.6 — The Unlimited Pass (Homogeneous)

Problem. Bluebikes is pricing a monthly plan. A representative rider's demand for rides is \(q(p) = 10 - p\); one more ride costs the system essentially nothing, so \(MC = 0\). Find the optimal two-part tariff \((F, p)\) and compare profit to charging a single price per ride.

Two-part optimum

From CC 12.2: \(p^{\ast} = MC = 0\), \(F^{\ast} = CS(0) = \tfrac12(10)(10) = \$50\).

Profit per consumer \(= 50 + 0\cdot 10 = \$50\). Output \(q = 10\) (the competitive level).

Compare & check

Uniform monopoly: \(\pi(p) = p(10 - p)\), \(p^{\ast} = 5\), \(q = 5\), \(\pi = \$25\), with \(CS = \$12.50\) left over.

Two-part doubles profit (\$50 vs \$25), drives \(CS \to 0\), and produces the efficient \(q = 10\). Total surplus rises \$37.50 → \$50 — and the firm takes all of it.

Takeaway. With homogeneous consumers, a two-part tariff replicates first-degree PD by a different mechanism: the fee is the surplus-capture instrument, and \(p = MC\) keeps the pie as large as possible. Charge for the pass, not the ride — which is exactly what every unlimited plan you own already does.

Five reveals on the canonical result, which is Walter Oi's 1971 "Disneyland Dilemma" — say the name and the paper, because the title used to carry it and the result deserves the attribution. The numbers: \(p = 0 = MC\), \(F = CS(0) = \$50\), profit \$50, double the \$25 uniform-monopoly profit from the same firm at the same cost. Note the efficiency too: output is the competitive \(q = 10\) rather than the monopoly \(q = 5\), total surplus rises from \$37.50 to \$50, and the firm captures every dollar of it. Re-framed from a theme park to an unlimited pass for a reason worth saying out loud: the optimum here is \(p^{\ast} = 0\), so the honest real-world match is a plan with NO per-use charge, not a park that still charges for rides. Bluebikes, a climbing-gym membership, a ski season pass and a transit pass are all the same object — a fee that captures the surplus and a marginal price of zero that keeps consumption efficient. Ask the class what would happen if Bluebikes charged \$3 a ride on top of the membership: rides fall below 10, the pie shrinks, and the firm can no longer charge as much for the pass. That is the whole intuition for why \(p = MC\) is not generosity. Numbers are stylised (a real Bluebikes month is nearer \$30 than \$50) — the shape is the point, and the shape is exact. The broader tie-in stands: "high membership, low or zero markup inside" is everywhere, from Costco to AWS to every streaming plan they pay for. Heterogeneous consumers break the clean result, which is W12.7 next.

Two-Part Tariff with Different Customer Demands

Hold the per-unit price at MC for now, and ask only what single fixed fee \(F\) to charge when the firm can't tell the two types apart.

(W12.7 next lets the firm choose \(p\) too — and the answer stops being \(p = MC\).)

Fee = A (the low type's surplus): both customers stay → collect \(2A\).

Fee = B (the high type's surplus): only the high type stays (A < B) → collect \(B\).

Charge whichever is larger: serve everyone at fee A for \(2A\), or exclude the low type and charge \(B\). The firm compares \(2A\) vs \(B\).

Sets up the heterogeneous two-part tariff (leads into Walkthrough 12.7). Per-unit price is still set at MC, so each type buys efficiently (Q_L, Q_H where each demand meets MC). The wrinkle: one fixed fee must serve a mixed population the firm cannot screen. Low type's surplus = A, high type's = B (B > A). Option 1: fee = A keeps both customers, yielding 2A. Option 2: fee = B drives out the low type (their surplus A < B), yielding B from the high type alone. The firm picks max(2A, B): serve-all vs exclude-the-low-type. Walkthrough 12.7 plugs in numbers and finds excluding the low type can win.

Walkthrough 12.7 — Heterogeneous Types: Include or Exclude?

Problem. Two equal types: \(q_A(p) = 10 - p\), \(q_B(p) = 6 - p\). \(MC = 0\). Set \(F\) low to keep both in, or high to extract from A and exclude B?

Strategy I — include both

\(F\) binds on B: \(F = (6-p)^2/2\). Per pair: \(\pi^{\text{I}} = (6-p)^2 + p(16-2p) = 36 + 4p - p^2\).

FOC: \(p^{\ast} = 2\), \(F^{\ast} = (4)^2/2 = 8\). \(\pi^{\text{I}} = 36 + 8 - 4 = \$40\).

Strategy II — exclude B

Charge \(F = CS_A(0) = (10)^2/2 = 50\), set \(p = 0\). A pays \$50; B walks. Per pair: \(\pi^{\text{II}} = \$50\).

Compare: \(50 > 40\). Excluding the low-WTP type wins — the fee that retains B (\$8) is only \(1/6\) of the fee A alone will pay (\$50).

How lopsided must the crowd be to change that? With \(k\) low types per high type, inclusion overtakes exclusion at \(k \approx 1.6\) — once low-demand buyers are about 62% of the market.

Takeaway. With heterogeneous types the firm faces a coverage-vs-extraction tradeoff — and excluding the low type can dominate. This is why Equinox (\$300/mo) and Disney Incredi-Pass (\$1,629) deliberately don't serve the mass market, while Costco (\$65) does. Each picks its spot on the trade by the type distribution it faces.

And notice what broke. Strategy I sets \(p^{\ast} = 2\) while \(MC = 0\) — the \(p = MC\) rule from CC 12.2 fails here. Once the fee is capped by the low type's surplus, the firm stops leaning on \(F\) and starts taking margin on A's larger volume. \(p = MC\) survives only with identical customers, or when you exclude down to one type (Strategy II, where \(p = 0 = MC\) returns).

Five reveals — the heterogeneity complication that breaks the clean CC 12.2 result. Strategy I (serve both) maxes at \(p=2\), \(F=8\), \(\pi=\$40\). Strategy II (exclude B) charges A alone \(F=\$50\), \(\pi=\$50\) — and wins. The intuition: keeping B in forces a low fee on BOTH consumers, and the volume gain from B doesn't compensate. The real-world payoff: high-end-only firms (Equinox, Incredi-Pass) vs mass-market (Costco) are choosing different points on the coverage-extraction trade, driven by their type distributions. THE POINT NOT TO SKIP, because students will otherwise think this slide contradicts the last three. Strategy I's optimal per-unit price is \(p^{\ast} = 2\), and marginal cost is ZERO. So \(p > MC\), and the clean CC 12.2 result -- price at marginal cost, take everything through the fee -- has just failed. Say why: with identical consumers the fee could absorb the entire surplus, so any markup only shrank the pie you were about to collect. Here the fee is capped by the LOW type's surplus, which is much smaller than the high type's, so \(F\) is a blunt instrument; raising \(p\) sacrifices a little of that small fee but earns margin on A's much larger volume, and on net it pays. Note too that \(p = MC\) comes BACK under Strategy II, because excluding B leaves one type and the CC 12.2 logic applies again. The rule was never about two-part tariffs as such, it was about homogeneity. Then the threshold, which turns "it depends on the distribution" into arithmetic: with one high type and \(k\) low types, inclusion overtakes exclusion at about \(k = 1.6\), so low-demand buyers must be roughly 62% of the market before serving everyone wins. Sanity-check it against the real firms on the slide -- Costco faces a market that is overwhelmingly ordinary households, so it includes; Equinox and the Incredi-Pass are deliberately fishing in a pool where the high type is common enough that exclusion pays. Worth connecting back explicitly: W12.3 had the identical structure on the quantity-menu side, where the firm also did better dropping the low type unless Light buyers were more than two thirds of the market. Same question, same shape of answer, twice -- coverage versus extraction is the recurring trade of §12.3 and §12.5, not a quirk of either example.

§12.6

Bundling

When valuations are negatively correlated, the package beats the parts

Bundling

Bundling — a pricing strategy in which a firm sells two or more products together at a single price.

When to use it

1. The firm has market power and can prevent resale.

2. Consumers' demand for the second product is negatively correlated with their demand for the first.

Everyday example: Internet + streaming sold together — e.g. \$70/month for Internet plus a set of streaming services, instead of \$40 Internet + \$15 Netflix + \$15 HBO Max + \$8 Disney+ à la carte.

Formal bundling intro (a second-degree PD tool). Definition: two-or-more products at one price. Two conditions: (1) market power + no resale; (2) negatively-correlated cross-product valuations — the key driver, demonstrated numerically in the next two slides with Madison and Dakota. The Internet+streaming line is the everyday hook.

Bundling — Positively Correlated Demand

Two consumers — Madison and Dakota — shopping for streaming. First, positively correlated demand: whoever values one service more values the other more. (Notflix = a fictional service streaming obscure movies.)

Positively Correlated Valuations (per subscriber-month)

NetflixNotflixBundle
Madison\$18\$2\$20
Dakota\$20\$3\$23

Sell separately: \$18 Netflix + \$2 Notflix, both buy both → \$40 total. (\$20 for Netflix would lose Madison; \$3 for Notflix would too.)

Bundle: \$20 bundle, both buy → \$40 total.

No benefit to bundling when valuations are positively correlated — both consumers rank the two services the same way, so one price captures no extra surplus.

Positive-correlation case: Madison and Dakota both value Netflix high and Notflix low. Separate sale: Netflix at $18 (both buy) = $36, Notflix at $2 (both buy) = $4, total $40. Bundle at $20 (both buy) = $40. Identical — bundling adds nothing because the WTP spread isn't compressed when valuations move together.

Bundling — Negatively Correlated Demand

Same market, now negatively correlated demand — the consumer who values Netflix more values Notflix less.

Negatively Correlated Valuations (per subscriber-month)

NetflixNotflixBundle
Madison\$18\$3\$21
Dakota\$20\$2\$22

Sell separately: \$18 Netflix + \$2 Notflix → \$40 total — identical to the positive-correlation case. So the entire difference shows up on the bundle side.

Bundle: \$21 bundle, both buy → \$42 total (+\$2).

Bundling raises revenue \$40 → \$42 with negative correlation: it compresses the spread in total WTP (both value the bundle near \$21–22), so a single price captures more.

Negative-correlation case: now Madison/Dakota disagree on which service is worth more, so their BUNDLE valuations bunch together ($21 vs $22). Separate sale still yields $40 (same per-product constraints). But the bundle at $21 captures both for $42 — the +$2 is the Adams-Yellen gain from negative cross-WTP correlation (formalized in CalculusCorner 12.3 / Walkthrough 12.8).

Pure vs Mixed Bundling

Pure bundling: the firm offers the products only as a bundle — no standalone option.

Mixed bundling: the firm lets consumers buy the products separately or as a bundle — whichever they prefer.

Example: fast-food value meals — order à la carte, or take the combo for less than the sum of its parts.

Why mixed can beat pure. Three buyers, \(MC=0\): two lopsided — \((100,0)\) and \((0,100)\) — and one balanced, \((60,60)\).

Separate \$240 < pure bundle at \$100 \$300 < mixed \$320: bundle \$120 for the balanced buyer, each app \$100 standalone for the lopsided ones. The extremes would refuse a \$120 bundle — standalones catch them anyway.

Pure vs mixed bundling. Pure = bundle-only (Microsoft 365 subscription). Mixed = bundle + standalones (Adobe single-app vs all-apps; fast-food combos vs a la carte). Mixed WEAKLY dominates pure (Adams-Yellen): never worse, since the firm can always price the standalones out of reach and reproduce pure bundling, but strictly better only when some buyers are lopsided enough to walk away from the bundle while still paying for one component. The three-buyer box is the demonstration, and it is worth doing because nothing else in the chapter shows mixed strictly winning. Two lopsided buyers value (100, 0) and (0, 100); one balanced buyer values (60, 60), so bundle values are 100, 100 and 120. Pure bundling has to choose: price at 120 and sell only to the balanced buyer for \$120, or price at 100 and sell to all three for \$300. Best pure is \$300. Mixed does better by splitting them up -- bundle at \$120 takes the balanced buyer, and a \$100 standalone price on each app takes the two lopsided buyers, for \$320. Check the incentives out loud: the lopsided buyer gets zero surplus from a \$120 bundle (their bundle value is only 100) so they genuinely refuse it, while \$100 for the one app they want is exactly worth it; the balanced buyer will not pay \$100 for a single app they value at 60, so they take the bundle. Nobody is misbehaving, and the firm collects from all three at different prices. That is the general lesson: pure bundling forces ONE price on a population whose bundle values differ, and standalones let the firm stop losing the buyers whose value is concentrated in one component. Worth flagging that W12.8's example does NOT show this -- there mixed ties pure at \$180, because its two types are symmetric extremes with identical bundle values, so the bundle already extracts every dollar available. Mixed adds nothing when there is nothing left to add. The McDonald's photo is the everyday illustration.

Bundling — the CD-Era “Forced Bundle”

The album bundles 1 hit with 11 filler tracks into one $15 product. Want just the hit? You still buy all 12.

How the bundle captures consumer surplus:

  • A fan who loves all 12 songs would gladly pay $15 — nothing extra captured.
  • A casual listener who wants only the hit also pays $15 — the firm captures the surplus she'd have kept if she could buy one song.

In the 1990s the marginal cost of pressing & shipping a CD was high — selling single songs wasn't worth it, so the bundle was the product.

Bundling is second-degree PD because it screens on WTP without observing type. The low-demand buyer who values only one song is forced to pay the full bundle price, surrendering surplus. Simplified for class: a bundle lets the firm charge one high price that the low-demand buyer still pays, transferring her surplus to the producer.

Tech Unbundles — iTunes à la Carte

The MP3 pushed the marginal cost of one more song to ≈ $0. iTunes (2003) sold songs individually at $0.99.

  • Consumer surplus ↑: the one-song buyer pays $0.99, not $15.
  • Producer revenue ↓: buyers who'd have paid $15 now grab just their 3 favorites = $2.97.

Unbundling handed surplus back to consumers — and collapsed total industry revenue.

Unbundling reverses the CD-era surplus grab: once a single song costs ~$0 to deliver, competition (and Apple) forces a la carte pricing, returning surplus to low-demand buyers and erasing the bundle premium. Technology (MC → 0) reshapes which PD strategy is feasible.

The “All-You-Can-Eat” Bundle — Spotify

A subscription is a new kind of bundle: instead of 12 songs, Spotify bundles all 100M songs for one flat monthly price.

Self-selection (versioning):

  • Low-WTP / price-sensitive → the free, ad-supported tier.
  • High-WTP → Premium ($12.99/mo) — ad-free, offline, any order.

It re-bundles the unbundled customer — a stable, high-value revenue stream from the highest-WTP listeners.

Spotify's tiers are textbook second-degree versioning: the free/ad tier and the $12.99 premium tier are the menu, and listeners self-select by WTP. The all-you-can-eat subscription also solves the unbundling revenue problem — it re-aggregates demand into a recurring, high-margin stream.

Pure vs Mixed Bundling — and Why It Pays

Pure bundling: only the package sells. Microsoft 365 — no standalone Word subscription.

Mixed bundling: bundle and standalones. Adobe — apps \$22.99 each, all-apps \$59.99.

Sets up CC 12.3 and W12.8. Microsoft 365 = pure (subscription is bundle-only); Adobe CC, Disney+/Hulu, and the 2024–2026 fast-food meal deals (\$5 McDonald's, Wendy's Biggie) = mixed. The figure plots consumers as points \((v^X, v^Y)\): a single bundle price captures everyone above a diagonal line, and when valuations are negatively correlated the bundle WTP distribution is compressed toward the mean, letting one price grab more buyers. Calculus Corner 12.3 — Adams-Yellen Bundling

Two equal-mass types, valuations \((v^X, v^Y)\), \(MC = 0\); bundle WTP \(V = v^X + v^Y\). Three strategies, ranked from worst to best:

1. Separate sales — set \(p_X\) and \(p_Y\) on their own.

↓  beaten by pure bundling when \(\text{Cov}(v^X, v^Y) < 0\)

2. Pure bundling — one price \(P\) for the bundle only. Negatively-correlated WTP shrinks the bundle's spread, so a single price catches more buyers.

↓  never beats mixed bundling — weakly

3. Mixed bundling — the bundle and the standalones. Extracts from lopsided types who'd skip the bundle but pay for one component.  ← never worse
Strictly better only when such types exist — see the three-buyer case in §12.6. In W12.8 it merely ties pure.

Why bundling helps. \(\text{Var}(V) = \text{Var}(v^X) + \text{Var}(v^Y) + 2\,\text{Cov}\). When \(\text{Cov} < 0\), the bundle's WTP is less dispersed than its parts — a tighter distribution lets one price near the median capture more.

The sign of the correlation is the whole test.

Adams & Yellen (1976, QJE).

The bundling ranking in one chain: separate sales ≤ pure bundling (when valuations are negatively correlated) ≤ mixed bundling. Note both links are WEAK inequalities and say so, because the slide used to read 'beaten by mixed bundling always', which overstates it. Mixed can never be worse -- price the standalones out of reach and you have reproduced pure bundling -- but it is strictly better only when some buyers are lopsided enough to refuse the bundle while still buying one component. Every example in this chapter happens to be a tie on that second link except the three-buyer case shown back at the pure-vs-mixed slide, which is the one place students can see mixed strictly win (\$320 against \$300). Mechanism is variance reduction: Var(V) = Var(v^X) + Var(v^Y) + 2Cov, so Cov < 0 shrinks the bundle's WTP spread and one price does better than two. Mixed bundling then adds standalone options to catch the extreme types a pure bundle would lose. The sign of the correlation is the whole test (Adams & Yellen 1976 QJE). Microsoft Office is the canonical case — Word-heavy and Excel-heavy users have negatively-correlated WTP. W12.8 makes it numerical.

Walkthrough 12.8 — Pure Bundling, Negatively-Correlated WTP

Problem. Word (W) and Excel (E), \(MC = 0\). Two equal types — A: \((v^W, v^E) = (80, 10)\); B: \((10, 80)\). Compare separate sale vs pure bundling.

Takeaway. The bundle compresses both types' WTP to a common \$90, so one price captures both. Microsoft's Office bundle survives because writers and analysts have negatively correlated WTPs — neither pays \$80 for the other's app, but both pay \$90 for the package. Same logic: Disney+/Hulu, the fast-food meal deal.

Would mixed bundling do better here? No — it ties at \$180. Both types value the bundle at exactly \$90, so one price already takes every available dollar and standalones have nothing left to catch. Mixed only pulls ahead when bundle values differ — the three-buyer case in §12.6.

Five reveals — the Adams-Yellen result made numerical. The setup is engineered so valuations are perfectly negatively correlated: A loves Word, B loves Excel. Separate sale forces \$80 prices that each capture only one type (\$160 total). The bundle, valued at \$90 by both, captures both at one price (\$180). The covariance is explicitly \(-1225<0\), satisfying the CC 12.3 condition. The +12.5% comes entirely from compression. Microsoft 365, Disney+/Hulu, and meal deals are all this mechanism — bundle goods whose buyers have opposing tastes. Pre-empt the obvious question, because CC 12.3 ranks mixed at the top and this example does not show that: mixed bundling here earns exactly \$180, the same as pure. The reason is that both types value the bundle at precisely \$90, so total available surplus is \$180 and the single bundle price already captures all of it -- there is no buyer left over for a standalone to pick up. It is a tie, not a contradiction, and it is worth saying so explicitly rather than letting a sharp student think the ranking is wrong. The case where mixed strictly wins needs buyers whose BUNDLE values differ, which is the three-buyer example on the pure-vs-mixed slide (\$320 vs \$300).

Theory and Data 12.2 — Cable TV, and the Cousin of Bundling: Tying

Crawford-Yurukoglu (2012, AER)

Across 33 metro markets, bundling channels into tiers (vs à-la-carte) reduces consumer surplus ~5% on average — but total-welfare effects range \(-1.7\%\) to \(+6.0\%\), because tiering keeps niche networks alive that wouldn't survive standalone.

Bundling welfare is empirically mixed — sign depends on industry demand and supply structure.

Tying — procompetitive or not?

Tying: sell A only if the buyer also takes B. Microsoft 1998 (IE + Windows); EU fined Apple €1.8B (Mar 2024) for App Store anti-steering; Apple-Epic ruling (Apr 2025).

Two theories: Adams-Yellen (procompetitive surplus capture) vs leverage (use dominance in A to foreclose B). Modern antitrust weighs both empirically.

Two faces of §12.6. Crawford-Yurukoglu is the empirical anchor: cable tiering cuts CS ~5% but total welfare is ambiguous because tiering subsidizes niche-channel entry — same “welfare is empirical” lesson as Heads Up 12.3. §12.7

Network Goods & Two-Sided Platforms

Cross-side network effects: the price structure matters, not just the level

What Is a Network Good?

A network good is one whose usefulness depends on the existence of a physical, virtual, or social network — its value comes from the other people using it.

Examples you carry every day:

  Smartphones

  Streaming apps

  VR headsets

  Voice assistants

Ch 11 asked why this creates market power. §12.7 asks the harder question: once you have a network good, how do you price it — and to whom?

Strip away the network and the good loses its value — a phone with nobody to call, a feed with nobody to follow.

Deliberately a REFRESH, not new material: Ch 11 already defined a network good with the games-console example, where it appeared as one of the five sources of market power (your first console pulls in your friends, which pulls in your next console). Say that out loud so the repetition reads as intentional, then make the pivot on the key-result box — Ch 11 asked why network goods confer market power, §12.7 asks how a firm holding one should PRICE it. That is the whole reason the section exists. Work the four examples by asking which network each one depends on, because the three kinds are worth separating: a smartphone needs a physical network (towers, cables) plus a social one (people to contact); streaming needs a virtual network (catalogue, recommendation data, the brand ecosystem); VR needs a virtual network of content and an emerging social one, which is precisely why it keeps stalling — the hardware is fine, the network is thin; voice assistants need a virtual ecosystem of skills and devices. VR is the useful case to dwell on, since it shows a good can be technically excellent and still fail for want of a network. The closing line is the intuition pump for everything that follows: value is not intrinsic to the object. A phone with nobody to call is a paperweight. Once students accept that, the upward-sloping demand segment and the tipping point stop looking like tricks.

Network Effects: Why Size Matters

Same-side (direct). More users like you make the good better for you. WhatsApp is worth more when your friends are on it.

Cross-side (indirect). More users on the other side make the good better for you. More Airbnb hosts help guests; more guests attract hosts.

Cross-side effects are why growth feeds on itself: each side pulls the other in. And they are why the firm's problem stops being “what price?” and becomes what price to each side — the price structure.

Neither side is worth much alone — each one is the other's product.

Size is not a result of a good product here — for a network good, size is part of the product. That is what makes these markets tip.

The bridge slide, and the one distinction the rest of §12.7 depends on. Deliberately NOT a copy of the 1116 mechanism slide: that slide's content is the externality definition, the fact that the benefit reaches all users, and diminishing returns — and #/net-externality on the very next page already does all three with a better figure. Repeating it here would be a near-duplicate. What 1116 has that we lacked is the same-side / cross-side split, which is the actual hinge of the section. Draw the difference with two questions. Same-side: does WhatsApp get better when MORE PEOPLE LIKE YOU join? Yes — direct, one population. Cross-side: does Airbnb get better for a guest when more HOSTS join? Yes — and that is a different population entirely. Uber, credit cards, app stores, dating apps, ad-supported media and consoles are all cross-side; a fax machine or WhatsApp is mostly same-side. Have them classify three or four out loud, because the classification determines the pricing problem. Then land the consequence, which is the reason this slide exists before the theory: with only same-side effects there is one population and therefore one price to set. With cross-side effects there are two populations, each side's participation is an input into the other side's value, and the firm has TWO prices to choose. So the object of choice becomes the relative price across sides — the structure — which is exactly the Rochet-Tirole-Weyl result later in the section. The closing line is the compressed version: for a network good, size is not evidence of quality, it is a component of quality, which is why these markets tip to one winner instead of settling into shares.

Network Externalities

  • Network externality: a good becomes more valuable as more people join (HuskyHive, the AI study network two slides from now, gains value with every Husky who joins).
  • Network effect: a new user benefits from existing users and simultaneously raises the good's value for everyone — the external benefit reaches all users, including the first.
  • Diminishing returns: each additional user adds less external benefit as the network grows.
  • Reverse network effect: when a user leaves, value falls for those who remain — not a negative externality, just the removal of a benefit.

Each new subscriber shifts demand out (DP→DS); the gap is the external benefit the market price ignores.

Network goods (§12.7). The defining feature: value rises with the number of users, so one person's joining confers an external benefit on everyone already in. The demand curve shifts right as the network grows; the wedge between the price-taker's private demand and the social demand is the external benefit. Diminishing returns eventually set in, and churn (reverse network effects) removes the benefit rather than imposing a true negative externality. Why the Leader Pulls Away — Metcalfe’s Law

Three candidate scalings for the value of a network of size \(n\).

Metcalfe’s Law. \(n\) users make \(\binom{n}{2} = \dfrac{n(n-1)}{2} \approx \dfrac{n^2}{2}\) possible connections, so \(V(n) \propto n^2\). Double the users, roughly quadruple the value.

The part that matters for pricing. \(V'(n) \propto n\) — each new user adds more than the last one did. Marginal value is increasing, not diminishing.

That single fact is why the demand curve two slides from now bends the wrong way, why there is a tipping point at all, and why a merger in a network market is worth more than the sum of its parts.

Honest caveat (Odlyzko–Tilly). You do not connect to everyone. Empirically value is nearer \(n\log n\) — still super-linear, so the tipping logic survives, but \(n^2\) overstates it.

Adapted from the 1116 ch19 deck, with one addition that earns it a place in a theory course: the DERIVATIVE. Students meet Metcalfe as a slogan ("value grows as n squared"), but the pricing content is in \(V'(n) \propto n\) — marginal value INCREASING in n. Say why that is strange out loud: every other good in this course has diminishing marginal value, which is what makes demand slope down. Here it does the opposite, and that is the whole reason the next few slides can show a demand curve with an upward-sloping segment, a tipping point, and two stable equilibria. Without this slide those pictures look like a trick. Do the arithmetic once, concretely: 10 users give 45 links, 20 users give 190 -- barely double the users, more than four times the connections. Then the honest correction, because the n-squared claim is overstated and students should not carry it as fact: most users never interact with most others, so Odlyzko and Tilly's n log n (a Zipf-style argument) is the better empirical fit. Emphasise that the QUALITATIVE conclusion is unchanged -- n log n is still super-linear, so marginal value still rises and the leader still compounds. Only the magnitude of the merger premium changes. Tie forward: this convexity plus fixed capacity is what generates the S-shaped network demand in Fig 12.7, and it is why the platform's problem in §12.7 is about getting the loop STARTED rather than about the price level.

Fixed-Capacity Network Good

  • Short-run supply is vertical — capped by the firm's fixed investment (capacity). Example: an airline with a fixed number of planes.
  • Capacity expands in large increments (S0 → S1).
  • Each capacity level has its own demand curve, reflecting the higher value of a larger network — as it expands the network effect raises demand, partly offset by the price effect.
When capacity is fixed (planes, server farms, spectrum), short-run supply is a vertical line and grows only in lumps. Each capacity level pairs with its own demand curve because a bigger installed base is worth more to every user. Expanding capacity (S0 to S1) sells more, but the network effect (demand out to D1) keeps price from collapsing. Figure: 1116 Worth Publishers.

The Unique Network Demand Curve

Value rises as more people subscribe, so the curve has two parts:

1. Upward-sloping (Core Users). Network effect > price effect. Core users (least price-sensitive) build the network, so price rises as it grows.

2. Downward-sloping (Casual Users). The network matures, the network effect weakens (diminishing returns), so price effect > network effect — casual users buy as price drops.

The signature shape of a network good. Early on, adding users raises willingness-to-pay faster than the larger quantity lowers it (network effect > price effect), so the demand curve slopes UP through the core-user region. Once the network is large, diminishing network benefits let the ordinary price effect take over and the curve bends back DOWN through the casual-user region. The firm rides up the curve while building, then down it while harvesting. Figure: 1116 Worth Publishers.

Pricing Over the Network Life Cycle

1. New network. Price low to attract the first users.

2. Growing. Prices rise — a good used by more people is worth more.

3. Mature. Prices fall to sell a greater quantity.

HuskyHive’s answer, part 1. Don’t charge on day one — at stage 1 the product barely works, because it is the network. Give it away, raise the price as the study groups and the training data thicken, then cut it once the campus saturates. The low launch price is an investment in the network, not a discount.

The pricing path traces the network demand curve. Launch low to seed adoption (penetration pricing); raise price as the installed base and the network effect grow; then cut price once the network matures to reach the price-sensitive casual users. VR headsets illustrate it: the Rift DK1 launched at $300, usage climbed and plateaued, and nominal headset prices drifted down across 2016-2022. Figures: VR usage + Oculus Rift + headset price history. This is the first of the two answers the HuskyHive slide promised, so close the loop out loud. The reason not to charge on day one is not generosity or growth-hacking folklore -- it is that for a network good the early product is genuinely worth little, since the network IS the product, so a high launch price is charging a lot for the weakest version you will ever ship. Price low to seed, raise as the installed base and the training data grow, cut at maturity to reach the price-sensitive tail. Then flag the discipline problem: stage 3 is where firms get greedy instead of cutting, which is exactly the Uber take-rate story at the end of the section (15-20% up to 26.9%). Market Equilibrium for a Network Good

  • Equilibrium on the downward slope: usually reached on the downward-sloping part of the network demand curve, after most network effects are realized (point d, where demand meets MC).
  • Volatility: not all network goods reach or sustain equilibrium — expansion and decline can be rapid.
  • Economies of scale: large fixed costs (software, infrastructure) + low marginal costs (one more user ≈ free) → big economies of scale, like monopolies and oligopolies.
Network goods settle on the DOWNWARD slope of the network demand curve (point d, demand = MC), after the network effect has mostly played out. The cost structure mirrors a monopoly/oligopoly: huge fixed costs to build the platform, near-zero marginal cost to add a user -> large economies of scale. fc_mc.png: data-center fixed cost ($7-12M per megawatt) vs ~$5 marginal cost per user.

Finding an Equilibrium

Equilibrium = the downward-sloping network demand curve meets MC (point d).

Tipping point (critical mass) — point b: the quantity at which network effects are strong enough to keep the network growing. It must expand from the origin to b to sustain growth.

Vicious cycle: fails to reach the tipping point → users leave → value falls for others → sharp decline and likely failure.

Virtuous cycle: past the tipping point → network effects push demand right → more value → expansion until equilibrium.

Equilibrium is where the DOWNWARD portion crosses MC (point d). The catch is getting there: the network must first claw from the origin past the tipping point b (critical mass). Below b, the network effect is too weak -- churn feeds a vicious cycle toward collapse. Above b, the network effect dominates -- a virtuous cycle pushes demand right to equilibrium. Whether a network lives or dies hinges on crossing b.

Virtuous and Vicious Cycles — Real Cases

VICIOUS

Vicious: below the tipping point, users leave → value falls for the rest → collapse (Google+ → 2019 shutdown; MySpace, Friendster, Vine).

Virtuous: past the tipping point, scale compounds → more value → more users (Amazon Prime; ChatGPT & AI assistants).

VIRTUOUS

The cycle made concrete, flanked by evidence. LEFT (vicious): Google+ and a graveyard of social networks that never crossed critical mass -- MySpace, Friendster, Vine, Yik Yak, Gowalla. RIGHT (virtuous): Amazon Prime's self-reinforcing scale and the explosive AI-assistant market led by ChatGPT. The curve in the middle is the mechanism: below the tipping point the network unravels; above it, network effects compound to equilibrium.

The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

Riders will not come without drivers; drivers will not come without riders. The platform needs both sides at once — and each is waiting for the other.

This is a coordination game with two stable equilibria — the empty platform and the full platform are both self-consistent. Nobody is behaving irrationally in the empty one. So the platform's first problem is not choosing a price, it is breaking a tie between equilibria — which is what a subsidy buys.

Subsidize a side

Uber guaranteed drivers an hourly rate before the riders existed — paying one side to show up first.

Borrow a network

Threads launched off Instagram’s 2B users and hit 275M in months — skip the cold start entirely.

Start tiny and dense

Airbnb began with air mattresses in the founders’ apartment, then one city at a time — small enough to tip.

HuskyHive’s answer, part 2. A student team has no adjacent network to borrow and no capital to subsidize with — so of the three, only start tiny and dense is actually available. One course, one semester. Critical mass in a single section is far smaller than critical mass on a campus.

Solve the coordination problem once and the cross-side flywheel sustains itself — which is why the next slide’s subsidy is an investment, not a loss.

Without it, "subsidize the side with the stronger cross-side externality" arrives as an assertion. The 2316 framing is the middle box, and it is worth dwelling on: the empty platform is not a failure of rationality, it is an EQUILIBRIUM. Given that no drivers are online, not opening the app is a best response; given that no riders are hailing, not driving is a best response. Both the empty and the full configuration are self-consistent, so the platform is not solving a pricing problem first -- it is solving a selection problem between equilibria. Then the three solutions, which are worth having students classify rather than memorise: pay one side to arrive first (Uber's hourly guarantees, a direct transfer); import a solved network (Threads off Instagram -- the fastest app to 100M ever, because the coordination problem was already solved elsewhere); or shrink the problem until it tips (Airbnb's air mattresses, then city by city, because critical mass in ONE dense market is far smaller than critical mass globally). Ask which of the three is available to a platform with no adjacent product and no capital -- the answer is the third, which is why so many platforms look absurdly small at the start. Hand straight into the next slide: once you accept that the loop must be started, paying one side is obviously worth it if what you buy exceeds what you spend, and that trade-off is precisely the \$1-cut arithmetic there.

§12.8 — A Note on Arthur Cecil Pigou (1877–1959)

Pigou was Alfred Marshall's chosen successor at Cambridge, holding the Professor of Political Economy chair from 1908 to 1943. His 1920 The Economics of Welfare did three lasting things: it built the welfare-economics framework for asking when intervention raises social welfare; it introduced the externality; and it classified price discrimination into the first / second / third-degree taxonomy this whole chapter is built on.

That taxonomy is also the bridge to Chapter 17. The Pigouvian tax — a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost — internalizes negative externalities and restores efficiency. One economist shapes two parts of this book.

Chapter Summary

Pigou's taxonomy

First (know each buyer), second (know the distribution), third (observe a group label). Each needs less, extracts less.

Perfect PD

\(Q_{PD} = Q_C\), \(CS = 0\), \(DWL = 0\). Efficient and fully extractive. \(\pi_{PD} = TS_C\).

Third-degree ★

\(L_i = -1/\varepsilon_{D,i}\) per segment. More elastic → lower price. The Ch 11 rule, \(K\) times. But output may not move — and then total surplus falls.

Two-part tariff

Homogeneous: \(p^{\ast} = MC\), \(F^{\ast} = CS(MC)\). Replicates perfect PD. Heterogeneous: exclusion can win.

Bundling

Pure bundling beats separate sale iff \(\text{Cov}(v^X, v^Y) < 0\) (Adams-Yellen). Mixed is never worse — strictly better only if some buyers are lopsided enough to skip the bundle.

Two-sided platforms

Price structure reflects own elasticity + cross-side externality. Subsidize the strong-externality side.
