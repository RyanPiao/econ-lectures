From Price-Taker to Price-Maker

Ch 10 — perfect competition

  • Firm is a price-taker: \(\partial p/\partial q_i = 0\)
  • \(MR = p\), so profit-max sets \(p = MC\)
  • Free entry → \(p = \min ATC\), \(\pi = 0\)
  • Welfare theorem holds: \(Q^*\) maximizes total surplus

Ch 11 — monopoly

  • Firm is a price-maker: \(\partial p/\partial q < 0\)
  • \(MR < p\), so profit-max sets \(MR = MC\)
  • Price sits above \(MC\) — the markup
  • Welfare theorem fails: \(Q_M < Q_C\), deadweight loss returns

One assumption changes everything. A monopolist faces the entire market demand curve, so selling one more unit forces the price down on every unit it sells. That single fact — \(\partial p/\partial q < 0\) — generates marginal revenue below price, a positive markup, and the deadweight loss the §10.9 price floor previewed — only now the firm chooses it.

Bridge slide. Students just finished Ch 10 (the price-taker, welfare theorem, \(N^*=50\) gigafactories). Today we flip the single load-bearing assumption. In Ch 10 the firm's output was a vanishing share of the market, so \(\partial p/\partial q_i = 0\). A monopolist is the market, so \(\partial p/\partial q < 0\): more output means a lower price on all units. That is the entire theory of market power in one inequality. Frame today's deadweight loss as the §10.9 price floor — but chosen by the firm, not imposed by government.

Spotify is not a price-taker. Apple Music has ~⅓ its subscribers; Amazon, YouTube Music, Tidal are smaller still. When Spotify raises its price, the price of Spotify Premium rises — some subscribers churn, most stay.

Barriers to Entry — the Moat

Barriers to entry — obstacles that keep new firms out, letting incumbents sustain economic profit in the long run.

20th century

Physical moats — railroads, oil wells, steel mills. Barriers made of capital.

21st century

Digital moats — software ecosystems, data, network effects. Lock in billions at near-zero marginal cost.

Market Power, Made Formal

Chapter 9's gigafactory faced a flat residual demand: it could sell 50 GWh or 100 at the going \$84/kWh. A monopolist cannot. Spotify is the only seller of Spotify Premium, so it faces the entire market demand curve:

\(\displaystyle \text{market power} \iff P'(q) < 0\)

A one-unit increase in output lowers the price the firm can charge on all its units. Selling more requires charging less; charging more means selling less. The two choices are duals — pick \(q\), read \(P\) off demand.

What market power lets the firm do: charge above marginal cost. A price-taker who set \(p > MC\) would lose every sale to a rival at \(p = MC\). A monopolist, with no rival to undercut it, sustains the wedge:

markup \(= P - MC > 0\)

That wedge is what the welfare theorem cares about. Ch 10's equilibrium was efficient because \(p = MC\). Any wedge between consumer benefit and producer cost generates deadweight loss — the rest of Part IV.

A subtle point: Spotify is not literally the only music platform. But it is large and differentiated enough that its residual demand — what's left after consumers choose between it and substitutes — still slopes down. The "monopoly" treatment is an approximation; Chapter 13 refines it with explicit rivals.

The definitional axis of the whole chapter. Market power = downward-sloping residual demand = \(P'(q) < 0\). Stress the duality: the firm chooses price or quantity, not both; we conventionally write it as choosing \(q\) and reading \(P\) off demand. The markup \(P - MC\) is the object of interest, and the welfare theorem failure traces entirely to it. The residual-demand caveat keeps us honest: Spotify isn't a textbook monopoly, but the model captures the price-setting logic, and Ch 13 puts the rivals back in.

Five Sources of Monopoly Power

Most real monopolies combine several — but each starts here.

1. Control of a key input. Own a resource rivals can’t replicate — ore, land… or software.

2. Switching costs & network effects. The cost of leaving — and the pull of where everyone already is.

3. Product differentiation & branding. Make consumers see your product as genuinely one-of-a-kind.

4. Economies of scale & natural monopoly. One firm serves the whole market more cheaply than two.

5. Government-created monopoly. Patents, franchises, licenses — the state grants exclusivity.

The five-source list is the spine of LO 11.1. Preview that we'll do one slide each, every example pairing an old-economy case with a 2020s digital one. Number 4 (natural monopoly) is the one with a real cost-curve story; the others are mostly about exclusion.

4 · Economies of Scale & Natural Monopoly

Natural monopoly — one firm can supply the whole market at lower average cost than two could. Arises with very high fixed cost + very low marginal cost.

Electric grid. ~$21.9B/yr US fixed cost; the cost of one more kWh is tiny. Two competing grids = both charge more.

Starlink. ~$10B+ sunk, 8,300+ satellites, 10M+ subscribers (2026). One more subscriber ≈ the cost of mailing a dish. A rival must rebuild the whole constellation.

This is the only source with a genuine cost-curve story (ties straight back to Ch 9 LRATC falling continuously). The test: does ATC keep falling across the whole relevant range? If yes, one firm is cheaper — splitting the market raises everyone's cost. Starlink is the 21st-century natural monopoly “in the making.” This sets up the regulation discussion in §11.5. Natural Monopolies

A natural monopoly is an extreme economy of scale: LRATC declines continuously as output rises.

Efficient as one firm — cheapest for a single firm to make the whole industry output.

High fixed cost, low MC — the grid is huge to build, ~free to use.

Splitting raises cost — two firms each duplicate the fixed cost: ATC jumps $100 → $200.

Split the market and each firm sits at point a — half the output, $200/mo. Competition here would DOUBLE costs. That’s why regulators grant (and then regulate) a single provider.

Why Is the 51st Unit Worth Less to a Monopolist?

A price-taker's 51st unit adds exactly the price: the price doesn't move, so \(MR = p\). That was Ch 9.

A monopolist's 51st unit adds the price minus the price cut needed to sell it — and that cut applies to every unit already being sold:

\(MR = \underbrace{P(q)}_{\text{quantity effect}} + \underbrace{q\,P'(q)}_{\text{price effect } < 0}\)

The first term is the revenue from the new sale at the new price. The second is the revenue lost on the \(q\) units the firm now sells for less. Since \(P'(q) < 0\), the sum is strictly below \(P\): the \(MR\) curve lies under demand at every \(q > 0\).

Fig 11.2 — \(MR\) lies strictly below demand for \(q > 0\).

The economic heart of marginal revenue. The contrast with the price-taker is the whole story: when your output moves the price, the 51st unit costs you revenue on units 1–50. Decompose \(MR\) into the quantity effect (\(+P\), revenue from the new unit) and the price effect (\(qP' < 0\), lost revenue on inframarginal units). The price effect is exactly what's absent under perfect competition (\(P'=0\)), which is why \(MR=p\) there and \(MR < p\) here. The figure makes "below demand" literal — Calculus Corner 11.1 next proves the same thing.

Calculus Corner 11.1 — MR and the Inverse-Elasticity Rule

Total revenue is \(TR(q) = P(q)\cdot q\). Differentiate with the product rule:

\[ MR(q) = P(q) + q\,P'(q) \]

Factor out the price to expose the elasticity:

\[ MR = P\left(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_D}\right) \]

where \(\varepsilon_D = \dfrac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\dfrac{P}{Q} < 0\) for downward-sloping demand.

Two limiting cases tie it together:

\(|\varepsilon_D| \to \infty\) (perfectly elastic = price-taker): \(1/\varepsilon_D \to 0\), so \(MR \to P\). Exactly Ch 9.

\(|\varepsilon_D| = 1\) (unit-elastic): \(MR = 0\). Total revenue is at its peak — one more unit adds nothing.

\(|\varepsilon_D| < 1\) (inelastic): \(MR < 0\). Revenue is falling as the firm sells more.

Calculus Corner 11.1 — kept in the sidebar per the book's voice-protection rule. The product-rule derivation is two lines; the payoff is the inverse-elasticity form \(MR = P(1+1/\varepsilon_D)\), which is the engine of the entire chapter (it becomes the Lerner index in §11.5). The three limiting cases are worth drilling: perfectly elastic recovers the price-taker, unit-elastic puts \(MR=0\) at the revenue peak, and inelastic gives \(MR < 0\). That last case is why no profit-maximizer ever operates on the inelastic part of demand (Heads Up 11.3, coming up).

Linear Demand: Same Intercept, Twice the Slope

For linear demand \(P(q) = a - bq\), we have \(P'(q) = -b\), so:

\(MR(q) = a - bq + q(-b) = a - 2bq\)

Commit this to memory: linear \(MR\) has the same vertical intercept \(a\) and twice the slope of demand. It crosses the horizontal axis at \(q = a/(2b)\) — exactly half the demand intercept \(q = a/b\).

Heads Up 11.1

\(MR = P\) only at \(q = 0\). A common trap is to set \(MR = P\) at the optimum, as if the firm were a price-taker. For linear demand, \(MR = P\) requires \(a - 2bq = a - bq\), i.e. \(q = 0\). The curves cross only at the vertical intercept; at every positive \(q\), \(MR\) lies strictly below \(P\). If your FOC has \(MR = P\), recompute.

The single most useful fact for solving monopoly problems by hand: linear demand → \(MR = a - 2bq\). Same intercept, double slope, x-intercept at half. Heads Up 11.1 heads off the most common student error — importing the price-taker's \(MR=p\) into the monopolist's FOC. The two curves meet only at the price axis; everywhere else the gap grows linearly in \(q\). Drill this before the Walkthrough, because every monopoly solve starts by writing down \(MR\).

Two Sign-Convention Traps

Heads Up 11.2 — signed vs absolute elasticity

Demand elasticity \(\varepsilon_D < 0\), so \(-1/\varepsilon_D > 0\). Some books write \(L = 1/|\varepsilon_D|\); this book uses signed \(\varepsilon_D\) and writes \(L = -1/\varepsilon_D\). They are arithmetically identical.

When you see \(\varepsilon_D = -2\), the predicted markup is \(-1/(-2) = 0.5 = 50\%\).

Heads Up 11.3 — never operate where demand is inelastic

If \(|\varepsilon_D| < 1\) at some \(q\), then \(MR(q) < 0\) — every extra unit reduces total revenue. A profit-maximizer would cut \(q\): that raises revenue and lowers cost.

The optimum always lies in the elastic region, \(|\varepsilon_D| > 1\). If your candidate has \(|\varepsilon_D| \le 1\), recompute.

Both traps come from the same place: \(\varepsilon_D\) is negative, and the interesting boundary is \(|\varepsilon_D| = 1\), where \(MR = 0\) and total revenue peaks. To the elastic side, the firm still gains from selling more; to the inelastic side, it loses.

Two checks every monopoly answer must pass. Heads Up 11.2 is bookkeeping: signed vs absolute elasticity give the same markup; we use signed throughout, so \(L = -1/\varepsilon_D\). Heads Up 11.3 is a genuine economic constraint — the optimum is always on the elastic part of demand, because inelastic demand has \(MR < 0\) and the firm could strictly improve by producing less. Tell students to verify \(|\varepsilon_D| > 1\) as a sanity check on every solve; we do exactly that in each Walkthrough's Check step.

§11.4

The Profit-Max FOC

\(\dfrac{d\pi}{dq} = MR(q) - MC(q) = 0 \iff MR = MC\)

Produce Until Marginal Revenue Meets Marginal Cost

Profit is revenue minus cost. Differentiate and set to zero:

\(\pi(q) = P(q)q - C(q) \;\Rightarrow\; MR(q) = MC(q)\)

Identical logic to Ch 9's \(p = MC\) — produce until the next unit's revenue contribution equals its cost — but with \(MR\) replacing \(p\) because the firm is no longer a price-taker.

The recipe: (1) read \(MC\) off the cost function; (2) read \(MR\) off inverse demand; (3) set \(MR = MC\), solve for \(Q_M\); (4) read \(P_M = P(Q_M)\) off the demand curve.

Step through the worked airline example: \(MR = MC\) sets \(Q^* \approx 45\); read \(P^*=\$218\) up on demand.

The chapter's central computational rule. \(MR = MC\) is the producer's analog of the consumer's \(MU/p\) ratio — a first-order condition pinning the optimal choice. The four-step recipe is what students should internalize: MC from cost, MR from inverse demand, equate, then go up to the demand curve for price (the most-missed step — they stop at \(MR=MC\) and read the price off \(MR\)). The second-order condition (\(MC\) rising faster than \(MR\) falls) holds automatically for well-behaved costs; we won't recheck it each time. Monopoly Is Not a Guarantee of Profit

If demand sits entirely below ATC, the monopolist still produces at MR = MC — but the price it reads off demand is below ATC. It loses money.

Short run: keep operating if P > AVC. Long run: exit — exactly like a competitive firm.

Monopoly gives the power to restrict output and raise price. It does not give the power to create demand that isn’t there. Being the sole seller of something nobody wants is not profitable.

§11.5 — the central tool

The Lerner Index

Markup as inverse elasticity: \(\dfrac{P - MC}{P} = -\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_D}\)

Read Demand Elasticity Off a Price Tag

Define the markup as a fraction of price — the Lerner index \(L = (P-MC)/P\). Substitute the FOC \(MR = MC\) and the inverse-elasticity form:

\(P\!\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon_D}\right) = MC \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{P-MC}{P} = -\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_D}\)

The profit-maximizing markup is exactly the negative inverse of demand elasticity. It's local — it holds at the operating point — but there it's exact for any smooth demand, not just linear.

\(\varepsilon_D = -2 \Rightarrow L = 0.5\) (price 50% over \(MC\)). \(\varepsilon_D = -10\) (nearly a price-taker) \(\Rightarrow L = 0.1\). More elastic demand → smaller markup.

Fig 11.5 — markup \(L = -1/\varepsilon_D\); Spotify and Wegovy bracket the range.

The chapter's most useful empirical product, and the reason the Lerner index is the central tool in industrial organization. The derivation is one substitution: plug \(MR = P(1+1/\varepsilon_D)\) into \(MR = MC\) and rearrange. The result converts price-cost margins (sometimes observable) into elasticities (rarely observable directly). Emphasize "local but exact": it holds at the firm's operating point for any smooth demand. The figure previews the two anchors — Spotify near the elastic end (\(L=0.30\)) and Wegovy near the ceiling (\(L=0.996\)) — which the next two Walkthroughs compute.

Heads Up 11.4 — Observed Markups May Diverge from \(-1/\varepsilon_D\)

\(L = -1/\varepsilon_D\) is what theory predicts if the firm sits at the static profit-max FOC. Real firms often don't, for at least five reasons:

1. List \(\ne\) effective price. Spotify's ARPU is ~€4.70 — far below the \$11.99 list — because the base spans Family, Duo, Student, and cheap emerging-market plans. Compute \(L\) at ARPU and \(\varepsilon_D \approx -2.87\).

2. Free-tier substitution. Spotify's ad-supported free tier is an unusually cheap substitute. A churned subscriber often drops to Free, not to Apple Music — raising effective elasticity.

3. Competition from substitutes. Apple, Amazon, YouTube Music, Tidal, podcasts, and radio all bound the price. The "monopoly" framing is a residual-demand approximation — Ch 13 puts the rivals back in.

4. Dynamic pricing. Spotify trades static profit for user-base growth and lock-in. A firm pricing below its static optimum to grow looks like a low-Lerner outlier even if long-run profit is higher.

5. Strategic preemption. A high price invites entry; Spotify's committee forecasts competitor responses we don't see. Bottom line: the Lerner equation tells you what should hold under the simplifying assumptions — deviations are diagnostic, not refutations.

The honesty slide for the Lerner index. Five reasons observed markup diverges from \(-1/\varepsilon_D\): (1) list vs effective price — ARPU is much lower than \$11.99, moving implied elasticity to \(-2.87\); (2) the free tier is a cheap substitute the academic paid-Premium studies miss; (3) real competitors bound the price (Ch 13); (4) Spotify is consciously trading static profit for growth and lock-in; (5) high prices invite entry, so the firm preempts. The meta-lesson: the Lerner index is a diagnostic, not a mechanical elasticity calculator. This is exactly why \$8.40-MC arithmetic gives \(-3.33\) but the truth is closer to \(-2\).

Reacting to Market Changes — When Marginal Cost Rises

A price-maker re-optimizes when costs change — just like a price-taker. Suppose a fuel-price spike lifts the airline’s marginal cost from \(\$56\) to \(\$80\) per seat. Re-run \(MR = MC\) on the same demand \(P = 380 - 3.6Q\):

\(MR = 380 - 7.2Q = 80 \;\Rightarrow\; Q\) falls \(45 \to 41.7\)M
\(P = 380 - 3.6(41.7) = \$230\)

\(MC\uparrow \Rightarrow Q\downarrow,\ P\uparrow\) — but \(P\) rises only \(\$12\) on a \(\$24\) cost jump. With a downward-sloping \(MR\), the monopolist passes through about half the cost increase.

Higher \(MC\) (\(MC_1\!\to\!MC_2\)) moves the optimum from \(b\) to \(d\): less output, a higher price.

Goolsbee "Figure 9.4" comparative static, recast on the airline (MC=$56). The key result: with a flat MC, a cost increase moves the firm up its MR curve, cutting Q and raising P — but because MR slopes down, the pass-through is partial. For linear demand P=a-bQ with constant MC=c, P_m=(a+c)/2, so dP_m/dc = 1/2: price rises by exactly half the MC increase ($12 on $24). Q falls by ΔMC/(2b)=24/7.2=3.33. Contrast Ch 10's price-taker, where a cost shock to one firm doesn't move the market price at all.

Now a positive shock — a new nonstop route doubles the addressable market — shifts demand out by 20M seats at every price. The new inverse demand is

\(P = 452 - 3.6Q\)

Marginal cost is back at \(\$56\). Follow the same three-step procedure.

1

Derive marginal revenue

\(MR = 452 - 7.2Q\)

2

A parallel outward shift (intercept 380 to 452, i.e. +20M units at every price) raises both the profit-maximizing quantity and price — unlike the MC change, which traded them off. Same three-step recipe as the five-step method slide: MR from inverse demand, MR=MC for Q*, demand for P*. Reinforce that the firm always reads price UP on demand, never off MR.

§11.6

Deadweight Loss from Monopoly

\(DWL_M = \displaystyle\int_{Q_M}^{Q_C}\!\big[P^d(q) - MC(q)\big]\,dq\)

The Units That Never Trade

Ch 10 proved the competitive \(Q_C\) maximizes total surplus. A monopolist sets \(MR = MC\) instead of \(P = MC\), producing \(Q_M < Q_C\). The units between \(Q_M\) and \(Q_C\) would have traded competitively — their value to consumers exceeds their cost — but don't. That lost surplus is the deadweight loss.

Recall from Principles — 1116 §11.3

"The Welfare Cost of Monopoly" drew this as the triangle between demand and \(MC\), bounded by \(Q_M\) on the left and \(Q_C\) on the right. In Principles you measured it as a triangle; here we measure it as an integral — which generalizes to non-linear demand and rising \(MC\).

Fig 11.4 — the Harberger triangle between \(Q_M\) and \(Q_C\).

The welfare cost of monopoly, geometric and analytic. The mechanism: \(MR = MC\) (not \(P = MC\)) restricts output to \(Q_M < Q_C\), and every un-traded unit on \([Q_M, Q_C]\) had consumer value above its marginal cost — mutually beneficial trades that don't happen. The 1116 recall (verified against source §11.3) ties today's integral to the triangle students already know. The integral form is the generalization: it's exact for any demand and any \(MC\), reducing to the triangle when both are linear (Calculus Corner 11.2 next). Frame this as the §10.9 price floor again — same triangle, but the firm chose the restriction.

Calculus Corner 11.2 — Deadweight Loss as an Integral

The deadweight loss is the un-captured surplus on the un-traded units:

\[ DWL_M = \int_{Q_M}^{Q_C}\!\big[P^d(q) - MC(q)\big]\,dq \]

For \(P^d = a - bq\) and constant \(MC = c\), it reduces to the Harberger triangle:

\[ DWL_M = \tfrac12(P_M - c)(Q_C - Q_M) \]

The integrand \(P^d - MC\) is positive on \([Q_M, Q_C]\) — by definition, demand lies above \(MC\) where the competitive market would still produce. The integral sums those gaps.

We've now seen these surplus integrals four times: Ch 2 introduced them, Ch 3 used them for tax DWL, Ch 10 proved the welfare theorem, and Ch 11 measures the loss from a price-cost wedge.

For convex \(MC\) (rising at an accelerating rate), the triangle underestimates the true integral; the gap closes faster than linearly near \(Q_C\).

Calculus Corner 11.2 — the surplus integral's fourth appearance, sidebar'd per voice protection. The integral form is the master object; the triangle is its linear-demand-constant-MC special case. Emphasize the integrand's sign: \(P^d - MC > 0\) on \([Q_M, Q_C]\) precisely because that's where competitive trades would still be mutually beneficial. The "four appearances" arc (Ch 2 definition, Ch 3 tax DWL, Ch 10 welfare theorem, Ch 11 monopoly DWL) is one of the most reused tools in the book. The convexity caveat matters for Walkthrough 11.5, which checks integral vs triangle on rising MC.

Walkthrough 11.5 — DWL with Rising \(MC\): Integral vs Triangle

Problem. \(P^d(Q) = 100 - Q\), \(MC(q) = 20 + 0.1q\). Find \(Q_M\), \(P_M\), \(Q_C\); compute DWL exactly by integration and compare to the triangle.

Set up & solve

\(MR = 100 - 2Q\). FOC: \(100 - 2Q = 20 + 0.1Q \Rightarrow Q_M = 80/2.1 = 38.10\), \(P_M = \$61.90\).

Competitive: \(100 - Q = 20 + 0.1Q \Rightarrow Q_C = 80/1.1 = 72.73\), \(P_C = \$27.27\).

Exact: \(DWL = \int_{38.10}^{72.73}[80 - 1.1q]\,dq = \$659.48\).

Triangle & comparison

Base \(= 72.73 - 38.10 = 34.63\). Height \(= P_M - MC(Q_M) = 61.90 - 23.81 = 38.09\). Triangle \(= \tfrac12(34.63)(38.09) = \$659.48\).

Exact \(= \$659.48\); triangle \(= \$659.48\). They agree — because \(MC\) is linear, so the integrand is linear and its area is exactly a triangle.

Takeaway. The Harberger triangle is exact whenever demand and \(MC\) are both linear. If \(MC\) rises convexly, the triangle underestimates; concavely, it overestimates. The integral is always the exact computation.

Six step-reveals. The surprise students should feel: even with rising (linear) marginal cost, the integral and the triangle give the identical \$659.48. The reason (reveal 5) is the lesson — a linear integrand always integrates to a triangle, so the Harberger formula is exact for linear demand + linear MC. The caveat (reveal 6) is when it breaks: convex MC makes the triangle an underestimate, concave an overestimate. For undergraduate problems the triangle is almost always good enough, but the integral is the fallback that's always exact. Verified numerically in reviews/ch11-verify.py.

The Societal Tax: A 30% Price Floor

Apple extends its monopoly from the iPhone into the App Store — the only channel to reach an iPhone user. Its marginal cost to host one more app is fractions of a cent, yet it takes a 30% cut of every sale.

Consumers would value those apps above the cost to make them but below the post-cut price — so the transaction never happens. The software never built, the utility never felt, is the deadweight loss.

The “Stripped” Economy — Damaged Goods

The flip side of versioning: a firm will deliberately make the cheap version worse — spending nothing, or even money, to degrade it — so high-WTP buyers refuse to trade down and pay for the premium tier.

The "damaged goods" strategy — the most counter-intuitive result in PD. X-inefficiency (Leibenstein) is distinct from DWL and rent-seeking: it's internal slack — the firm isn't even at min ATC. Competition is what keeps Ch 9's cost curves binding; monopoly lets the firm sit above them. §11.7

Natural Monopoly

When the cost function itself forces one firm: \(c(Q) \le \sum_i c(q_i)\)

Regulating the Natural Monopolist

Natural monopolies have economies of scale so large that one firm serves the whole market at lower cost than several could. You can’t just “add competition” — so policy must pick how to regulate:

1

Public ownership — the government owns and operates the monopoly. (e.g., municipal water)

2

Regulated private ownership — a private firm operates under government price & quantity limits. (e.g., the electric utility)

3

Franchise the right to operate — award the market to the firm offering the most competitive price and output.

Whichever path, the regulator’s real lever is the price it lets the monopolist charge — so the next question is: which price?

A natural monopoly exists when economies of scale are so large that average cost keeps falling over the whole relevant range — so one firm is the cheapest way to serve the market (utilities: water, electricity grid, gas pipes). You can't just "add competition," so policy picks among public ownership, regulated private ownership, or franchise bidding. The next two slides are the two textbook pricing rules.

Regulating the Natural Monopolist

Average-Cost Pricing Rule

  • Require the monopolist to set price = average total cost (PR = ATC, at point b).
  • The firm earns a normal profit, but price is higher and output lower than under marginal-cost pricing.

Regulation in Practice

  • Often rate-of-return regulation (prices that ensure a normal return on investment),
  • or price caps PR (maximum price limits).
  • Estimating demand and cost curves accurately is challenging.
Average-cost pricing (P = ATC at b) is the workhorse of real regulation: it forces price down from the monopoly level (a) while still letting the firm break even (normal profit) — no subsidy required. The catch is informational: the regulator must estimate the firm's true costs, and rate-of-return rules can invite gold-plating (the Averch-Johnson effect). Price caps push the firm to cut costs but risk under-investment.

Regulating the Natural Monopolist

Marginal-Cost Pricing Rule: set price = marginal cost (PC = MC, at point c) — socially optimal, but if price < ATC the firm runs a loss, so a government subsidy may be needed.

Amtrak is the textbook case: it runs persistent operating losses and depends on federal subsidies — price-near-MC made real.

Marginal-cost pricing (P = MC at c) is efficient — the last unit's price equals its cost, no deadweight loss — but for a natural monopoly with falling ATC, MC lies below ATC, so the firm loses money on every unit (the shaded subsidy rectangle). Someone must cover it: a government subsidy (Amtrak, transit, rural broadband) or a two-part tariff (a fixed connection fee + per-unit price = MC, as utilities actually bill).

When the Math of Cost Forces One Firm

Natural monopoly is a fact about the cost function, not a strategic choice — it is defined by subadditivity:

\(c(Q) \le \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n c(q_i)\) for any split \(\textstyle\sum_i q_i = Q\)

One firm producing \(Q\) costs no more than the same \(Q\) split across many firms. The classic recipe: positive fixed cost + constant \(MC\), giving \(ATC = F/q + \mu\), which falls forever.

Heads Up 11.5

Natural monopoly is a cost story, not a size story. A small market is a natural monopoly if fixed cost is large relative to demand. "Monopoly" (one firm exists) \(\ne\) "natural monopoly" (one-firm production is cost-minimizing).

Fig 11.6 — declining \(ATC\): one firm always undercuts two.

The "why" of monopoly, third case. Legal protection and strategic exclusion are choices; natural monopoly is a property of the cost function — subadditivity. The textbook recipe is \(c(q) = F + \mu q\): \(ATC = F/q + \mu\) declines everywhere, so splitting output across firms duplicates the fixed cost. Real examples are large-fixed-cost, low-MC infrastructure: electricity distribution, water/sewer, freight rail, wired telecom, last-mile broadband. Heads Up 11.5 is the trap — natural monopoly is about cost, not firm size or market size; a single strategic firm with no fixed-cost advantage is a regular monopoly, not a natural one. This is the Ch 10 Starlink contrast made formal.

Calculus Corner 11.3 — Subadditivity and the Failure of \(p = \min ATC\)

For \(c(q) = F + \mu q\), split \(Q\) across \(n\) firms:

\[ \sum_i c(q_i) - c(Q) = nF + \mu Q - (F + \mu Q) = (n-1)F > 0 \]

Subadditive at every \(Q\): splitting pays the fixed cost \(n\) times instead of once.

\[ ATC(q) = \tfrac{F}{q} + \mu, \quad \frac{dATC}{dq} = -\tfrac{F}{q^2} < 0 \]

\(\min ATC\) does not exist on \((0, \infty)\): \(ATC\) declines forever, approaching \(\mu\) only as \(q \to \infty\). There's no finite output where a competitive market can settle.

Chapter 10's free-entry condition \(p = \min LRATC\) fails. The cost function pushes the market toward concentration: one big firm pays \(ATC \to \mu\); many small firms each pay far more. The cost-minimizing structure is one firm — regardless of antitrust law.

Calculus Corner 11.3 — why the Ch 10 machinery breaks. Two results: subadditivity is exact (\(\sum c(q_i) - c(Q) = (n-1)F > 0\), the cost of duplicating the fixed cost), and \(ATC\) is everywhere declining (\(dATC/dq = -F/q^2 < 0\)). The punchline: \(\min ATC\) doesn't exist on \((0,\infty)\), so Ch 10's free-entry equilibrium \(p = \min LRATC\) has no solution. The cost function itself tips the market to one firm. This is the formal statement of what the Ch 10 Starlink slide showed graphically — \$10B sunk + near-zero MC means a second entrant can't survive.

Walkthrough 11.6 — Natural-Monopoly Cost Subadditivity

Problem. A firm has \(c(q) = 100 + 5q\). (a) Verify subadditivity at \(Q = 20\). (b) Show \(ATC\) is everywhere declining. (c) Confirm \(p = \min ATC\) has no solution.

Set up & solve

One firm at \(Q=20\): \(c(20) = 100 + 100 = \$200\). Two firms at 10 each: \(2(100+50) = \$300\). \(200 < 300\) ✓ — saving \(= \$100 = F\).

General: \(\sum c(q_i) - c(Q) = (n-1)(100) > 0\) for any \(n > 1\). Subadditive everywhere.

ATC & min check

\(ATC(q) = 100/q + 5\), \(dATC/dq = -100/q^2 < 0\) — strictly declining on \((0,\infty)\).

\(\lim_{q\to\infty} ATC = 5\), never achieved at finite \(q\). So \(p = \min ATC\) has no finite solution — no competitive equilibrium exists.

Takeaway. Positive fixed cost + constant \(MC\) is the textbook recipe for natural monopoly. The competitive \(p = \min ATC\) has no solution; the market necessarily concentrates. Whatever the policy response, it must take this as given — which is §11.8.

Five step-reveals on the cleanest possible natural-monopoly cost function, \(c(q) = 100 + 5q\). The \(Q=20\) check is concrete: one firm \$200, two firms \$300, the \$100 gap is exactly the duplicated fixed cost. The general result \((n-1)F\) and the declining \(ATC\) (derivative \(-100/q^2\)) confirm subadditivity and the non-existence of \(\min ATC\). The takeaway hands off to regulation: since no competitive equilibrium exists, society must choose how to handle the one firm — public ownership, rate-of-return, or price-cap. Regulation in the Wild — PG&E and the Zero-\(MC\) Platforms

Application — PG&E 2023 General Rate Case

PG&E requested a \$15.4B revenue requirement; the CPUC adopted \$13.5B, with an allowed return on equity ~10%. The arithmetic follows the \(ATC\)-pricing logic of W11.7: estimate operating cost + depreciation + a fair return on the rate base, then set price to recover it.

The Averch–Johnson effect: because allowed profit scales with the capital base, the firm over-invests in capital. That distortion pushed the UK, Australia, and the EU toward price-cap ("RPI − X") regulation.

Application — Microsoft 365 & Adobe

Provisioning one more software license costs ~\$0; nearly all cost is fixed (R&D, sales, support). With \(MC \to 0\), the FOC \(L = -1/\varepsilon_D\) pushes the markup toward 1.

Microsoft 365 E3 rose \$23 → \$26/user/month (July 2026); Adobe moved customers into pricier Creative Cloud Pro. Switching costs (file-format lock-in, learning curves) lower elasticity, so enterprise-SaaS Lerner indices run 0.7–0.9 — closer to Wegovy than to Spotify.

Two faces of natural monopoly. PG&E is the canonical regulated utility: the General Rate Case is W11.7's \(ATC\)-pricing made institutional — cost + depreciation + allowed return on the rate base, recovered through price. Flag the Averch-Johnson distortion (capital over-investment because profit scales with the rate base), which is why much of the world shifted to RPI−X price caps. The software platforms are the zero-MC limit: when \(MC \to 0\), \(L \to 1\) regardless of elasticity, and switching-cost lock-in lowers elasticity further, putting enterprise SaaS at Lerner 0.7–0.9. Two very different industries, same subadditive-cost logic.

Generic-Drug Entry

How fast monopoly power dissolves when entry barriers fall

Walkthrough 11.8 — The Truvada Generic-Entry Collapse (2020)

Problem. Gilead's Truvada (HIV PrEP) listed near \(P_1 = \$1{,}600\)/month under patent, \(MC \approx \$50\). Oct 2020: Teva launched the first generic; within a year the price fell to ~\$50 with multiple makers. Compute pre- and post-entry Lerner indices.

Pre-entry (monopoly)

\(L_1 = (1600 - 50)/1600 = 0.969\). Implied \(\varepsilon_D = -1/0.969 = -1.032\) — just barely elastic, a no-substitutes therapeutic.

Post-entry (competition)

\(L_2 = (50 - 50)/50 = 0\). Multi-source generic competition collapses the markup to ~zero — the market turns Bertrand-competitive (Ch 13).

Welfare: the \$1,550 that flowed to Gilead as monopoly profit becomes consumer surplus, and the DWL units convert into actual consumption.

Takeaway. Patent length is a deliberate static-vs-dynamic tradeoff: longer patents fund more R&D (dynamic gain) but extend the static DWL window (static loss). When the patent expires, the welfare-theorem failure is restored in months.

Heads Up 11.6 — Coase conjecture

A durable-goods monopolist with no commitment may be pushed toward \(p = MC\) anyway: buyers anticipate future price cuts and wait. Hence leasing (Xerox), planned obsolescence, and the shift to subscriptions.

Five step-reveals — market structure is not fixed. Truvada pre-entry: \(L=0.969\), \(\varepsilon=-1.032\), the textbook patent monopoly at the unit-elastic corner. Post-entry: \(L=0\), the markup gone in twelve months as Teva and others enter and the market turns Bertrand. The \$1,550 of monopoly profit becomes consumer surplus. The deep lesson is the patent tradeoff — longer patents buy R&D at the cost of a longer DWL window. Heads Up 11.6 (Coase conjecture) is a forward pointer: durable-goods monopolists without commitment may be forced toward \(p=MC\) because buyers wait for cuts — the reason Xerox leased copiers and software moved to SaaS. §11.10 — A Note on Augustin Cournot (1801–1877)

In 1838 the French mathematician Antoine Augustin Cournot published Recherches sur les principes mathématiques de la théorie des richesses. In a chapter on monopoly pricing he wrote down — almost word for word — the derivation in Calculus Corner 11.1: take \(R(p) = p\,D(p)\), differentiate to \(dR/dp = D(p) + p\,D'(p)\), set it equal to marginal cost, solve.

It was the first calculus-based model in economics — at a time when economic writing was verbal or geometric. The book was ignored in his lifetime; Walras and Jevons rediscovered him in the 1870s–80s. Abba Lerner (1934) built directly on it, turning the markup into the measurement tool that bears his name.

Cournot also wrote down, in the same 1838 book, the duopoly model that is the foundation of Chapter 13.

Chapter Summary

Market power

A downward-sloping residual demand, \(P'(q) < 0\). Selling more lowers price on all units — the source of \(MR < P\).

Marginal revenue

\(MR = P(1 + 1/\varepsilon_D)\). Linear demand: same intercept, twice the slope. FOC: \(MR = MC\).

Lerner index ★

\(L = (P-MC)/P = -1/\varepsilon_D\). Markup is the inverse of elasticity — the central empirical tool.

Deadweight loss

\(Q_M < Q_C\) makes a Harberger triangle. Aggregate US cost: 5–10% of consumption (EMX 2023).

Natural monopoly

Subadditive cost (\(F + \mu q\)) → declining \(ATC\), no \(\min ATC\). \(ATC\) pricing kills ~99% of DWL.

Spotify ↔ Wegovy

\(L = 0.30\) (\(\varepsilon=-3.33\)) to \(L = 0.996\) (\(\varepsilon=-1.004\)) bracket the realistic range.

Why \$11.99? Because \(MR = MC\), and the markup it implies is \(-1/\varepsilon_D\). The Spotify thread continues: Chapter 12 lets the firm charge different prices to different groups — Student, Duo, Family, Premium.
