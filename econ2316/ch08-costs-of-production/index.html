Before isocost — what counts as a cost?

Costs That Matter

Not every cost should change your decision — some do.

Section opener for the Costs-That-Matter mini-unit (ported from 1116 Ch 9). Before we get into isocost geometry and Lagrangian cost-minimization, students need to know WHAT counts as a cost. The opportunity-cost framework — explicit + implicit costs, and the resulting distinction between accounting and economic profit — is the conceptual foundation for everything in §8.1 onward. Concretely: when the firm chooses (K, L) to minimize cost, the relevant "cost" is the economic one, including the opportunity cost of owner-supplied inputs. Five slides walk through the restaurant-industry case study (income tailwind, explicit vs implicit cost, accounting profit, economic profit / normal-rate-of-return, entry/exit dynamics). The dramatic payoff: a shop earning $200K accounting profit can be zero in economic terms once you net out the owner's forgone salary + capital return.

Macro to Micro — Profits Drive Entry & Exit

Whether economic profit shows up at all depends on the overall economy — demand, input prices, interest rates, taxes, the wage you could earn elsewhere. Macro conditions shape every micro decision.

Positive economic profit

A firm is earning more than the next-best use of its time + capital.

New sellers enter — chasing the same opportunity.

Result: supply ↑, price ↓, profits shrink toward zero.

Negative economic profit

A firm is earning less than the next-best use — bleeding value.

Owners quit — leaving for higher-return alternatives.

Result: supply ↓, price ↑, profits drift back toward zero.

Long-run equilibrium: economic profit is driven to zero. Surviving firms earn a normal rate of return, no more. (We’ll formalize entry & exit in Ch 9 — Profit Maximization.)

Bridge from cost theory to market structure. The economic-profit framework explains entry/exit BEHAVIOR. When restaurants are boom-times (positive economic profit), new entrants pile in; supply rises, price falls, profits compress. When rents + labor rise faster than revenues (negative economic profit), shops close; supply falls, price rises, profits recover. Long-run equilibrium: zero economic profit — surviving firms cover all opportunity costs but no more. In our 2316 narrative, this sets up the perfect-competition framework in Ch 9. Pedagogically, this answers the question every student asks: "if economic profit is zero in the long run, why does anyone start a business?" Answer: short-run economic profit can be positive while you're early; entry erodes it; the timing of entry is the entrepreneur's bet.

The flip side — what to ignore

Costs That Do Not Matter

Sunk costs are gone — they should never enter the next decision.

Second half of the Costs-That-Matter mini-unit. Mirror image of the previous block: students just learned to ADD implicit/opportunity costs to the decision; now they learn to SUBTRACT sunk costs. Sunk costs are payments already made that can't be recovered — by definition they're identical across every future decision, so they cancel out of any forward-looking comparison. Three pillars: (1) the SR/LR variable-vs-sunk distinction (long run has no fixed costs because every committed factor eventually rolls off; sunk is different — it's the residual that can't ever be undone); (2) the AI training-vs-inference case study (training is paid once, inference is recurring) — the SAME $191M Gemini Ultra training cost is sunk the day training finishes; (3) the sunk-cost fallacy — the systematic human error of letting unrecoverable past spending drive forward decisions. Closes with Walgreens permanent closures: in late 2024 / 2025, Walgreens shut ~1,200 stores; the rational call required ignoring billions in prior store buildouts.

The sunk-cost fallacy in three lines. The $191M training cost is GONE — it doesn't change whether you continue the proprietary product or switch. The rational comparison is: future revenue + future cost under each path. If the open-source alternative has lower future cost AND comparable performance, switch. The "we already spent $191M" framing is psychologically powerful but economically irrelevant. Daniel Kahneman: humans treat sunk costs as if they were retrievable; we throw good money after bad. To pick the cost-minimizing recipe today, the firm needs two market-set prices: the wage (\(w\)) for labor and the rental rate (\(r\)) for capital.

Identifying Minimum Cost — Combining Isoquants & Isocost Lines

Remember, the firm's problem is one of constrained minimization.

  • Firms minimize costs subject to a given amount of production.
  • Cost minimization is achieved by adjusting the ratio of capital to labor.

Graphically, cost minimization requires tangency between the isoquant associated with the chosen level of production, and the lowest-cost isocost line.

Isoquant fixes what the firm must produce; the isocost line fixes at what cost. Tangency picks the cheapest feasible bundle.

Conceptual setup framing cost-min as constrained minimization. Three takeaways: (1) constrained problem — firm can't set zero inputs because of output requirement; (2) substitution lever = K/L ratio; (3) graphically, cost-min = tangency between isoquant and lowest isocost. Sets up the Apple-1 application next, then the algebra two slides later.

Identifying Minimum Cost — A Graphical Approach

A Graphical Approach

  1. Isocost lines represent how much each set of inputs costs.
  2. Isoquants represent the production function and show the levels of inputs necessary to achieve a given level of output.
  3. The firm's objective is to produce \(\bar Q\) at the lowest cost possible.
    1. The lowest cost is on the isocost line closest to the origin.
    2. This occurs where the isocost line is tangent to the isoquant — at point \(B\).

\(C_C\) sits below the isoquant — cheap, but doesn't produce enough output. \(C_A\) is feasible at point \(A\) but more expensive than necessary. \(C_B\) is the lowest isocost that still touches the isoquant — the tangency point \(B\) is the cost-minimizing combination.

The headline graphical slide for cost-min. Three takeaways: (1) isocost line says HOW MUCH a recipe costs; (2) isoquant says WHAT the recipe produces; (3) cost-min combines both — pick the LOWEST isocost still touching the isoquant. The picture: C_C is too cheap (below the isoquant — infeasible); C_A is feasible (crosses the isoquant at A) but overspends; C_B is the Goldilocks — the lowest C that still touches. Numerical Example — Same Isoquant, Different Input Prices

Target the same isoquant \(\bar Q = 50\) with Cobb-Douglas \(Q = \sqrt{K\cdot L}\) (so \(K L = 2{,}500\)). Closed-form cost-min:

\( L^* = 50\,\sqrt{R/W}, \quad K^* = 50\,\sqrt{W/R}, \quad C^* = 100\,\sqrt{W\cdot R} \)

  Where \(L^*\) and \(K^*\) come from — 4 steps

1 · Tangency

\(MRTS = \dfrac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \dfrac{K}{L} = \dfrac{W}{R}\)

⇒ \(K = (W/R)\,L\)

2 · Stay on the isoquant

\(\sqrt{KL} = 50\)

⇒ \(KL = 2{,}500\)

3 · Substitute 1 into 2

\((W/R)L^2 = 2{,}500\)

⇒ \(L^* = 50\sqrt{R/W},\; K^* = 50\sqrt{W/R}\)

4 · Add up the bill

\(C^* = WL^* + RK^*\)

⇒ \(C^* = 100\sqrt{WR}\)

Step 1 is the tangency condition — we prove it formally a few slides ahead (“Identifying Minimum Cost — The Tangency Math”). For now: at the optimum the isocost slope \(-W/R\) equals the isoquant slope \(-MRTS\).

Case A — baseline

\(W = \$10\), \(R = \$20\)

slope \(-W/R = -0.5\); \(K/L = 0.5\)

  • \(L^* = 50\sqrt{2} \approx \mathbf{70.7}\)
  • \(K^* = 50/\sqrt{2} \approx \mathbf{35.4}\)
  • \(C^* = 10\cdot 70.7 + 20\cdot 35.4 = \mathbf{\$1{,}414}\)

\(W = \$20\), \(R = \$20\)

slope \(-W/R = -1\); \(K/L = 1\)

  • \(L^* = \mathbf{50}\)
  • \(K^* = \mathbf{50}\)
  • \(C^* = 20\cdot 50 + 20\cdot 50 = \mathbf{\$2{,}000}\)

\(W = \$5\), \(R = \$20\)

slope \(-W/R = -0.25\); \(K/L = 0.25\)

Takeaway. Same output (\(\bar Q = 50\)), different input prices ⇒ different cost-min recipe AND different total cost. As the wage rises (C→A→B), the firm shifts toward more capital, less labor, and total cost climbs from \(\$1{,}000 \to \$1{,}414 \to \$2{,}000\). Identifying Minimum Cost — The Tangency Math

Mathematically, tangency occurs where the slope of the isocost line equals the slope of the isoquant — and the isoquant’s slope is the MRTS:

\[ -\frac{W}{R} \;=\; -\frac{MP_L}{MP_K} \;\;\longrightarrow\;\; \text{MRTS}_{LK} \;=\; \frac{MP_L}{MP_K} \;=\; \frac{W}{R} \]

Rearranged — the equimarginal principle:

\[ \frac{MP_L}{W} \;=\; \frac{MP_K}{R} \]

Costs are minimized when the marginal product per dollar spent is equalized across inputs.

Intuition. If \(MP_L/W > MP_K/R\), a dollar spent on labor buys more output than a dollar spent on capital — so shift toward labor. Stop only when no further re-mix saves money. The next slide works both directions, case by case.

Same logic as Ch 5's consumer equimarginal principle (\(MU_1/p_1 = MU_2/p_2\)): the producer-side dual swaps marginal utilities → marginal products and good prices → input prices.

Cost-Minimization — The Problem

A firm with technology \(f(K, L)\) faces market input prices \(w\) (wage) and \(r\) (rental rate). It must deliver at least \(q\) units of output. Choose the input bundle that drives total spending to its lowest value:

\[ \min_{K, L \,\geq\, 0} \;\; w L + r K \quad \text{subject to} \quad f(K, L) \geq q. \]

Why \(\geq\), not \(=\)?

Producing extra output requires extra inputs, which costs money. So at the optimum the constraint binds: \(f(K^*, L^*) = q\). We can write \(=\) once we know the optimum sits on the boundary.

Why not min unconstrained?

Unconstrained min of \(wL + rK\) is \(K = L = 0\) — trivial. The output requirement is what makes the problem interesting. \(q\) is handed down from elsewhere (Ch 9 will endogenize it).

The problem statement. Two technical points worth dwelling on: (1) the inequality vs. equality — we write \(\geq q\) but know it binds because free-disposal monotonic production means producing more requires more inputs; (2) the importance of the output constraint — without it the answer is \((0,0)\), which is uninteresting. The Ch 6 analog is expenditure-min: \(\min p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2\) s.t. \(u(x) \geq \bar u\). Same mathematical structure. The closing parenthetical "(Ch 9 will endogenize it)" sets up the chapter sequence: cost-min takes \(q\) as given; profit-max chooses \(q\) optimally given output price.

§8.3 The Lagrangian & Tangency

FOCs · the multiplier \(\mu\) · the cost-min interior solution

Setting Up the Lagrangian

Attach a multiplier \(\mu\) to the binding output constraint \(q - f(K, L) = 0\):

\[ \mathcal{L}(K, L, \mu) \;=\; w L + r K + \mu \bigl(q - f(K, L)\bigr) \]

First-order conditions (at an interior optimum):

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial L = 0\)

\(w = \mu \cdot MP_L\)

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial K = 0\)

\(r = \mu \cdot MP_K\)

\(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \mu = 0\)

\(f(K, L) = q\)

Divide the first two: \(\;w/r = MP_L/MP_K = \text{MRTS}_{LK}\) — the tangency. The third pins the solution to the isoquant.

What is \(\mu\)? The shadow value of the output constraint — the marginal cost of an extra unit of output. In §8.5 we'll prove (via the envelope theorem) that \(\mu = \partial c/\partial q = MC\). The Lagrange multiplier is marginal cost.

Standard Lagrangian setup. The three FOCs give us the three pieces: \(w = \mu \cdot MP_L\), \(r = \mu \cdot MP_K\), \(f(K, L) = q\). Dividing the first two cancels \(\mu\), giving the tangency. The third FOC restores the constraint. Pedagogically: \(\mu\) appears as a bookkeeping device but has deep economic meaning — it IS marginal cost, by the envelope theorem we'll prove later. This is the producer-side analog of \(\lambda\) being the marginal utility of income in the consumer's problem. Foreshadow: §8.5 will derive \(\mu = MC\) formally. For now treat \(\mu\) as "shadow price of output".

§8.4 The Cost Function

Cobb-Douglas closed form · conditional input demands · comparative statics

Cost Function & Conditional Input Demands

Cost function

\[ c(w, r, q) \;=\; \min_{K, L} \,(w L + r K) \;\; \text{s.t.} \;\; f(K, L) = q \]

The minimum total spending required to produce \(q\) units of output at input prices \(w, r\). Function of three arguments: two prices and a quantity.

Conditional input demands

\[ L^*(w, r, q), \quad K^*(w, r, q) \]

The cost-minimizing input bundle conditional on producing exactly \(q\). Solves the FOCs. Distinguishes from unconditional input demand (Ch 9) where \(q\) is also a choice variable.

Three properties to know. The cost function is (i) non-decreasing in each price, (ii) non-decreasing in \(q\), and (iii) concave in each input price — doubling a price less-than-doubles cost because the firm substitutes away. We'll prove (iii) graphically in §8.6 (Shephard).

Definitional slide. Three key objects: cost function \(c(w, r, q)\), conditional input demand \(L^*(w, r, q)\), conditional input demand \(K^*(w, r, q)\). All three are functions of the same three arguments. The "conditional" qualifier matters — it distinguishes from Ch 9's unconditional input demand which lets \(q\) adjust too. Property (iii) — concavity of \(c\) in prices — is the substitution-effect insurance the firm gives itself. Doubling \(w\) doesn't double cost because the firm shifts toward capital. Foreshadow Shephard's lemma in §8.6 — same envelope-theorem mechanics on the input-price arguments instead of the output argument.

The Cobb-Douglas Cost Function — Where It Comes From

Start from the cost-min problem for \(f(K, L) = A K^{\alpha} L^{\beta}\): minimize \(wL + rK\) subject to \(A K^{\alpha} L^{\beta} = q\). Four steps get you the whole cost function.

Step 1 — Tangency

With \(MP_L = \beta q/L\) and \(MP_K = \alpha q/K\):

\(\dfrac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \dfrac{\beta K}{\alpha L} = \dfrac{w}{r} \;\Longrightarrow\; K = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}\dfrac{w}{r}\,L\)

Step 2 — Impose the output target

Substitute that \(K\) into \(A K^{\alpha} L^{\beta} = q\):

\(A\left(\dfrac{\alpha w}{\beta r}\right)^{\!\alpha} L^{\alpha+\beta} = q\)

Step 3 — Solve for the input demands

Only \(L\) is left, so solve — then back out \(K^*\) from Step 1:

\(L^* = \left(\dfrac{q}{A}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}\!\left(\dfrac{\beta r}{\alpha w}\right)^{\!\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}\)

Step 4 — Add up the bill

Plug \(L^*, K^*\) into \(c = wL^* + rK^*\) and collect terms:

both pieces share \(\left(\tfrac{q}{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}} w^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}} r^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}\)

\(c(w, r, q) = (\alpha + \beta)\left(\dfrac{q}{A}\right)^{\!1/(\alpha+\beta)} \!\left(\dfrac{w}{\beta}\right)^{\!\beta/(\alpha+\beta)} \!\left(\dfrac{r}{\alpha}\right)^{\!\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}\)

Under CRS (\(\alpha+\beta=1\)) this collapses to \(c(q) = \left(\tfrac{w}{\beta}\right)^{\beta}\left(\tfrac{r}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\tfrac{q}{A}\) — linear in \(q\). Walkthrough 8.1 runs the gigafactory numbers through exactly these four steps.

What this formula actually tells you (3 ideas):

1. Returns to scale → cost shape

CRS (\(\alpha+\beta=1\)): cost is linear in \(q\).
DRS: cost rises faster than \(q\) (convex).
IRS: cost rises slower (concave).

2. Cost shares = exponents

\(\dfrac{wL^*}{c} = \beta,\;\dfrac{rK^*}{c} = \alpha\). Gigafactory labor share = 0.6 = \(\beta\), capital share = 0.4 = \(\alpha\) — regardless of prices.

3. Only \(w/r\) matters

Double both prices ⇒ cost doubles, but the input mix \((K^*, L^*)\) is unchanged. Only the ratio \(w/r\) drives the mix.

The Envelope Theorem — The Planner’s Problem

The setup. The gigafactory’s finance team needs to budget for next quarter. Output target rises from \(q = 32\) to \(q = 33\) GWh. What’s the cost of that extra GWh? In other words, what is \(\partial c/\partial q\)?

The hard way

When \(q\) rises by 1, both \(K^*\) and \(L^*\) re-adjust. Naïvely:

\(\dfrac{\partial c}{\partial q} = w \dfrac{\partial L^*}{\partial q} + r \dfrac{\partial K^*}{\partial q}\)

Three derivatives. Need to know how the firm re-optimizes — painful, error-prone, often impossible without re-solving the full Lagrangian.

The envelope shortcut

At the optimum, the FOCs make the two indirect adjustments cancel exactly. What’s left is just the multiplier \(\mu\):

\(\dfrac{\partial c}{\partial q} = \mu = MC\)

One identity does the work of three derivatives. Read \(\mu\) directly off the cost-min solution — no need to know how \(K^*\) or \(L^*\) move.

So what are the direct and indirect effects? — Use the Lagrangian

Start from \(\mathcal{L} = w L + r K + \mu(q - f(K, L))\). When \(q\) ticks up by 1, the total change in \(\mathcal{L}\) at the optimum is:

\(\dfrac{d\mathcal{L}^*}{dq} \;=\; \underbrace{\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q}}_{\textbf{direct}} \;+\; \underbrace{\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial K}\cdot\dfrac{dK^*}{dq} \;+\; \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial L}\cdot\dfrac{dL^*}{dq}}_{\textbf{indirect} \;=\; 0 \text{ by FOCs}}\)

Direct effect = \(\mu\)

\(\mathcal{L}\) depends on \(q\) only through the constraint term \(\mu(q - f)\). Hold \(K, L\) fixed and differentiate: \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial q = \mu\). The multiplier itself — the shadow value of one more GWh.

Indirect effects = 0

\(K^*, L^*\) do move, but the gradients they multiply — \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial K\) and \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial L\) — are zero by the FOCs. Zero × anything = zero. The K and L re-adjustments contribute nothing extra.

Net result. \(\partial c/\partial q = \text{direct effect alone} = \mu = MC\). One identity does the work of three derivatives.

Consumer-side parallel. In Ch 5, the same mechanics gave us \(\lambda = \partial v/\partial I\) = marginal utility of income. Here it gives us \(\mu = \partial c/\partial q\) = marginal cost. Same envelope, two sides of the market.

(2) The hard way vs the easy way (red vs green). (3) NEW concrete decomposition: use the Lagrangian L = wL + rK + μ(q − f). Calculus Corner — The Envelope Theorem: \(\mu = MC\)

Recall the Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L} = wL + rK + \mu(q - f)\). Differentiate the optimized cost with respect to \(q\):

\[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial q} \;=\; \underbrace{w \frac{\partial L^*}{\partial q} + r \frac{\partial K^*}{\partial q}}_{\text{indirect effects}} \;+\; \underbrace{0}_{\text{direct}} \]

Use the FOCs \(w = \mu f_L,\, r = \mu f_K\). The indirect-effects bracket becomes \(\mu(f_L \partial L^*/\partial q + f_K \partial K^*/\partial q) = \mu \cdot \partial f/\partial q = \mu \cdot 1 = \mu\):

\[ \boxed{\;\frac{\partial c}{\partial q} \;=\; \mu \;=\; MC\;} \]

The Lagrange multiplier on the output constraint is marginal cost. No coincidence — \(\mu\) is the shadow price of one extra unit of output, which is by definition marginal cost.

Same envelope mechanics as the consumer's \(\lambda = \partial v/\partial I = \) marginal utility of income (Ch 5).

The envelope theorem in two lines. The "indirect effects" cancel because they're balanced exactly by the FOCs and the binding constraint. What's left is the direct effect of \(q\) on the Lagrangian, which is the multiplier \(\mu\). So \(\partial c/\partial q = \mu = MC\) — an identity, not a coincidence. This is the producer-side analog of \(\lambda = \) marginal utility of income from Ch 5. The envelope theorem is the workhorse of comparative statics in microeconomics; we'll use it again in §8.6 (Shephard's lemma — same mechanics but on price arguments) and in Ch 9 (Hotelling's lemma — same mechanics for the profit function). Shephard’s Lemma: if you have a model of \(c(w, r, q)\), just differentiate it:

\(\dfrac{\partial c}{\partial w} = L^*(w, r, q), \qquad \dfrac{\partial c}{\partial r} = K^*(w, r, q).\)

You recover the firm’s input choices from cost data alone — without re-solving the Lagrangian, without observing \(K\) and \(L\) directly.

The duality. The production function \(f(K, L)\) and the cost function \(c(w, r, q)\) are two equivalent descriptions of the same technology. From either one, all the empirical objects we care about — demands, marginal cost, factor shares, returns to scale — follow.

Motivation slide — sets up WHY students should care about the next slide's formal lemma + the duality square. Three teaching moves: (1) The "analyst's problem" frame. In empirical industrial organization, firms' interior decisions are private — you can't peek inside Tesla's factory to see how K and L are mixed. What's PUBLIC is the cost data. Shephard's Lemma is the bridge: it lets you recover the unobservable input choices from the observable cost shape. This is what makes the lemma a workhorse of modern IO — papers like Berry-Levinsohn-Pakes 1995, Olley-Pakes 1996, De Loecker-Eeckhout-Unger 2020 ALL run on Shephard-style cost-function machinery to recover production parameters from cost data. (2) The duality framing. Production function and cost function are two equivalent representations of the same underlying technology. Knowing one (in principle) tells you everything about the other. The cost function is "easier" because it's expressed in terms of observable variables (prices, output). The production function is "harder" because it's expressed in terms of unobservable variables (input bundles). Duality lets us work in the easier representation and translate. Shephard's Lemma

Apply the envelope theorem to the input-price arguments of the cost function:

\[ \frac{\partial c(w, r, q)}{\partial w} \;=\; L^*(w, r, q), \qquad \frac{\partial c(w, r, q)}{\partial r} \;=\; K^*(w, r, q). \]

  Yes — this assumes the firm is already cost-minimizing. Every object here is an optimized one: \(c(w,r,q)\) is the minimized cost, and \(L^*, K^*\) are the conditional input demands — the cheapest way to hit \(q\). The proof below replaces \(w\) by \(\mu f_L\) and \(r\) by \(\mu f_K\), which are the first-order conditions, so the identity holds only at the optimum. For a firm sitting off its cost-min bundle — like the installed \((K,L) = (4,4)\) gigafactory — \(\partial c/\partial w \neq L\).

Two-line proof. Differentiate \(c = wL^* + rK^*\) w.r.t. \(w\):

\[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial w} \;=\; L^* \;+\; \underbrace{w \frac{\partial L^*}{\partial w} + r \frac{\partial K^*}{\partial w}}_{= \, \mu \left(f_L \partial L^*/\partial w + f_K \partial K^*/\partial w\right) \,=\, \mu \cdot 0 \,=\, 0} \]

Two facts kill the cross-terms. (i) Optimality: the FOCs give \(w = \mu f_L\) and \(r = \mu f_K\), which is how \(\mu\) gets factored out. (ii) The constraint: \(f = q\) holds at every \(w\), so differentiating it gives \(f_L \partial L^*/\partial w + f_K \partial K^*/\partial w = 0\). What's left is \(L^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

The empirical reach is enormous. From a fitted cost function, you can recover input demands by simple differentiation — no need to re-solve the Lagrangian.

Same envelope mechanics as \(\mu = MC\), just applied to a different argument. Drill the proof: differentiate \(c = wL^* + rK^*\) w.r.t. \(w\). The direct effect is \(L^*\). The two indirect effects (from \(L^*\) and \(K^*\) adjusting in response to \(w\)) collapse to zero by the FOCs and the binding constraint. Key empirical point: from a parametric cost function fitted to firm data, you can READ OFF the conditional input demand at any prices by differentiating — without observing inputs at all. This is what makes Shephard's lemma a workhorse of empirical industrial organization. Verification at the gigafactory (W8.4): \(\partial c/\partial w = (\beta/w) \cdot c = (0.6/0.1) \cdot 0.8707 = 5.224 = L^*\). ✓ Exact identity. Both sides are optimized objects — c(.) is the MINIMISED cost and L*, K* are the CONDITIONAL INPUT DEMANDS. Optimization enters at exactly one step: substituting w = mu*f_L and r = mu*f_K, which are the first-order conditions. Take those away and the cross-terms do not collapse. Point back to the "Why the Firm's Bundle Isn't Always Cost-Minimizing" slide for the contrast: the gigafactory's installed (4,4) is NOT cost-minimizing at current prices, so you may NOT differentiate its actual spending and call the answer labour demand. Short Run vs Long Run — Definitions

Short run

Capital fixed at \(\bar K\); only labor adjusts. One-dimensional problem — the output constraint pins \(L\) uniquely.

\(c_{SR}(q; \bar K) = r \bar K + w \cdot L^*_{SR}(q; \bar K)\)

No Lagrangian, no tangency — just invert \(f(\bar K, L) = q\) for \(L\).

Long run

Both \(K\) and \(L\) adjust freely. Two-dimensional problem — Lagrangian + tangency, as in §8.3-8.4.

\(c_{LR}(q) = \min_{\bar K} c_{SR}(q; \bar K)\)

The lower envelope of the SR family — today's headline figure.

HeadsUp: short run isn't clock time. SR and LR are defined by which inputs are flexible, not by elapsed time. A semiconductor fab's short run can last five years (clean-room construction is glacial). A restaurant's short run is the lunch shift (table count fixed within the hour). A battery plant's short run is roughly a quarter (capital sunk; workforce adjustable). The institutional content is what's flexible, not how long.

Short-Run vs Long-Run Marginal Cost Curves

Just as with average costs:

  • Short-run marginal cost is the cost of producing an additional unit of output when capital is fixed.
  • Long-run marginal cost is the cost of producing an additional unit of output when both capital and labor are variable.

What does this imply for the shape of the marginal cost curves?

In general, the long-run marginal cost curve will be flatter than the short-run marginal cost curve.

Intuition: in the SR, the firm absorbs all extra output by adding labor to a fixed \(\bar K\) — diminishing \(MP_L\) makes the next unit increasingly expensive. In the LR, the firm can also scale capital up, so MC rises more gently.

The MC analog of the previous slide. SR-MC rises steeply because of diminishing marginal product of labor (the fixed K̄ acts as a binding constraint). LR-MC rises more gently because the firm can scale BOTH inputs proportionally, sidestepping the diminishing-MP_L trap. Graphically: SR-MC curve is steeper than LR-MC, and the two cross at the firm's planning output (where the firm's K̄ matches the LR-optimal K). Short-Run and Long-Run Cost Curves — Economies of Scale

What happens to the long-run \(ATC\) curve as a firm grows?

  • The answer reveals information about economies in the production process.
  • Similar to returns to scale — but focused on the cost side.

Economies of Scale: costs rise more slowly than production.

Constant Economies of Scale: costs rise at the same rate as output.

Diseconomies of Scale: costs rise more quickly than production.

Introduces the three regions of the LR-ATC curve: economies of scale (LRATC falling), constant economies (flat), diseconomies (rising). This is the LR-ATC analog of returns-to-scale from production theory, but stated in COST terms. The key distinction the next slide will make: returns to scale require all inputs scale proportionally; economies of scale don't impose that — the firm picks any input mix that's cost-minimizing at each output. The mini figure shows a stylized U-shape with three colored arrows under it marking the regions.

The U-Shape Implication — Not the Same as Returns to Scale

Given these relationships, what does the common “U-shape” of the long-run \(ATC\) curve imply for production?

  • At first, average cost per unit falls (economies of scale). Eventually, as output rises considerably, diseconomies of scale take hold.

What factors might cause diseconomies of scale to set in?

  • Overcrowding, overutilization of capital, organizational complexity, …

Not the same as returns to scale!

  • Returns to scale describes how production changes when all inputs are changed by a common factor.
  • Economies of scale does not impose this “common factor” rule on input proportions — the firm picks the cheapest mix at each \(Q\).
Critical distinction students often blur. Returns to scale is a PRODUCTION concept — what happens to Q when all inputs scale by t. Economies of scale is a COST concept — what happens to ATC as Q grows, with the firm free to pick any input bundle. They can diverge: even pure CRS (constant returns to scale) production can produce a U-shaped LR-ATC if input prices vary with quantity, or if the firm has lumpy fixed costs that get amortized at first then bind. Diseconomies usually come from organizational coordination costs (rising with firm size) — labor monitoring, communication overhead, multi-layer management — none of which is a pure-production phenomenon. The book makes this distinction explicit; the lecture should too.

Application — Are Rooftop Solar Panels Driving Up Electricity Costs?

Concrete application of economies of scale on the cost side. Residential rooftop solar costs roughly 2× commercial solar per kWh — same panels, same tech, but installation, financing, and maintenance overhead don't scale on a single-home installation the way they do on a 100-MW utility plant. CA's regulated pricing has a cross-subsidy structure (high marginal kWh price recovers grid fixed costs from non-rooftop customers), which gives an artificial incentive to put panels on your own roof. Once enough households defect to rooftop, the fixed-cost burden on the remaining customers rises — pushing more to install rooftop, etc. Stanford economist Severin Borenstein has documented this dynamic; the citation in textbook is to MIT energy researchers (Trancik et al.). Economies of Scope

Economies of Scope

The simultaneous production of multiple products comes at a lower cost than if a firm made each product separately and added up the costs.

Why might a firm observe economies of scope?

  1. Flexible inputs or production processes
    • Oil refineries produce many petroleum products simultaneously via distillation — a single barrel yields gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, LPG, and more at a much lower aggregate cost than producing each separately.
  2. Expertise translatable across products / services
    • For instance, life and auto insurance — same actuarial and claims-handling know-how.

Products from one barrel of crude oil (gallons, 2011)

Definition and intuition. Economies of scope = it's cheaper for one firm to make multiple goods together than for several firms to specialize in one each. Two distinct mechanisms: (1) Joint production / flexible inputs — a single physical process (oil distillation) splits an input (crude) into multiple outputs. The 42-gallon barrel produces ~45 gallons of refined products (slight volume gain from cracking). (2) Knowledge spillovers — same skilled workforce, IT systems, risk models, etc. can serve multiple product lines. Insurance is a classic example: actuaries who price life policies can also price auto, with shared overhead. Don't confuse with economies of SCALE (one product, growing quantity); scope is multi-product. Both lower total cost, but through different mechanisms.

  • \(TC(Q_1, Q_2)\) — total cost for a single firm to produce both.

  • \(TC(Q_1, 0) = \$90\text{B}\)   (Pro Max only)
  • \(TC(0, Q_2) = \$70\text{B}\)   (iPhone 17 only)
  • \(TC(Q_1, Q_2) = \$125\text{B}\)   (joint)
\( \text{SCOPE} = \dfrac{(\$90\text{B} + \$70\text{B}) - \$125\text{B}}{\$125\text{B}} = \dfrac{\$35\text{B}}{\$125\text{B}} = \mathbf{0.28} \)

SCOPE \(= 0.28 > 0\) ⇒ Apple gains strong economies of scope. Producing both phones together saves 28% versus two specialist firms producing them separately.

Hands-on application of the SCOPE measure. The formula is just the percentage cost savings from joint production relative to the joint cost. If SCOPE > 0, there ARE economies of scope; if SCOPE < 0 (joint cost exceeds separate sum), there are DISeconomies of scope — better to split. Numbers are stylized but plausible: Apple FY2024 iPhone segment revenue ~$200B, COGS roughly 55-60% = ~$110-120B, so joint TC ~$125B is reasonable. The "would have cost separately" $90B + $70B = $160B is the counterfactual where Apple ran two completely independent firms making each model, each with its own R&D, marketing, supply chain, retail, etc. Walk through the calc step-by-step: numerator 35, denominator 125, ratio 0.28. Teach the rule: positive SCOPE → joint production is cheaper.

Apple Pencil — cylindrical Li-ion cells

AirPods — same cylindrical Li-ion cells

APPLE PENCIL BATTERY   =   AIRPOD BATTERY

§8.8 Cost Minimization in Real Data

De Loecker, Eeckhout & Unger (2020) — Rising US Markups

The cost-min FOC \(w = \mu \cdot MP_L\) rearranges to:

\( \dfrac{wL}{pQ} \;=\; \dfrac{\mu}{p} \cdot \beta \;=\; \dfrac{\beta}{\text{markup}} \)

Holding \(\beta\) fixed, a falling labor share of revenue must come from a rising markup.

DEU 2020 QJE: US Compustat aggregate markup rose from 21% (1980) to 61% (2014) above marginal cost.

Methodology: De Loecker & Warzynski (2012 AER) directly inverted the cost-min FOC — the same FOC we wrote on slide 10. Empirical IO uses this chapter's machinery verbatim.

The chapter's headline empirical payoff. The cost-min FOC \(w = \mu \cdot MP_L\) gives us a way to back out the unobservable marginal cost from observable wage and output. Specifically: at the cost-min bundle, \(wL/pQ = (\mu/p) \beta = \beta/\text{markup}\). So with \(\beta\) estimated separately (from production-function regressions), the labor share of revenue identifies the markup. DEU 2020 applied this to US Compustat firms 1955-2014 and found markups rose from 21% to 61% above MC — concentrated in the upper tail. Methodology has critics (Bond, Hashemi, Kaplan, Zoch 2020 JME — substituting revenue elasticity for output elasticity makes the formula tautological); the theoretical underpinning is uncontested. Connect to Ch 11 (monopoly): the rising markup story is what Ch 11 will formalize as the Lerner index L = -1/ε.

Why TSMC Doesn't Worry About a Wage Shock

A 20% wage shock raises cost by the labor-share-weighted portion. At Cobb-Douglas \(\beta\):

  • Gigafactory (\(\beta = 0.6\)): \(\Delta c = 1.2^{0.6} - 1 = +11.6\%\)
  • TSMC fab (\(\beta \approx 0.3\)): \(\Delta c = 1.2^{0.3} - 1 = +5.6\%\)
  • Amazon WH (\(\beta \approx 0.7\)): \(\Delta c = 1.2^{0.7} - 1 = +13.7\%\)

Capital intensity insulates. A US TSMC fab can absorb a 200% US-vs-Taiwan wage gap with only ~6% higher cost. A battery plant cannot.

Source: BLS Industry Productivity Database, NAICS 3359 (batteries) and NAICS 3344 (semiconductors); TSMC Arizona fab data from 2024 Reuters.

Operational answer to "why are US battery plants in trouble but US semiconductor fabs are fine?" The technology's capital intensity (captured by \(\alpha\)) determines wage-shock sensitivity. TSMC's Arizona fab cluster has Cobb-Douglas \(\alpha \approx 0.7\), \(\beta \approx 0.3\) (from BLS NAICS 3344 cost shares within K+L). A 20% wage shock raises cost only ~6% because labor is a small share. Tesla's gigafactory has \(\alpha = 0.4, \beta = 0.6\) — wage shock raises cost ~11.6%. Amazon warehouses have \(\beta \approx 0.7\) — even more sensitive. The structural parameter \(\beta\) IS the wage-shock pass-through elasticity. This is the empirical payoff of cost-share = production exponent (slide 15 takeaway). For TSMC, the $65B Arizona investment is justified by capital-intensity insulation against the wage gap.

Historical Note — Ronald Shephard (1912-1982)

Ronald William Shephard, American mathematical economist. Born Wichita, Kansas; PhD in mathematics, Princeton 1940.

World War II at the RAND Project (precursor to RAND Corp.), applying operations research to military logistics — a problem domain dense with cost-minimization structure.

1953-1976: Professor of Operations Research at UC Berkeley. Fellow of the Econometric Society, 1968.

The 1953 monograph

Cost and Production Functions, Princeton Mathematical Notes (1953). Theorem 6 is what we now call Shephard's lemma.

Written, by Shephard's own admission, "in self-defense" — he kept finding economists confusing the algebraic relationship between \(c\) and \(f\). The proof uses the support-function approach from convex analysis — a generation ahead of the rest of the field.

Mainstream economics took ~20 years to absorb it. By the mid-1970s "Shephard's lemma" was a textbook term. No Nobel — but every empirical-IO paper of the last 50 years uses his identity.

Historical bracket. Shephard built the duality framework that the entire chapter rests on. The 1953 monograph was self-published as a RAND technical report, distributed primarily within Cowles Commission circles. Mainstream economics absorbed it slowly. The lemma is now in every intermediate text — Varian, Nicholson-Snyder, Perloff, Goolsbee. No Nobel — Shephard was a methodological economist whose contributions were absorbed into the standard graduate apparatus rather than associated with a flashy empirical finding. But the duality framework underlies modern empirical IO: De Loecker-Warzynski markup estimation, Hsieh-Klenow misallocation, Olley-Pakes / Levinsohn-Petrin / ACF production-function estimation — all rest on Shephard's identities. He died in 1982 at age 70.

The Rental Rate \(r\) Is Really an Interest Rate FYI

All chapter we wrote \(TC = wL + rK\) and took \(r\) as handed down by the market (\(r = 0.13\)/yr at the gigafactory). But what is \(r\)? Tie up \$1 in a machine and you give up what that dollar would have earned elsewhere. That forgone return is the cost of capital.

What sits inside \(r\)

The interest rate — what the money could earn instead — plus depreciation, since the machine wears out. Nothing more mysterious than that.

The investment rule: keep adding capital until the return on the last unit equals the interest rate. Exactly the logic of \(MP_K/r = MP_L/w\), read one input at a time.

Why you already know what happens next

2010–2021: fed funds near 0.8% ⇒ \(r\) low ⇒ capital cheap relative to labor ⇒ the automation and AI capex wave.

2022–2024: hikes to ~5.3% ⇒ \(r\) jumps ⇒ the isocost line rotates ⇒ firms tilt back toward labor.

That is §8.2’s comparative statics — only now the shock comes from the Fed, not from a wage negotiation.

A central bank decision is, quite literally, a shock to one of the two prices in your cost-minimization problem.

The single most useful idea in this appendix: r is not a mystery parameter, it is (interest rate + depreciation). Two payoffs. (1) It retro-explains the chapter's own implicit-cost discussion — the "normal rate of return" and the owner's forgone investment return in the Costs That Matter unit are the SAME object as r. (2) It makes monetary policy a cost-minimization shock: when the Fed moved from ~0.8% to ~5.3%, r rose, the isocost line rotated, and the cost-min K/L ratio tilted toward labor — precisely the rotation students drew in §8.2, and the mirror image of the UAW wage shock. Good place to ask: "we did a wage shock in §8.4 — draw the rate-hike version yourselves." Answer: isocost pivots the other way; K falls, L rises.

So Who Sets That Rate? FYI

Panel A — the market

Savers supply funds (slopes up — higher rates reward saving). Borrowers demand them (slopes down — lower rates make more projects pay). They meet at \(i_0\).

Panel B — the firm

The firm is a price-taker in the credit market. It takes \(i_0\) as given and invests up to \(I_0\), where \(MRP_K = i_0\).

The linkage

More saving ⇒ \(S_0 \to S_1\) ⇒ rate falls to \(i_1\) ⇒ every firm slides down its \(MRP_K\) curve and invests more \((I_1)\).

This is where our \(r\) comes from. Just as the labor market handed the firm \(w\), the credit market hands it the interest rate inside \(r\). The firm is a price-taker in both — which is exactly why \(w\) and \(r\) sit in \(TC = wL + rK\) as given numbers rather than choices.

Vertical sub-slide under "The Rental Rate r Is Really an Interest Rate", adapted from ECON 1116 Ch 15 (loanable-funds-firm), figure reused. It answers the obvious follow-up to the parent slide: if r contains an interest rate, WHO SETS THAT RATE? Answer: the loanable-funds market, and the individual firm takes it as given. Walk the two panels left to right. PANEL A: saving supply slopes up, borrowing demand slopes down, equilibrium at i0 — this is a market the firm participates in but cannot move. PANEL B: the firm's MRP_K curve slopes down (diminishing marginal product of capital, straight out of Ch 7), and it invests until MRP_K equals the going rate. THE COMPARATIVE STATIC to draw with them: a savings glut or a Fed cut shifts S0 to S1, the rate falls to i1, and every firm slides down its MRP_K curve to a higher I1 — the economy-wide version of the automation-wave story on the parent slide. That is the whole justification for treating w and r as parameters in TC = wL + rK. But First You Have to Get the Money FYI

Cost-minimization quietly assumes the firm can buy whatever \(K\) the tangency calls for. In practice the money has to come from somewhere — and which door is open to you depends on what kind of firm you are.

1. Bank loans — steady cash flow, real collateral

2. Bonds — borrow from many investors at once

3. Stocks / IPO — sell ownership itself

4. Venture capital — bets on unproven, fast-growing firms

5. Private equity — buy and restructure mature firms

Sam’s burger shop realistically gets door 1 — a bank loan, priced against collateral and last year’s cash flow. Doors 3–5 are effectively closed to him. Tesla and SpaceX live behind doors 3, 4 and 5.

Bridge slide, adapted from ECON 1116 Ch 15's "Five Channels" (figure reused). The economics point for 2316: our cost-min model treats K as freely purchasable at price r, but the CHANNEL determines both whether a firm can reach its optimal K at all and what r it actually faces. A bank prices a burger-shop loan off collateral and demonstrated cash flow; an equity investor prices off expected future growth. Same textbook symbol r, radically different real number and radically different access. This is the setup for the next slide's contrast — do not moralize yet, just lay out the plumbing.

Door 1 — Bank Loans FYI

Bank loans — borrow from one bank, repay with interest, usually pledge collateral. The most common source of outside capital for most firms.

Commercial & industrial loans — granted on credit history, business potential, and collateral.

Limitations — banks are conservative: amounts are capped, rates rise for riskier borrowers, and unproven ideas are often denied.

This is Sam’s door. A bank prices his loan off collateral and last year’s receipts — so the \(r\) he faces is set by his past, not by his prospects. That is the whole constraint in one sentence.

Adapted from ECON 1116 Ch 15. The 2316 point is in the closing box: the CHANNEL determines the r the firm actually faces. A bank underwrites backward-looking evidence — collateral, credit history, demonstrated cash flow — so a profitable but unspectacular business gets a capped loan at a rate reflecting its past. Contrast this with equity investors on the coming slides, who underwrite forward-looking expectations. Same symbol r in TC = wL + rK, radically different number and radically different access.

Door 2 — Corporate Bonds FYI

When bank borrowing is insufficient or too restrictive, firms issue bonds — IOU certificates promising to repay over time to thousands of investors at once.

Three parts: face value (par, repaid at maturity) · coupon rate (periodic fixed interest) · maturity date (months to decades, even perpetual).

Yield = coupon ÷ current price. Yield rises with risk — riskier bonds must pay more. Advantage: risk is spread across many investors. Catch: a bond is a fixed claim against the firm, and future issuance depends on its credit rating.

US firms issued over \$1.5 trillion in bonds in 2024 — the bond market is roughly twice the size of the stock market.

Bonds are still debt — fixed repayment, no ownership given up. The firm’s credit rating is what sets the yield it must offer, and that yield is the interest rate sitting inside its \(r\).

Merges ECON 1116 Ch 15's "Corporate Bonds" and "The Bond Market" slides at the instructor's request. Teaching sequence: a bond scales a loan across thousands of lenders, which lowers the rate versus a single bank. Yield = coupon/price, so yield moves INVERSELY with price and RISES with risk. Both loans and bonds are DEBT — fixed claims, no dilution of ownership. The scale point (roughly 2x the stock market) surprises students who assume equities dominate corporate finance. The 2316 hook is the closing box: credit rating -> yield -> the interest rate component of r. A downgrade is, mechanically, an increase in one of the two input prices in TC = wL + rK.

Door 3 — Stocks & IPOs FYI

Stocks sell partial ownership (equity). Shareholders are owners; an IPO is the first public sale. Market cap = the firm’s stock-market value; capital gains = price rises. Traded on NYSE / Nasdaq.

Advantages: raise big capital, no repayment obligation, limited liability.

Disadvantages: give up ownership and profits (dividends), mandatory disclosure, outside influence on management.

Equity has no coupon. Unlike debt, nothing must be repaid on a schedule — which is exactly why it funds long-horizon, uncertain projects that no bank would underwrite. Press ↓ for the largest example ever.

Adapted from ECON 1116 Ch 15. Equity = selling ownership: no repayment obligation, but dilution of control and profits. The 2316 framing to stress: debt requires a contractual repayment stream, so lenders demand predictable cash flow; equity's return is residual, so equity investors can rationally fund a decade of losses if the expected payoff is large enough. That asymmetry, not virtue or luck, is why capital-hungry moonshots are equity-financed. Next slide: the SpaceX IPO, which actually happened in June 2026.

1. You would be working for free

If the business returns exactly the market rate, economic profit = 0 — the normal rate of return from §8.1. You supplied labour, bore risk, and gave up liquidity, and were paid only what the passive alternative pays. To be worth it, the business must beat the market rate — that margin is the compensation for effort and risk.

2. Everyone cannot index

The fund’s 10% is not created by the fund — it is the earnings of the real firms it holds. If nobody built or ran those firms, there would be nothing to index and no 10%. Individually rational, collectively impossible: the passive return is derived from production, so someone must produce.

3a. The case that it is fair

Capital markets price expected future cash flows, which is exactly how genuinely new things get funded — nobody can collateralize a reusable rocket. Sam’s shop is already financeable precisely because it is proven.

3b. The case that it is not

Story-driven capital also inflates bubbles (Cisco, GameStop) and starves ordinary productive firms. Access tracks narrative and networks as much as merit — and Sam’s output is real today.

Positive economics says where capital flows. Whether you like the destination is normative — and that one is yours.

Chapter Summary

Cost-min problem

\(\min wL + rK\) s.t. \(f(K, L) \geq q\). Lagrangian FOCs ⇒ tangency \(\text{MRTS}_{LK} = w/r\); equimarginal \(MP_L/w = MP_K/r\).

Cost function (Cobb-Douglas)

\(c = (\alpha+\beta)(q/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}(w/\beta)^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}(r/\alpha)^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}\). Under CRS, linear in \(q\); cost shares = production exponents.

Marginal & average cost

\(MC = \partial c/\partial q = \mu\) by envelope theorem. \(MC = AC\) at \(AC\)'s minimum. Under CRS, \(MC = AC\) everywhere.

Shephard's lemma

\(\partial c/\partial w = L^*,\; \partial c/\partial r = K^*\). Recover conditional input demands from the cost function by differentiation.

SR vs LR

SR: \(K\) fixed at \(\bar K\). LR: both adjust. LR cost = lower envelope of SR family. SR isn't a clock-time concept — it's a flexibility concept.

Duality framework

Cost function and production function are equivalent representations of the firm's technology. Same machinery as consumer expenditure ↔ Hicksian.

Coming Up — Chapter 9: Profit Maximization & Firm Supply

Today we built the cost function \(c(w, r, q)\) and the conditional input demands — what the firm spends given output.

Chapter 9 asks the second producer-theory question: given the cost function and an output price \(p\), how much output to produce?

\( \max_q \;\; p \cdot q - c(w, r, q) \quad \Rightarrow \quad p = \dfrac{\partial c}{\partial q} = MC \)

The headline result \(p = MC\) is the envelope-theorem identity from today, viewed from the output-choice side:

  • Supply curve: The firm's supply is inverse-\(MC\) (above min AVC) — built directly from today's cost function.
  • Hotelling's lemma: Same envelope mechanics as Shephard — \(\partial \pi/\partial p = q^*\), recovers supply from profit function.
  • Shutdown decision: Run iff \(p \geq \min AVC\) (the SR/LR distinction from today determines AVC).
  • Tesla wage shock cont'd: Even when \(q\) endogenizes, the cost-min mix at each \(q\) is what we built today.
