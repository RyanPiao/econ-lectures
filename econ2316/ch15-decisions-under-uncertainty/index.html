§15.1

Lotteries & Expected Value

The right way to describe a risky prospect — and why its average payoff is only half the story

A Lottery Is States, Payoffs, and Probabilities

A lottery lists state-contingent payoffs and their probabilities:

\(L = (x_1, \pi_1;\ x_2, \pi_2;\ \ldots;\ x_n, \pi_n)\)

with \(\pi_i \geq 0\) and \(\sum_i \pi_i = 1\). The expected value is the probability-weighted average payoff:

\(E[L] = \sum_{i=1}^n \pi_i\, x_i\)

A fair coin paying \$50 or \$150 has \(E = \$100\). A 1-in-100 hospitalization costing \$50,000 has \(E[\text{loss}] = \$500\).

Heads Up 15.1 — \(\pi_i\) is a probability, not a profit

In Chapters 9–14, \(\pi\) meant profit. From here on, \(\pi_i\) is the probability of state \(i\). The letter \(p\) is reserved for prices and per-unit premiums. Read \(\pi_2 = 0.05\) as “state two realizes 5% of the time.”

This lets us write expected utility cleanly as \(\sum_i \pi_i\, u(x_i)\) without overloading any symbol.

What EV misses. A sure \$1 has \(E = 1\). So does “win \$1,000,000 with prob \(\tfrac12\), lose \$999,998 with prob \(\tfrac12\).” Same expected value — but almost no one is indifferent between them. Expected utility (§15.2) exists to formalize that difference.

Define the primitives carefully: a lottery is a probability distribution over payoffs (usually terminal wealth). EV is mechanical — the probability-weighted mean. Heads Up 15.1 is the notation hinge of the chapter: π flips from profit (Parts III–IV) to probability, permanently. Then the punchline that motivates everything: two lotteries with identical EV ($1) can be wildly different in risk, and real humans are not indifferent. EV is necessary but not sufficient to rank risky prospects — that gap is the whole reason for expected utility.

§15.2

Expected Utility & the vNM Axioms

Rank lotteries by the average utility of wealth — and risk attitude lives in the curvature of \(u\)

Expected Utility and Four Axioms

For an agent with Bernoulli utility \(u\) on wealth, the expected utility of a lottery is

\(EU[L] = \sum_{i=1}^n \pi_i\, u(x_i)\)

The agent ranks lotteries by \(EU\). Named for Daniel Bernoulli (1738), who used \(\ln W\) to resolve the St. Petersburg paradox — the fair price reflects expected utility, not expected payoff.

\(u\) is unique up to a positive affine transform \(\tilde u = a + bu\) (\(b>0\)): the level of utility is meaningless — only its shape matters.

Von Neumann–Morgenstern (1944): four axioms are jointly equivalent to the existence of such a \(u\).

Completeness — any two lotteries are comparable

Transitivity — rankings don't cycle

Continuity — no “lexicographic” jumps

Independence — mixing with a common third lottery \(C\) preserves the ranking

Independence is the substantive new axiom — and the one most often violated in the lab (Allais 1953, Ellsberg 1961). More on that at the close.

The representation theorem. EU is the probability-weighted average of a Bernoulli utility on wealth. Stress affine invariance: replacing u with a+bu (b>0) leaves all rankings unchanged, so only the curvature of u has economic content — the level is a free normalization. The first three axioms mirror Ch 4's deterministic preference axioms; independence is genuinely new — it says a common consequence C cancels out of a pairwise comparison. Flag that independence is exactly the axiom Allais and Ellsberg violate experimentally; that's the bridge to behavioral economics in Ch 18, previewed at the end of today.

Walkthrough 15.2 — Three Agents, One Lottery

Problem. A lottery pays \$0 or \$100, each with probability \(\tfrac12\), so \(E[L] = \$50\). Compare each agent's \(EU[L]\) to the sure \(u(50)\).

Risk-neutral — \(u=W\)

\(EU = \tfrac12(0)+\tfrac12(100) = 50\); \(u(50)=50\). Indifferent.

Risk-averse — \(u=\sqrt{W}\)

\(EU = \tfrac12(0)+\tfrac12(10) = 5\); \(u(50)=\sqrt{50}\approx 7.07\). \(5 < 7.07\) → takes the sure \$50.

Risk-loving — \(u=W^2\)

\(EU = \tfrac12(0)+\tfrac12(10{,}000) = 5{,}000\); \(u(50)=2{,}500\). \(5{,}000 > 2{,}500\) → takes the lottery.

Takeaway. Same \$50 expected payoff for all three. They differ only in how utility scales with wealth. Concave \(u\) = risk-averse (averaging utilities falls below the utility of average wealth); convex = risk-loving; linear = risk-neutral. Risk attitude is encoded entirely in the shape of \(u\).

Four reveals. Identical EV ($50) across three agents; the verdict flips purely with curvature. Risk-neutral u=W: EU = u(E[W]) exactly (the knife-edge). Risk-averse √W: EU = 5 < √50 ≈ 7.07, strictly prefers the sure thing. Risk-loving W²: EU = 5000 > 2500, strictly prefers the gamble. The geometric reading: for concave u, the average of the two endpoint utilities (the chord midpoint) sits below the curve at the average wealth. This is the “chord below curve” picture — and the next section proves it's exactly Jensen's inequality.

§15.3

Risk Aversion, Concavity & Jensen

Why the shape of \(u\) is risk attitude — a theorem, not a pattern

Calculus Corner 15.1 — Jensen's Inequality

Result. For concave \(u\) and any random wealth \(W\),

\(E[u(W)] \leq u(E[W])\)

strict for strictly concave \(u\) on any non-degenerate \(W\).

Proof sketch. Concavity ⇒ \(u\) lies below its tangent at \(\bar W = E[W]\):

\(u(W) \leq u(\bar W) + u'(\bar W)(W - \bar W)\)

Take expectations; the linear term has mean \(0\), leaving \(E[u(W)] \leq u(\bar W)\).

Equivalence: \(u\) is concave \(\Longleftrightarrow\) the agent is risk-averse. Concavity is necessary and sufficient.

Chord below curve: \(EU \approx 4.461 < u(E[W]) \approx 4.605\). The gap is risk aversion.

The theorem behind W15.2's pattern. Jensen: for concave u, the expectation of u is at most u of the expectation. The one-line proof is the tangent-line trick — concavity puts u below every tangent, so below the tangent at the mean; taking expectations kills the linear term (mean-zero deviation), leaving the inequality. The equivalence is the load-bearing statement: concavity is not just sufficient for risk aversion, it's necessary — if u bends the wrong way anywhere, a 50/50 lottery between those points violates risk aversion. The figure is the log-utility {50,150} picture: green dot u(E[W]) on the curve, red dot EU on the chord, the gap is the utils-denominated risk premium that §15.4 converts to dollars.

Walkthrough 15.3 — Log-Utility Certainty Equivalent

Problem. \(u(W)=\ln W\); a 50/50 lottery lands wealth at \$50 or \$150. Find \(EU\), the certainty equivalent \(CE\), and the risk premium \(\rho\).

1. \(E[W] = \tfrac12(50)+\tfrac12(150) = 100\).

2. \(EU = \tfrac12\ln 50 + \tfrac12\ln 150 = \tfrac12\ln 7{,}500 \approx 4.460\).

3. \(CE = e^{EU} = \sqrt{7{,}500} = 50\sqrt{3} \approx \$86.60\).

4. \(\rho = E[W] - CE = 100 - 86.60 \approx \$13.40\).

Under log utility, the \(CE\) is the geometric mean \(\sqrt{W_L W_H}\), always below the arithmetic mean — the gap is \(\rho\).

Jensen again: \(E[u(W)] = 2.5 < u(E[W]) = 2.739\).

Five reveals — the canonical CE computation. The log trick: ½ln50 + ½ln150 = ½ln(50·150) = ½ln7500, so CE = exp(EU) = √7500 = 50√3 ≈ 86.60. Risk premium ρ = 100 − 86.60 = 13.40: Maya would pay up to \$13.40 to convert this lottery into its certain mean. The deep fact: under log utility the certainty equivalent of a 50/50 lottery is the GEOMETRIC mean of the two outcomes, which is always below the arithmetic mean — and the AM–GM gap is exactly the risk premium. The figure is a second Jensen picture with √W and a four-point lottery, reinforcing “centroid below curve.”

Walkthrough 15.4 — Quadratic-Utility CE

Problem & solve

Problem. \(u(W) = 2W - W^2/100\) on \([0,100]\), with \(u'>0\). A 50/50 lottery between \$40 and \$60.

\(u(40) = 80 - 16 = 64\); \(u(60) = 120 - 36 = 84\). \(EU = \tfrac12(64)+\tfrac12(84) = 74\).

Solve \(2\,CE - CE^2/100 = 74\) ⇒ \(CE^2 - 200\,CE + 7{,}400 = 0\).

\(CE = 100 - \sqrt{2{,}600} \approx 49.01\) (lower root, since \(CE \leq E[W]\)).

Check & compare

\(\rho = 50 - 49.01 \approx \$0.99\). Check: \(u(49.01) \approx 98.02 - 24.02 = 74.00\) ✓.

Tiny premium (\$0.99) vs log utility's \$13.40 — because both the curvature and the spread (\(\pm10\) vs \(\pm50\)) are smaller here.

Caveat. Quadratic utility has a known defect — increasing absolute risk aversion in wealth (backwards) — but it gives a clean closed-form, which is why mean-variance analysis uses it.

Takeaway. Any concave Bernoulli utility gives a \(CE\) via \(u(CE) = EU\); the risk premium scales with both local curvature and lottery spread. Differences across functional forms reduce to differences in Arrow–Pratt curvature — the subject of §15.5.

Six reveals. The quadratic case shows the same recipe with a closed-form quadratic solve: compute EU directly, then solve u(CE)=EU. Because expected utility of a quadratic depends only on the mean and variance of the lottery, this is the form behind mean-variance portfolio theory. The risk premium here (\$0.99) is far below the log case (\$13.40) for two reasons — smaller spread (±10 vs ±50) and different curvature. Flag the IARA defect (risk aversion rising in wealth is empirically backwards) so students don't over-use quadratic. The unifying claim: all these differences collapse to local Arrow–Pratt curvature, motivating §15.5.

§15.4

Certainty Equivalent & Risk Premium

The dollar value of eliminating a gamble — and what an insurer can extract

CE, Risk Premium, and the Insurance Reading

The certainty equivalent of a lottery is the sure payoff with the same utility:

\(CE = u^{-1}\!\big(E[u(L)]\big), \qquad \rho = E[L] - CE\)

Risk-averse ⇒ \(\rho > 0\); risk-neutral ⇒ \(\rho = 0\); risk-loving ⇒ \(\rho < 0\).

Insurance reading. \(\rho\) is exactly how much extra, above the actuarially fair premium, a risk-averse agent will pay for full coverage. An insurer can load premia up to \(\rho\) before she walks away.

\(\rho = E[W] - CE \approx \$13.40\) — the horizontal red segment.

The size of \(\rho\) depends on three things: the curvature of \(u\), where the lottery sits on the wealth axis, and the spread of the lottery. The next Walkthrough varies the spread.

Define CE as the inverse-utility of expected utility (well-defined because strictly increasing u is invertible), and ρ = E[L] − CE as the dollar premium. The insurance interpretation is the bridge to §15.6: facing a loss, terminal wealth is variable if uninsured, certain (W₀ − E[loss]) if fully insured at the fair premium. ρ is the maximum extra above fair she'd pay to convert variable into certain — the rent an insurer can extract. The figure shows the horizontal-distance reading of ρ on the wealth axis (vs the vertical utils gap in figure 15-2). Set up the three drivers of ρ: curvature, location, spread — W15.5 isolates spread.

Walkthrough 15.5 — Risk Premium as the Spread Grows

Problem. \(u(W)=\ln W\), \(W_0=100\), fair 50/50 lottery \(\pm\Delta\). Express \(CE(\Delta)\) and \(\rho(\Delta)\), then evaluate.

\(EU = \tfrac12\ln\!\big[(100-\Delta)(100+\Delta)\big] = \tfrac12\ln(10{,}000-\Delta^2)\).

\(CE = \sqrt{10{,}000 - \Delta^2}, \qquad \rho(\Delta) = 100 - \sqrt{10{,}000-\Delta^2}\).

\(\Delta\)\(CE\)\(\rho\)
10\(\approx 99.50\)\(\approx \$0.50\)
50\(\approx 86.60\)\(\approx \$13.40\)
99\(\approx 14.11\)\(\approx \$85.89\)

Small risks, small premia. A \(\pm\$10\) gamble (10% of wealth) costs only \(\approx\$0.50\) — half a percent of wealth.

Catastrophic tails, huge premia. Pushing the bad state from \$50 to \$1 (\(\Delta: 50 \to 99\)) raises \(\rho\) from \$13 to \$86 — far more than linear. Log utility cannot tolerate a positive chance of ruin.

This second regularity is why insurance markets exist: people pay premia against catastrophic loss that far exceed expected loss — so insurers can load above the fair price and still sell.

Four reveals. The closed form: CE = √(10000 − Δ²), ρ = 100 − CE. The table delivers two regularities. (1) Small risks have small premia: a 10%-of-wealth gamble costs ~0.5% of wealth — the basis of the Arrow–Pratt approximation next section. (2) Catastrophic tails have disproportionate premia: ρ grows superlinearly in Δ, exploding as the bad state approaches ruin (log utility → −∞ at zero wealth). The economic punchline: insurance markets exist primarily because of regularity (2) — the premium someone pays against a small chance of catastrophe vastly exceeds expected loss, leaving room for the insurer's loading. This tees up the entire §15.6 insurance section.

§15.5

The Arrow–Pratt Coefficient

A local measure of curvature — CARA, CRRA, log — and \(\rho \approx \tfrac12\sigma^2 A(W)\)

Calculus Corner 15.2 — The Small-Risk Approximation

Absolute and relative risk aversion:

\(A(W) = -\dfrac{u''(W)}{u'(W)}, \qquad R(W) = W\,A(W)\)

\(A>0\) for concave \(u\); zero for linear; invariant to affine transforms (the \(b\)'s cancel). \(R\) is dimensionless — comparable across wealth levels.

Result. For a small zero-mean risk \(\tilde\varepsilon\) with variance \(\sigma^2\):

\(\rho \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma^2\, A(W)\)

Derivation. Taylor-expand to second order:

\(E[u(W+\tilde\varepsilon)] \approx u(W) + \tfrac12 u''(W)\sigma^2\)

Expand the CE side \(u(W-\rho) \approx u(W) - u'(W)\rho\), equate, and solve:

\(\rho = -\tfrac12\dfrac{u''(W)}{u'(W)}\sigma^2 = \tfrac12\sigma^2 A(W)\)

The risk premium is half the variance times local risk aversion — the bedrock of insurance pricing and portfolio choice. Excellent for small risks; degrades quadratically for large ones.

The chapter's central derivation. A(W) = −u''/u' is the local curvature measure; the minus sign and division by u' make it positive for concave u and invariant to affine renormalization — so it's a property of preferences, not of the chosen utility scale. R(W) = W·A(W) is the dimensionless version reported in empirical work. The small-risk result is a two-sided Taylor expansion: expand u(W+ε) to second order (mean-zero kills the first-order term, leaving ½u''σ²) and expand the CE side u(W−ρ) to first order; equate and solve. Out pops ρ ≈ ½σ²A(W). This is THE workhorse approximation of applied risk economics — verify it numerically next.

CARA, CRRA, and Log — Three Workhorses

Form\(u(W)\)\(A(W)\)\(R(W)\)
CARA\(-e^{-\alpha W}\)\(\alpha\) (const)\(\alpha W\)
CRRA\(\frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\)\(\gamma/W\)\(\gamma\) (const)
Log\(\ln W\)\(1/W\)\(1\)

CARA: coverage independent of wealth — convenient, but real agents show decreasing ARA. CRRA: constant \(R\), the macro-finance workhorse. Log is CRRA at \(\gamma=1\) — the canonical knife-edge. Quadratic has the IARA defect (W15.4).

CRRA \(\gamma=2\) is more risk-averse than log; CARA's risk aversion is constant in \(W\).

The three forms every applied paper uses, organized by what stays constant. CARA (u = −e^{−αW}): A(W) = α constant, so optimal coverage and asset holdings are independent of initial wealth — analytically convenient but empirically wrong (real agents take on more risk as they get richer = decreasing ARA). CRRA (u = W^{1−γ}/(1−γ)): R(W) = γ constant, the macro-finance and consumption-CAPM standard. Log is the γ=1 limit (A=1/W, R=1), the case we've been computing all chapter. Quadratic is used only for closed-form mean-variance work despite its IARA flaw. The figure shows all three normalized; note CRRA γ=2 bends harder than log, CARA flattest at the right.

Heads Up 15.4 — one \(\gamma\) does not fit all

Lab lotteries: \(\gamma \in [0.15, 0.68]\). Labor supply: \(\gamma \approx 1\). Portfolios: \(\gamma \approx 2\). Deductible choice: \(\gamma\) in the hundreds. Equity premium: \(\gamma \approx 30\). No single global \(\gamma\) reconciles these — a systematic puzzle, not noise.

The numerical sanity check on CC 15.2. Under log utility A(W₀)=1/W₀ and the ±Δ lottery has variance Δ², so the AP formula collapses to ρ ≈ Δ²/(2W₀). The table shows near-perfect agreement for small Δ and graceful quadratic degradation: at Δ=1000 (10% of wealth) the error is still only 0.25%. Conclusion: trust the approximation for auto deductibles and small co-pays; use exact CE for catastrophic-loss insurance (§15.6). Heads Up 15.4 is the empirical bombshell: the SAME parameter γ estimated from lab lotteries (~0.3), labor supply (~1), portfolios (~2), deductibles (hundreds), and the equity premium (~30) refuses to agree — motivating the behavioral preview and the heterogeneity data next.

What the Data Say: Heterogeneity & Probability Weighting

Theory and Data 15.1 — Cohen & Einav (2007, AER)

105,000+ Israeli auto-insurance deductible choices. The headline is enormous heterogeneity: the median household's \(A(W) \approx 2.6\times10^{-5}\) (RRA \(\approx 0.37\), like the lab) — but the mean runs two orders of magnitude higher (RRA near 97) thanks to a long right tail.

A single representative \(\gamma\) is empirically inadequate — markets price against a distribution of risk preferences.

Theory and Data 15.2 — Barseghyan et al. (2013, AER)

4,170 households across home, auto-collision, and auto-comprehensive deductibles. A 5% claim probability is acted on as if it were 11% — \(\Omega(0.05)=0.11\), a 2.2× overweighting.

Probability weighting, not utility curvature, drives most of the deductible-choice puzzle. EU+CRRA cannot fit; EU with the empirical \(\Omega(p)\) can.

Two readings of the same anomaly: people behave as if very risk-averse over small stakes. Either curvature is heterogeneous and fat-tailed (Cohen–Einav), or small probabilities are overweighted (Barseghyan) — the behavioral resolution previewed at the close and developed in Ch 18.

The empirical reality check before insurance. Cohen–Einav 2007: structural estimation on 100k+ deductible choices finds the median agent is mildly risk-averse (RRA ≈ 0.37, matching Holt–Laury lab values) but the distribution has a long right tail, so the MEAN RRA is ~97 — absurd as a global parameter. Lesson: there is no representative agent; markets face a distribution. Barseghyan et al. 2013 nests EU+CRRA inside a probability-weighting model and finds the weighting function, not the utility curvature, does the work — a 5% risk is treated like 11%. Both point the same direction: the “deductible puzzle” (next, Heads Up 15.3) is resolved by heterogeneity + probability weighting, the behavioral departures of Ch 18.

§15.6

Insurance Demand: Fair & Loaded

The chapter's payoff — Mossin's theorem answers Maya's question

The Insurance Problem

Buy coverage \(q \in [0, L]\) at per-dollar premium \(p\), paying \(pq\) up front:

No loss (prob \(1-\pi\)): \(W_g = W_0 - pq\)

Loss (prob \(\pi\)): \(W_b = W_0 - pq - L + q\)

The wealth gap \(W_g - W_b = L - q\): full coverage \(q=L\) closes it. She maximizes \(EU(q) = (1-\pi)u(W_g) + \pi\,u(W_b)\).

The premium structure is everything:

Actuarially fair: \(p = \pi\) — insurer breaks even on expectation.

Loaded: \(p > \pi\) — the wedge \((p-\pi)/\pi\) covers admin, capital, and profit. Real markets always load.

At the optimum she equalizes marginal utility per premium dollar across states. Concavity makes \(u'\) larger when wealth is smaller — pushing toward equal wealth across states.

Set up the canonical one-loss insurance model. Coverage q between 0 and the loss L, priced at p per dollar of coverage. Two states: good (W_g = W₀ − pq) and bad (W_b = W₀ − pq − L + q). Key identity: the wealth gap across states is L − q, the uncovered piece — full coverage closes it, zero coverage leaves full exposure. Define fair (p=π, break-even) vs loaded (p>π) premiums; emphasize real markets always load (ACA MLR caps non-claims load near 25%, auto/home 20–50%). The geometric intuition primes Mossin: concavity means u' is higher in the low-wealth bad state, so she wants to shift wealth toward the bad state — i.e., insure — until marginal utilities per premium dollar equalize. Poll #2 next asks how much she should buy at a FAIR price.

Poll #2 — How Much to Insure at a Fair Price?

A risk-averse agent is offered insurance at an actuarially fair premium (\(p = \pi\)). How much coverage should she buy?

A) Partial — depends on how risk-averse she is
B) Full coverage \(q = L\) — regardless of how risk-averse
C) None — the premium equals the expected loss
D) It depends on her initial wealth

45 sec to vote

Down arrow reveals. Answer is B — the counterintuitive Mossin result: at a fair premium, EVERY risk-averse agent fully insures, no matter how mildly or severely risk-averse. A is the seductive trap: students reason “more risk-averse → buy more,” but at a fair price even the barely-risk-averse buy 100%; curvature affects how much she'll pay above fair, not how much she buys at fair. C confuses break-even pricing with zero demand — she still wants to eliminate variance. D misfires: with CRRA/log the fair-premium full-coverage result is wealth-independent. The mechanism: fair pricing makes the budget line's slope equal the odds, so the optimum lands on the 45° equal-wealth line — full coverage. Spend time here; it's the chapter's sharpest prediction.

Answer: B — Full Coverage, Always

At a fair premium the first-order condition forces \(u'(W_b) = u'(W_g)\); strict concavity (so \(u'\) is one-to-one) gives \(W_b = W_g\), which means \(q = L\). This is Mossin's theorem.

Trap A: curvature sets how much she pays above fair (the risk premium), not how much she buys at fair. At \(p=\pi\), even a barely-risk-averse agent insures fully.

Calculus Corner 15.3 — The Mossin Theorem

Differentiate \(EU(q)\): good-state wealth falls at rate \(-p\), bad-state at rate \(1-p\). The FOC is

\(\dfrac{u'(W_b)}{u'(W_g)} = \dfrac{p(1-\pi)}{(1-p)\pi}\)

Fair (\(p=\pi\)): RHS \(= 1\), so \(u'(W_b)=u'(W_g)\). Strict concavity ⇒ \(W_b = W_g\) ⇒ \(q = L\): full coverage.

Loaded (\(p>\pi\)): RHS \(> 1\), so \(u'(W_b) > u'(W_g)\). Concavity ⇒ \(W_b < W_g\) ⇒ \(q < L\): partial coverage.

Paying an extra premium dollar when the loaded price exceeds the actuarial price costs more than it returns in expected indemnity — so she stops short of full coverage. Coverage is monotonically decreasing in the loading.

Mossin (1968, J. Political Economy 76(4), DOI 10.1086/259427).

The central applied prediction. Full coverage is optimal only at fair premiums; any positive loading makes partial coverage rational. Real-world partial coverage is not irrationality — it's the optimal response to loaded premia.

The derivation behind Poll #2. Differentiate EU(q) = (1−π)u(W_g) + πu(W_b): the good state depends on q through −pq (rate −p), the bad state through −pq+q (rate 1−p). Setting dEU/dq=0 yields the marginal-rate-of-substitution = price-ratio condition u'(W_b)/u'(W_g) = p(1−π)/[(1−p)π]. Fair pricing collapses the RHS to 1 → equal marginal utilities → equal wealth (strict concavity makes u' invertible) → full coverage. Loaded pricing pushes RHS above 1 → bad-state wealth strictly below good-state → partial coverage, and coverage falls monotonically as loading rises until the agent walks away. This reframes real partial coverage as rational, not a behavioral anomaly. Get the JPE citation right (not J Public Economics).

Walkthrough 15.7 — Maya's Optimal Coverage

Problem. \(W_0=\$60{,}000\), \(u=\ln W\), \(\pi=0.05\), \(L=\$50{,}000\). Find \(q^{\ast}\) at the fair \(p=0.05\) and at a 30%-loaded \(p=0.065\).

Fair \(p=0.05\): RHS \(=1\) ⇒ \(W_g=W_b\) ⇒ \(q^{\ast}=L=\$50{,}000\). Full coverage. Both states: \$57,500.

Loaded \(p=0.065\): RHS \(\approx 1.321\). Solve \(\tfrac{W_g}{W_b}=1.321\) ⇒ \(q^{\ast}\approx\$35{,}993\).

Coverage \(\approx 72\%\) of \(L\). She absorbs \(\sim\$14{,}007\) of uncovered loss; welfare loss vs the fair benchmark \(\approx \$663\)/yr.

Fair optimum on the 45° line (full); loaded optimum off it (\(q\approx0.72L\)).

Takeaway & forward-pointer. With a 30% loading, Maya rationally covers \(\sim\!72\%\) — exactly the high-deductible Silver-plan pattern. But the model assumes one premium independent of risk type. If insurers can't observe risk type, they face adverse selection — the failure of Ch 16.

Four reveals — the chapter capstone, on the opening vignette's numbers. Fair case: RHS=1, so full coverage, both states at \$57,500, EU = ln57500. Loaded case (30% loading, p=0.065): RHS ≈ 1.321; solving the log-utility FOC W_g/W_b = 1.321 with W_g = 60000−0.065q and W_b = W_g−50000+q gives q* ≈ \$35,993 = 72% of L. She self-insures ~\$14,007 of the bad-state loss; the certainty-equivalent welfare loss vs the unattainable fair benchmark is ~\$663/yr — small but real. The figure is the state-contingent (W_g, W_b) diagram: fair budget line hits the 45° full-coverage line, loaded budget line's optimum sits below it. Forward-pointer to Ch 16: the one-premium assumption breaks under asymmetric information → adverse selection.

Insurance Markets in the Wild

ACA marketplace 2026. Maya's exact Mossin problem — 23.1M plan selections, ARP credits expired (OBBBA), 9.96% income cap. High-deductible Silver = rational partial coverage under MLR-capped loading.

Holt–Laury (2002). Ten paired lotteries elicit CRRA; most subjects imply modest \(\gamma \in [0.15, 0.68]\) — far below what field insurance choices need.

Parametric insurance. USDA RMA (\$192B liabilities); African Risk Capacity paid \$59.6M on drought triggers in 2024 — fast, no moral hazard, but basis risk.

Cyber insurance. ~\$16B GWP (2025), but correlated risk (one Exchange bug fires claims at once) breaks the textbook independence assumption.

US coverage rose \(\sim\!85\%\to\sim\!92\%\) with the ACA (2014) and ARP (2021).

Four applications spanning the Mossin model's reach and its limits. ACA 2026 is the live version of W15.7 — high-deductible Silver plans are exactly the loaded-premium partial coverage the theorem predicts; note the 2026 policy context (ARP expiry, 9.96% cap, 23.1M selections, not yet effectuated). Holt–Laury is the lab benchmark (γ ~0.3) that field insurance choices wildly exceed. Parametric insurance (USDA, African Risk Capacity) shows a design that kills moral hazard and adverse selection via exogenous triggers — at the cost of basis risk. Cyber insurance is the cautionary tale: correlated risks violate the independence the textbook model assumes, so Mossin is a benchmark, not a description. The FRED figure shows the ACA/ARP coverage gains. LTC market collapse (next slide via Heads Up) is the adverse-selection bridge to Ch 16.

Two Puzzles the EU Benchmark Leaves Open

Heads Up 15.3 — the deductible puzzle (Sydnor, Rabin)

Sydnor (2010) studied 50,000 home policies: people pay heavy loadings to cut a deductible from \$1,000 to \$500, saving \(<\$100\)/yr in expectation. Rationalizing this under EU+CRRA needs \(\gamma\) in the hundreds.

Rabin (2000) calibration theorem: turning down a 50/50 lose-\$100 / win-\$110 gamble at every wealth implies turning down absurd large gambles. EU curvature cannot be both small-stakes-averse and large-stakes-sane.

Heads Up 15.5 — a behavioral preview (→ Ch 18)

Three departures from EU resolve the puzzles:

Loss aversion (Kahneman–Tversky 1979): losses weigh \(\lambda \approx 2.25\times\) gains, relative to a reference point.

Probability weighting (Tversky–Kahneman 1992): small probabilities overweighted, large ones underweighted.

Ambiguity aversion (Ellsberg 1961): known beats unknown probabilities.

EU is the benchmark, not the last word. It nails the structure — concavity, certainty equivalent, Mossin — but real small-stakes behavior needs reference dependence and probability weighting. That's Chapter 18.

The honest epilogue. Heads Up 15.3: the Sydnor deductible puzzle — people massively overpay to shave deductibles, implying EU γ in the hundreds, which Rabin's calibration theorem shows is internally incoherent (a parameter that explains small-stakes aversion mechanically predicts insane large-stakes rejections). So either people aren't EU maximizers, or u has different small-vs-large curvature. Heads Up 15.5: the three canonical behavioral fixes — loss aversion (λ≈2.25), inverse-S probability weighting, and ambiguity aversion (Ellsberg) — all developed in Ch 18. Frame EU correctly for students: it is the indispensable benchmark whose clean predictions (Mossin, CE, Arrow–Pratt) we keep, augmented descriptively by behavioral departures. Don't let them dismiss EU; don't let them over-trust it.

Chapter 15 — The Uncertainty Apparatus

Lotteries & expected value (§15.1). Risky prospects are distributions over wealth; \(E[L]=\sum\pi_i x_i\) is necessary but not sufficient to rank them.

Expected utility (§15.2). Rank by \(\sum\pi_i u(x_i)\); the vNM axioms justify it; only the shape of \(u\) matters.

Risk aversion = concavity (§15.3). Jensen: \(E[u(W)]\leq u(E[W])\). The chord-below-curve gap is the premium.

CE & risk premium (§15.4). \(CE=u^{-1}(EU)\), \(\rho=E[L]-CE\); grows with curvature and spread.

Arrow–Pratt (§15.5). \(A=-u''/u'\); \(\rho\approx\tfrac12\sigma^2 A(W)\). CARA / CRRA / log organize the forms.

Insurance & Mossin (§15.6). Full coverage at fair premia; partial at loaded. Maya's high-deductible plan is rational.

One apparatus. Value lotteries by expected utility on wealth; risk aversion equals concavity (Jensen); local curvature equals Arrow–Pratt; the dollar premium for small risks equals \(\tfrac12\sigma^2 A(W)\); risk-averse agents fully insure at fair premia (Mossin), partially at loaded. Ch 16 explains why real markets fail to deliver Mossin's prescription even for agents who would gladly pay fair premia.

The ladder, one rung per section, plus the unifying thread: a single apparatus runs from lotteries to insurance. If students keep one chain: EU on a concave u → Jensen makes concavity = risk aversion → the gap is the certainty-equivalent risk premium → locally that premium is ½σ²A(W) → applied to insurance, fair premia yield full coverage (Mossin) and loaded premia yield partial. Maya's loaded-premium high-deductible plan is the rational endpoint, not an anomaly. Bridge to Ch 16: the model assumed one risk-type-independent premium; when insurers can't observe risk type, adverse selection can unravel the very market that would give risk-averse agents the coverage they want.
