Why Do Oligopolies Exist?

The same barriers to entry from Ch 11 (Monopoly) — but here they leave a handful of survivors, not one.

Economies of scale

Airlines, autos, chips: minimum efficient scale is billions in fixed cost. A new airline must match Delta’s entire route network first.

Network & switching costs

Only three big cloud providers — AWS, Azure, Google Cloud — because firms embed their systems and can’t easily switch.

Patents & brand

Proprietary technology and brand loyalty wall off the market. Apple moves; Samsung must react — not a no-name startup.

High barriers → few firms → each is large enough to matter → strategic interdependence. The structure is built by the same forces that build monopoly — they just stop one step short.

Oligopoly: Your Best Move Depends on Theirs

An oligopoly is a market with a small number of firms whose payoffs depend on each other's choices. Each firm optimizes conditional on a conjecture about what rivals will do — and the three classic models differ only in what each firm holds fixed.

Cournot

Firms pick quantities simultaneously; each takes rivals' \(q\) as fixed. (§13.3)

Bertrand

Firms pick prices simultaneously; each takes rivals' \(P\) as fixed. (§13.4–13.5)

Stackelberg

One firm moves first and commits; the follower observes and responds. (§13.6)

Each conjecture yields a different equilibrium markup. Empirical IO matches each industry to the model that fits its strategic structure — quantity competition for capacity-constrained commodities, price competition for differentiated goods, sequential moves where one player can commit visibly first.

The roadmap slide. Stress that the three models are not rival theories of “the truth” — they are three conjecture structures, and the right one depends on the industry. Cournot fits capacity-set-then-sell (oil, electricity, see Kreps-Scheinkman in §13.7); Bertrand fits posted-price homogeneous goods; differentiated Bertrand fits branded consumer goods (streaming, pharma); Stackelberg fits visible first-mover commitment (Saudi in OPEC+). We do one section each.

Monopoly Benchmark: Kung Fu Tea Alone

A monopolist produces where marginal revenue = marginal cost. With linear demand, \(MR\) is twice as steep as demand:

\(TR = (19.99 - 0.03Q)\,Q \;\Rightarrow\; MR = 19.99 - 0.06Q\)

Set \(MR = MC\):   \(19.99 - 0.06Q = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; Q^{\ast} \approx 316.5\) cups

Price (read up on demand):   \(P^{\ast} = 19.99 - 0.03(316.5) = \$10.495\)

Profit:   \(\pi = (P^{\ast} - MC)\,Q^{\ast} \approx (10.495 - 1)(316.5) \approx \$3{,}005\)

That \(\approx\$3{,}005\) is the ceiling — the most this market can yield, earned when one decision-maker controls all output. Part 2's question: how much of it survives once rivals compete?

The Upper Bound: Why Don't They Just Collude?

If a few firms could perfectly coordinate, they would jointly play monopoly — pick total \(Q\) to maximize \(\pi^{M}\) and split it. With \(N\) identical firms, each makes \(q^{M}/N\) and earns \(\pi^{M}/N\). This cartel outcome is the highest profit any oligopoly can sustain. So why doesn't it always happen?

1. Antitrust. Sherman Act §1 forbids explicit price-fixing; executives face prison.

2. Coordination cost. With \(N \geq 3\), agreeing on each firm's quota takes repeated negotiation.

3. Cheating incentive. Even with an agreement, each firm gains by quietly undercutting — the deep one.

Recall from Principles — 1116 §13.4

A cartel is an agreement to coordinate output or price and split the profits. The 1116 treatment used OPEC and the prisoner's-dilemma payoff matrix: each member gains from the deal, but each also gains from cheating on it.

Ch 13 formalizes the cheating incentive as the Bertrand-undercutting logic (§13.4). Ch 14's repeated games decide whether cartels survive.

Reason 3 is the deep one: even with no antitrust exposure and zero coordination cost, each firm has a unilateral incentive to defect. For Ch 13 the cartel is an upper benchmark only — the ceiling every model below falls short of.

Set the cartel as the ceiling (\(L=0.667\) on our running calibration) the way perfect competition (Ch 10) is the floor (\(L=0\)). The three reasons cartels break: antitrust (institutional), coordination cost (mechanical), cheating incentive (strategic — the one that survives even when the first two vanish). The cheating incentive is exactly what Bertrand formalizes in §13.4 and what Ch 14's folk theorem revisits. Don't derive the cartel here; just bound the problem.

The Modern Variant: Algorithmic Pricing

The DOJ and seven state AGs sued RealPage (Aug 2024): its YieldStar software allegedly acted as a hub-and-spoke device, pooling non-public future-pricing data from competing landlords and recommending prices that softened competition — without explicit communication.

Nov 2025 proposed consent decree: stop training on non-public competitor data. Underlying class-action settlement \$141.8M (preliminarily approved Oct 2025).

Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò & Pastorello (2020, AER): simulated Q-learning algorithms can independently learn supracompetitive collusive strategies — with punishment phases — and no communication at all.

Heads Up 13.1 — algorithmic pricing isn't automatically collusion

“The algorithm produced high prices” does not equal “the algorithm broke antitrust law.” Pricing software has a legitimate use: estimating your own demand.

The violation requires an agreement to share non-public information among competitors, or parallel conduct plus facts suggesting coordination. Watch the data flow, not the price level. §13.5 shows supracompetitive prices are fully consistent with non-collusive differentiated-Bertrand equilibrium.

The legal hinge is the data flow: each landlord independently using a pricing tool is legal; competitors feeding non-public future prices into a shared hub that recommends back is the alleged violation. Cournot: Compete by Choosing Quantity

A local boba shop might compete on price. But producers in larger or commodity markets often have to set production first — capacity constraints mean a price cut can't instantly steal all of a rival's customers.

Cournot competition — an oligopoly model in which each firm chooses its production quantity rather than its price. The market price then falls out of the total quantity all firms produce.

Assumptions — Cournot with identical goods

  1. Only a small number of firms.
  2. Firms make identical products.
  3. Firms compete by choosing a quantity.
  4. All output sells at the market price, set by the sum of quantities from all firms.
  5. Firms choose quantities simultaneously.
Frame Cournot vs Bertrand by the strategic variable. Cournot = quantity competition: fits commodity/capacity-constrained producers (oil, bottling, garbage) where you build capacity first and can't instantly poach every customer with a price cut. The 5 assumptions are the homogeneous-goods Cournot setup; we relax “identical products” in §13.5 (differentiated Bertrand). Next: a concrete plant-size example, then the model.

Setting Up the Cournot Model

Antoine Augustin Cournot

1801–1877

Assume two firms in a Cournot oligopoly:

Each firm has a constant marginal cost \(c\).

Firms 1 and 2 simultaneously choose quantities \(q_1\) and \(q_2\).

Their joint output \(Q = q_1 + q_2\) sets the one market price all firms face.

Linear demand: \(\;P = a - bQ\;\)  (intercept \(a\), slope \(-b\)). For our boba market: \(a = 19.99,\; b = 0.03,\; c = \$1\).

The canonical Cournot setup, with Cournot himself (1838, the first formal oligopoly model). Two symmetric firms, constant \(MC = c\), simultaneous quantity choice, one market price from joint output on linear demand \(P=a-bQ\). Tie to the running example: the boba market is exactly this with \(a=19.99,\,b=0.03,\,c=\$1\). Next we write each firm's profit, then derive its best response.

Each Firm's Profit Depends on the Other

Inverse demand on joint output: \(\;P = a - bQ = a - b(q_1 + q_2)\).

Firm 1

\(\pi_1 = q_1\,(P - c) = \underbrace{q_1 P}_{TR} - \underbrace{q_1 c}_{TC}\)

Substitute \(P\):   \(\pi_1 = q_1\big(a - b(q_1 + q_2) - c\big)\)

Firm 2

\(\pi_2 = q_2\,(P - c)\)

Substitute \(P\):   \(\pi_2 = q_2\big(a - b(q_1 + q_2) - c\big)\)

The whole game in one line: each \(\pi_i\) contains the other firm's quantity. Firm 1's best output depends on \(q_2\); firm 2's depends on \(q_1\) — strategic interdependence.

Three reveals. Profit is just \(q_i(P-c)\) = TR − TC, then substitute the demand for \(P\). The punchline (the screenshot's arrows): each firm's profit equation contains the OTHER firm's quantity — you cannot maximize \(\pi_1\) without a conjecture about \(q_2\). That is what makes it a game, and it forces the best-response idea on the next slides.

Residual Demand: Firm 1's Slice

Firm 1 can't control \(q_2\). So it treats firm 2's output as fixed and asks: what demand is left for me? Re-group the demand around \(q_1\):

\[ P = a - b(q_1 + q_2) = \underbrace{(a - b\,q_2)}_{\text{intercept}} \;-\; \underbrace{b}_{\text{slope}}\;q_1 \]

Intercept \(a - b\,q_2\): shifts down the more firm 2 produces.

Slope \(-b\): the same steepness as market demand.

Firm 1 now faces an ordinary downward-sloping residual demand — so it behaves like a monopolist on its own slice: set \(MR = MC\) to find its best response. (that derivation is next →)

The key conceptual move that makes Cournot tractable: once firm 1 takes \(q_2\) as given, the market demand becomes firm 1's RESIDUAL demand — same slope \(-b\), but an intercept \((a - bq_2)\) that firm 2 pushes down. Firm 1 is then just a monopolist on that residual demand: set \(MR = MC\). The next slide (perceived \(MR\)) and the Calculus Corner do exactly that and produce the best-response function.

Cournot: Each Firm's MR at Its Share of the Market

The puzzle. Streaming firms face implied \(|\varepsilon^{M}| = 3.33\). Symmetric four-firm Cournot would predict \(L \approx 1/(4 \cdot 3.33) = 0.075\). The actual streaming Lerner is \(0.300\). What's wrong? — homogeneous Cournot is the wrong model for streaming. Hold that thought through §13.5.

Setup. \(N\) firms simultaneously choose \(q_i\); total \(Q = \sum q_i\); inverse demand \(P(Q)\). Firm \(i\) maximizes \(\pi_i = P(Q)q_i - C_i(q_i)\), taking rivals' outputs as fixed.

Differentiating (\(\partial Q/\partial q_i = 1\)):

\(\dfrac{\partial \pi_i}{\partial q_i} = P(Q) + q_i\,P'(Q) - C_i'(q_i) = 0\)

Rearranged: \(\underbrace{P(Q) + q_i P'(Q)}_{\text{firm } i\text{'s perceived } MR} = MC_i\). This is the Ch 11 monopolist's \(MR = MC\) — but applied at firm \(i\)'s share-weighted marginal revenue. A Cournot firm is a monopolist over its slice of the residual demand.

The single most important idea in §13.3: Cournot's FOC is just Ch 11's \(MR=MC\) with the price-impact term \(q_i P'(Q)\) scaled by the firm's own output, not total output. Bigger firms internalize more of the price drop. As \(N\) grows and each \(q_i\) shrinks, the price-impact term vanishes and the firm becomes a price-taker — previewing the PC limit. The puzzle box motivates §13.5: linear-demand and constant-elasticity Cournot bracket the streaming Lerner but neither hits \(0.300\); differentiation does.

Calculus Corner 13.1 — Cournot Best Responses

Linear demand \(P = a - bQ\), constant \(MC_i = c\). The FOC \(a - b\big(\sum_j q_j\big) - bq_i = c\) gives firm \(i\)'s best response to rivals' total \(Q_{-i}\):

\(\mathrm{BR}_i(Q_{-i}) = \dfrac{a - c - b\,Q_{-i}}{2b}\)

Symmetric equilibrium (\(q_i = q^{\ast}\), \(Q_{-i} = (N-1)q^{\ast}\)):

\(q^{\ast} = \dfrac{a-c}{(N+1)b}, \quad P^{\ast} = \dfrac{a + Nc}{N+1}\)

SOC: \(2P' + q_i P'' < 0\); for linear demand \(= -2b < 0\) ✓. Firm output falls in \(N\), total output rises in \(N\), price falls in \(N\).

Best responses cross at the Cournot-Nash equilibrium — the fixed point where neither firm wants to deviate.

The derivation students must own. Each \(\mathrm{BR}_i\) is downward-sloping (quantities are strategic substitutes — if rivals make more, you make less). The two reaction curves cross once, at the symmetric Nash equilibrium. The closed forms \(q^{\ast}=(a-c)/((N+1)b)\) and \(P^{\ast}=(a+Nc)/(N+1)\) are the workhorses for W13.2/W13.3 and the interactive. SOC is automatic for linear demand. The downward slope (strategic substitutes) is what makes the first-mover advantage positive in Stackelberg later (§13.6).

§13.4

Bertrand Competition & the Paradox

If firms set prices instead of quantities, how few firms does it take to reach \(P = MC\)?

Bertrand Competition

  • Bertrand competition: two (or more) firms compete on price to sell the same good.
  • Firms set their prices simultaneously.
  • Consumers are indifferent between the brands and always buy from the seller with the lowest price.

That last assumption is the engine: undercut your rival by a single penny and you capture the entire market — so each firm always has a reason to cut price.

JOSEPH BERTRAND

1822–1890

The intuitive opener for §13.4 before the formal paradox. Three assumptions define the model: price is the strategic variable, prices are chosen simultaneously, and the goods are perfect substitutes so consumers buy purely on price. Emphasize the third — perfect substitutability means a one-penny price cut wins the whole market, which is the entire engine of the result. Joseph Bertrand (1822–1890) raised this as an 1883 critique of Cournot: real firms post prices, not quantities. Heads Up 13.3 — The Paradox Needs All Three Assumptions

The empirical fact that few-firm markets show large markups doesn't refute Bertrand — it refutes its three assumptions in pure form. Each fails somewhere:

Homogeneous products? — Spotify, Apple, Amazon, YouTube Music aren't perfect substitutes (algorithms, exclusives, bundles, integrations). Finite substitutability \(\gamma\) gives \(P > MC\). → §13.5

Unlimited capacity? — When capacity binds, undercutting stops paying: the rival's capacity caps your residual demand (Kreps-Scheinkman 1983). The two-stage game gives the Cournot outcome. → §13.7

One shot? — In an infinitely repeated game with a patient enough discount factor, grim-trigger strategies sustain \(P > MC\) (Friedman 1971; Stigler 1964). → Ch 14

The Bertrand paradox is best read as a diagnostic: it tells you which feature of a real market generates its markup. Each of the next three units relaxes exactly one assumption and recovers a finite markup.

The organizing slide for the rest of the chapter. Each of the three assumptions maps to a section: differentiation (§13.5), capacity (§13.7 Kreps-Scheinkman), repetition (Ch 14). For streaming, it's differentiation; for electricity/oil, it's capacity; for stable cartels, it's repetition. Calculus Corner 13.3 — Differentiated-Bertrand FOC

Linear differentiated demand: \(Q_i = \alpha - \beta P_i + \gamma P_j\), with \(0 \leq \gamma < \beta\). The cross-price coefficient \(\gamma\) is the substitutability dial.

Firm \(i\) maximizes \(\pi_i = (P_i - c)(\alpha - \beta P_i + \gamma P_j)\). FOC:

\(\alpha - \beta P_i + \gamma P_j - \beta(P_i - c) = 0\)

Best response \(P_i = (\alpha + \beta c + \gamma P_j)/(2\beta)\). Set \(P_i = P_j = P^{\ast}\):

\(P^{\ast} = \dfrac{\alpha + \beta c}{2\beta - \gamma}\)

\(\gamma \to \beta\) (near-perfect substitutes): \(P^{\ast} \to c\), the Bertrand floor. \(\gamma \to 0\) (independent): \(P^{\ast} \to (\alpha+\beta c)/2\beta\), each firm's monopoly price.

Prices are strategic complements — best responses slope up, equilibrium above \(MC\).

The escape from the paradox. Differentiation gives each firm a downward-sloping residual demand at any positive markup — a price cut moves only \(\beta - \gamma\) of volume per dollar, not the whole market. Key structural contrast with Cournot: here best responses slope UP (prices are strategic complements), vs Cournot's downward-sloping reaction curves (quantities are strategic substitutes). That sign difference is why Stackelberg price-leadership can backfire (§13.6 appendix / Ch 14). The dial is \(\gamma/\beta\): 0 = independent monopolies, 1 = homogeneous Bertrand floor; real markets are interior.

§13.6

Stackelberg Leader–Follower

Same payoffs as Cournot — only the timing changes. One firm commits first.

Stackelberg Competition

  • Stackelberg competition: Cournot-style competition — two (or more) firms compete on quantity to sell the same good.
  • Again, the firms' joint output sets the market price faced by all.
  • But they choose quantities sequentially: the Leader produces first, the Follower produces second.

Heinrich von Stackelberg

1905–1946

Formalized leader–follower (sequential) oligopoly, 1934.

Opener for §13.6. Stackelberg keeps the Cournot payoffs (quantity competition, identical good, joint output sets price) and changes only the timing: one firm commits first, the other observes and responds. Heinrich von Stackelberg (1905–1946) introduced it in 1934. The whole section turns on one fact carried over from §13.3: quantity reaction curves slope DOWN, which is what makes moving first valuable.

Sequential, Not Simultaneous

So far, firms with market power chose output or price simultaneously. In reality a firm may move before or after observing a rival's choice — that's Stackelberg competition: an oligopoly where firms produce sequentially.

Model assumptions — Stackelberg with identical goods

  1. The market has only a small number of firms.
  2. Firms make identical products.
  3. Firms compete by choosing a quantity to produce.
  4. All goods sell at the market price, set by the sum of quantities produced by all firms.
  5. Firms do not choose simultaneously: one firm chooses quantity first; the next firm observes it and then chooses its own.
The five assumptions are exactly Cournot's (small number, identical goods, quantity competition, price from total output) plus the one twist (#5): moves are sequential, not simultaneous. Everything else — the demand curve, the marginal cost, the best-response logic — is identical to §13.3. That's the point: hold the model fixed, change only the timing, and see what the first mover can do.

Why Moving First Pays

Same Cournot payoffs — but now one firm moves first. Here's the chain that makes that an advantage:

Reaction curves slope down

When a rival produces more, your best response is to produce less.

→

The leader commits big, first

A large, sunk, credible quantity the follower can't undo.

→

The follower retreats

It slides down its curve — the leader keeps the gap it created.

That's the first-mover advantage — it works because quantities are strategic substitutes. (For Bertrand prices — strategic complements — committing first can backfire.)

The conceptual heart of §13.6, as a three-step chain (revealed left to right). Because quantities are strategic substitutes (downward-sloping reaction), a credible commitment to flood the market makes the rival optimally retreat — so the leader grabs share the simultaneous Cournot game won't give it. Contrast Bertrand prices (strategic complements, upward-sloping): committing to a low price invites matching, so first-moving can hurt. The sign of the reaction-curve slope decides whether leadership helps. Sets up the boba solve next: the leader produces the monopoly quantity, the follower half the Cournot.

Calculus Corner 13.2 — Stackelberg Backward Induction

Leader commits \(q_1\); follower observes and picks \(q_2\). Same \(P = a - bQ\), \(MC = c\). Solve backward.

Step 1 — follower (same as Cournot BR): \(q_2(q_1) = (a - c - bq_1)/(2b)\).

Step 2 — leader substitutes the reaction function: \(\pi_1(q_1) = \big(\tfrac{a-c}{2} - \tfrac{bq_1}{2}\big) q_1\). FOC \(\Rightarrow\)

\(q_1^{\ast} = \dfrac{a-c}{2b}, \quad q_2^{\ast} = \dfrac{a-c}{4b}, \quad \pi_1^{\ast} = 2\,\pi_2^{\ast}\)

Total \(Q^{S} = \tfrac{3(a-c)}{4b} > Q^{C}\), so \(P^{S} < P^{C}\). The leader's commitment makes the follower scale back — the first-mover advantage.

Leader rides the follower's reaction curve to its highest iso-profit contour.

Backward induction is the method — solve the follower first, then the leader anticipating that response. The leader produces MORE than its Cournot self because it knows the follower will retreat along the reaction curve; the follower produces LESS. Total output rises, price falls, and the leader captures most of the residual surplus (\(\pi_1 = 2\pi_2\)). Crucial caveat for Ch 14: the first-mover advantage is positive ONLY because quantities are strategic substitutes (downward-sloping reaction). With strategic complements (Bertrand prices), committing first can backfire (Bulow-Geanakoplos-Klemperer).

Contestable Markets — When the Threat Is Enough

Baumol, 1982

Even a single firm prices competitively if entry is credible. With low sunk costs, a “hit-and-run” entrant disciplines the incumbent. A small-town gas station may price like a competitor.

When it breaks down

Contestability assumes low sunk costs. A frontier AI run (~$1B), hyperscale data centers, gigawatts of power — none recoverable if you fail. The desert highway’s lone station can charge monopoly prices.

The real question isn’t how concentrated a market is — it’s whether a credible challenger could actually enter. When the moat costs billions to cross, pure contestability is an illusion.

Contestable-markets theory is the counterweight to “concentration = market power.” Microsoft used it in 1998 (Schmalensee) — and it proved prophetic (Chrome displaced IE). But the 2020s digital economy is precisely the high-sunk-cost case where it fails. The number of sellers is an OUTCOME, not the cause — what matters is the cost of entry. (Ties to §13.7: entry conditions, not just the count of firms, set the markup.)

In 1998 the DOJ called Microsoft an illegal monopoly — bundling Internet Explorer with Windows to crush Netscape. Microsoft’s defense: the market is contestable — new technology could displace us at any time. Who was right?

The Microsoft case is the most famous contestability argument. The DOJ saw a 90%+ OS monopoly leveraging into browsers; Microsoft (via economist Richard Schmalensee) argued the software market was so dynamic that today's dominance is fragile — entry by unknown future tech keeps it disciplined. Low sunk costs in software (no factories, instant distribution) make browsers genuinely contestable. Contrast with the high-sunk-cost digital platforms where the same argument fails.

Contestable Today? — Cloud Computing

Just like frontier AI today, the cloud once had massive sunk costs (data centers, fiber). But as the industry matured, Microsoft and Google entered — Amazon’s lead narrowed (53.7% → 47.8%). High sunk costs can fall over time, turning a near-monopoly into a contestable market.

The forward-looking case. AWS looked unassailable (first mover, vast data centers). But cloud sunk costs, while huge, are increasingly shared/standardized, and deep-pocketed rivals (Azure, Google Cloud) could enter — so Amazon's share is eroding and it must price/innovate to defend it. The lesson for AI: today's ~$1B training runs look like an impassable moat, but if those costs fall (better chips, efficiency), AI could follow the cloud's path from sunk-cost fortress to contestable market.

Measuring Concentration: The HHI — and Its Limits

The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index sums squared market shares: \(\text{HHI} = \sum_i s_i^2\) (shares in %, range 0–10,000). \(N\) equal firms give \(\text{HHI} = 10{,}000/N\); a monopoly is \(10{,}000\).

It's the antitrust agencies' first-pass screen for merger review — higher HHI flags more concentration.

But concentration is not conduct. §13.5 showed two differentiated firms can hold \(L = 0.300\) while five homogeneous Cournot firms hold less — and Bertrand reaches \(L = 0\) with just two. HHI is a proxy, not a verdict. The strategic structure (this chapter) is what actually sets the markup.

HHI across industries — a screening tool, not a measure of market power itself.

HHI is the practical concentration measure students will meet in antitrust and IO. Give the clean facts: sum of squared shares, \(10{,}000/N\) for \(N\) equal firms, \(10{,}000\) for monopoly. The pedagogical hinge — and the chapter's closing caution — is the misconception box: concentration ≠ conduct. The whole chapter is a demonstration that the same number of firms can yield wildly different markups depending on the strategic variable, timing, and differentiation. HHI screens; the models explain. This sets up Ch 14, where game theory formalizes the conduct side completely.

Chapter 13 — The Six Results

Chamberlin LR tangency (§13.2). Free entry of differentiated firms → perceived demand tangent to ATC: \(P > MC\) and excess capacity, together.

Cournot \(N\)-firm (§13.3). \(q^{\ast} = \tfrac{a-c}{(N+1)b}\), \(P^{\ast} = \tfrac{a+Nc}{N+1}\), \(L^{\ast} = \tfrac{4}{5+N}\); \(L \to 0\) as \(N \to \infty\).

Bertrand paradox (§13.4). Homogeneous + unlimited capacity + one shot → \(P = MC\) with just two firms.

Differentiated Bertrand (§13.5). \(P^{\ast} = \tfrac{\alpha + \beta c}{2\beta - \gamma}\); finite markup for any \(\gamma < \beta\). Explains the streaming cluster.

Stackelberg (§13.6). Leader \(\tfrac{a-c}{2b}\), follower \(\tfrac{a-c}{4b}\); \(\pi_1 = 2\pi_2\). Output up, price down vs Cournot.

Kreps-Scheinkman (§13.7). Capacity-then-price replicates Cournot — the paradox is a teaching case; real capacity-bound markets play Cournot.

One spine. Welfare ranks \(TS^{PC} > TS^{Bdiff} > TS^{S} > TS^{C} > TS^{M}\); Lerner ranks the reverse. Every model is a point between the competitive floor and the cartel ceiling, selected by strategic structure.

The six results, one per major section, plus the unifying welfare spine. If students remember one thing: structure (number of firms) is not destiny — conduct (quantities vs prices, sequential vs simultaneous, differentiated vs homogeneous) sets the markup. Cournot \(N=1\) is monopoly; Cournot \(N\to\infty\) and Bertrand homogeneous are both PC; the interesting models live in between. The streaming \(L=0.300\) is the running example's resolution: Cournot-like competition softened by differentiation.
