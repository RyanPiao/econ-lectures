Part V in Formulas

The calculus behind Chapters 13–14 — strategic interaction, from best responses to the discount factor

Dr. Richeng Piao • Northeastern University

First: one idea replaces the single-firm FOC

When a few firms interact, each one’s best choice depends on the others’. Four ideas carry Chapters 13–14.

\( q_i^{*}(q_{-i}) \)

A best response. Your optimal move given what rivals do. It’s just Chapter 11’s \(MR = MC\), now with rivals’ outputs sitting inside your demand.

\( (s_1^{*}, s_2^{*}) \ \text{Nash} \)

A Nash equilibrium. A profile where every player is best-responding at once — nobody can gain by moving alone. The fixed point where best responses meet.

\( P \gt MC \ \text{persists} \)

The markup survives. With few firms or differentiated products, the wedge from Part IV doesn’t vanish — it lands between monopoly and perfect competition.

\( \delta \in [0,1) \)

The discount factor. How much a player weights the future. It decides whether repeated interaction can sustain cooperation the one-shot game can’t.

Chapter 13 solves the FOCs of four market models; Chapter 14 asks what an “equilibrium” even is — and when cooperation holds.

Oligopoly model 1 of 4

Monopolistic Competition = Free Entry Meets a Downward Demand

Many differentiated firms, entry until zero profit — but a markup that never dies.

The Chamberlin long-run tangency

Monopolistic competition

Ch 13 • §13.2
\[ P^{d}(q^{*}) = ATC(q^{*}) \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{dP^{d}}{dq} = \frac{dATC}{dq} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; P \gt MC \ \text{with excess capacity} \]

In plain English

Free entry competes profit to zero, so perceived demand ends up tangent to average cost — equal value and equal slope. But because that demand slopes down (each firm has a sliver of monopoly), the tangency lands where ATC is still falling. Two fingerprints follow: the markup \(P \gt MC\) survives, and the firm runs below efficient scale — excess capacity.

The matched-slope condition is what separates Chamberlin from perfect competition, where tangency happens at \(P = MC = \min ATC\).

Oligopoly models 2 & 3 of 4

Cournot & Bertrand = Two Ways a Few Firms Compete

Same firms, same demand — but competing in quantities vs. prices gives opposite answers.

Cournot — competing in quantities

Cournot competition

Ch 13 • §13.3
\[ P(Q) + q_i\,P'(Q) = MC_i \quad\xrightarrow{\text{symmetric, linear}}\quad q^{*} = \frac{a-c}{(N+1)b}, \;\; P^{*} = \frac{a + Nc}{N+1} \]

In plain English

Each firm chooses output, taking rivals’ quantities as given. The FOC is the monopolist’s \(MR = MC\) — but the price-cutting term \(q_i\,P'(Q)\) only weighs the firm’s own share, not the whole market. Solving the mutual best responses gives per-firm output that shrinks as \(N\) grows.

As \(N \to \infty\), \(P^{*} \to c = MC\): Cournot converges to perfect competition. At \(N = 1\) it’s exactly monopoly. The whole spectrum, one formula.

Bertrand — competing in prices, and the paradox

Bertrand competition

Ch 13 • §13.4
\[ P_1 = P_2 = MC, \qquad \pi_1 = \pi_2 = 0 \quad (\text{homogeneous goods}) \]

In plain English

Now firms post prices and buyers flock to the cheapest. Any price above MC invites a rival to undercut by a penny and steal the whole market — so undercutting runs all the way down to marginal cost. Two firms reproduce the competitive outcome. That’s the Bertrand paradox: “few firms” need not mean “high markups.”

Real duopolies don’t price at MC because one assumption fails: products differ (§13.5), capacity is finite (§13.7), or the game repeats (Ch 14). Each failure restores a markup.

Oligopoly model 4 of 4

Stackelberg = Moving First Is Worth Something

Same Cournot payoffs — but sequential timing hands the leader an advantage.

The leader solves the follower’s reaction first

Stackelberg • backward induction

Ch 13 • §13.6
\[ q_1^{*} = \frac{a-c}{2b}, \qquad q_2^{*} = \frac{a-c}{4b}, \qquad \pi_1^{*} = 2\,\pi_2^{*} \]

In plain English

The leader moves first, knowing the follower will best-respond. So it substitutes the follower’s reaction function into its own problem — backward induction — and commits to a larger quantity, crowding the follower out. Total output exceeds Cournot, price falls, and the first mover earns twice the follower’s profit.

Commitment is the whole story: the leader can’t take its quantity back, and that irreversibility is exactly what makes it powerful.

Now — what is an equilibrium?

Game Theory = The Machinery Underneath

Best responses, dominance, and the Nash fixed point that names it all.

Nash equilibrium — mutual best response

Nash equilibrium

Ch 14 • §14.2
\[ u_i\!\left(s_i^{*}, s_{-i}^{*}\right) \ \ge\ u_i\!\left(s_i, s_{-i}^{*}\right) \quad \text{for every } s_i \in S_i,\ \text{every } i \]

In plain English

A strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium when no player can do better by deviating alone, holding everyone else fixed. That’s the exact condition Cournot’s solution satisfied — game theory just names the fixed point. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, (Defect, Defect) is Nash even though both would prefer (Cooperate, Cooperate): Nash is about individual, not collective, rationality.

A strictly dominated strategy is never a best response — so iterated elimination of dominated strategies is a legitimate way to hunt equilibria.

Two refinements: mixing, and credible threats

Mixed strategies & SPNE

Ch 14 • §14.3–14.4

Mixed-strategy Nash

\[ \text{indifference: } u_i(\text{H}) = u_i(\text{T}) \]

Subgame perfection

\[ \text{Nash in \textit{every} subgame} \]

In plain English

Some games (matching pennies) have no pure equilibrium — so players randomize, choosing probabilities that leave the rival exactly indifferent. And in sequential games, backward induction keeps only subgame-perfect equilibria — the ones that don’t rely on threats a player wouldn’t actually carry out. Stackelberg’s solution is the SPNE of the quantity game.

The payoff — when can rivals cooperate?

Repeated Games = The Future Disciplines the Present

One number, the discount factor, decides whether a cartel holds itself together.

The grim-trigger threshold

Repeated games

Ch 14 • §14.5
\[ \frac{R}{1-\delta} \ \ge\ T + \frac{\delta P}{1-\delta} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \delta \ \ge\ \delta^{*} = \frac{T-R}{T-P} \]

In plain English

In a one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma, defection wins. Repeat it forever with “cooperate until someone cheats, then punish forever,” and cooperation becomes an equilibrium if players are patient enough. Compare the one-time temptation gain \(T-R\) to the discounted punishment loss: cooperation holds when \(\delta \ge (T-R)/(T-P)\).

With canonical payoffs \((T,R,P,S)=(5,3,1,0)\), \(\delta^{*} = 2/4 = 0.5\). Weight the future at least half as much as today and the cartel can sustain itself — no contract required.

The markup lands on a spectrum — and the future sets the rest.

Monopolistic competition

Free entry to zero profit: demand tangent to ATC, \(P \gt MC\), excess capacity.

Cournot & Bertrand

Quantities give \(P^{*} = (a+Nc)/(N+1)\), between monopoly and PC; prices give the \(P = MC\) paradox.

Stackelberg

Backward induction on the follower’s reaction: leader commits, \(\pi_1 = 2\pi_2\) — the first-mover advantage.

Nash & repeated games

Equilibrium = mutual best response; cooperation sustained iff \(\delta \ge (T-R)/(T-P)\).

Every oligopoly outcome is a Nash equilibrium of some game — the market structure just fixes the strategies and payoffs. And whether rivals escape the Prisoner’s Dilemma toward the cartel comes down to a single number: how much they value tomorrow.
