How do we turn satisfaction into a number?

The old hope (1890s)

Utility was thought to be a real, measurable, cardinal scale — one that says how much better a bundle is, not just which one wins.

The modern attempt — neuroeconomics

Grounds choice in first principles — calories, water, warmth — and uses fMRI and PET scans to watch the brain deciding.

Measuring “satisfaction” directly — a century-old dream.

Two problems a cardinal scale can't solve.

• Comparing two different consumers' preferences may make no sense at all.
• There is no real-world measuring stick for how much more a consumer likes \(A\) than \(B\).

Ordinal = comparing rankings.   Cardinal = comparing differences.   Consumer theory needs only the first.

Four steps. (1) The 1890s marginalists - Jevons, Menger, Walras - genuinely hoped utility was measurable. (2) Neuroeconomics is the modern echo: fMRI and PET to ground choices in biology. (3) Then the two objections that killed the cardinal programme: no interpersonal comparison, and no yardstick for "how much more." (4) The retreat that saves the theory - we only ever need the ranking. That sets up revealed preference on the next slide.

Individual Objectives & Preferences

Economists assume consumers are rational — able to optimize their consumption given scarce resources.

“De gustibus non est disputandum” — there is no disputing about taste. Economics doesn't judge what you want; it takes preferences as given.

To predict choices we need a consumer's preferences: between any two products \(a\) and \(b\), which is preferred — and how does that ranking behave?

We can't read minds — but we can watch actions. “Actions speak louder than words.”

Principle of Revealed Preference. If a person chooses \(a\) over \(b\) when both are affordable, they must prefer \(a \succeq b\).

Four steps. (1) Rationality means the consumer optimizes; we make "optimize" literal with the Lagrangian in Ch 5. (3) But we cannot read minds, so we infer the ranking from choices - revealed preference, the workhorse of empirical demand analysis: we never observe utility, we observe purchases. (4) The streaming panel makes it concrete - each subscriber's pick is a revealed ranking, and aggregating them gives market share. Preferences — comparing consumption outcomes

An ordinal ranking lets us order bundles from best to worst. For any two bundles \(a\) and \(b\), we allow exactly three answers:

\(a \succ b\)  —  strictly prefer \(a\) over \(b\)

\(a \prec b\)  —  strictly prefer \(b\) over \(a\)

\(a \sim b\)  —  indifferent between \(a\) and \(b\)

A consumer's preferences are just the list of all such comparisons. The "rules" of utility allow only an ordinal ranking — never a cardinal "how much."

Utility — A Ranking System

Utility is an abstract measure of the satisfaction a consumer gets from goods and services. The numbers are a bookkeeping device; the ranking is the economics.

Total utility

The total satisfaction from a whole bundle. This is what \(U(x_1, x_2)\) reports.

Marginal utility

The change in total utility from one more unit of one good. We compute it in §4.2 — and it is the ingredient the MRS is built from.

Misconception — “utility is measured in exact units.”

Hicks (1934): we only ever need an ordinal ranking. Total utils are an analytical fiction; the relative ranking is what's real.

\(U(A) = 100\) and \(U(B) = 50\) says only that \(A \succ B\).

Not that \(A\) is “twice as good,” nor worth two \(B\)s, nor that the consumer is twice as happy.

Four steps. (1) Utility is an abstract measure of satisfaction - the numbers are bookkeeping, the ranking is the economics. (2) Two flavours worth naming now: total utility, which is what U reports for a whole bundle, and marginal utility, the change from one more unit, which we compute shortly and which the MRS is built from. (3) The misconception to kill: utility is not measured in exact units. Hicks 1934 is the citation - modern theory needs only that consumers rank consistently. (4) Make the last line concrete and say it slowly: 100 and 50 tell you A beats B and nothing else. Students reliably want to say "twice as good," and every ordinal argument in the rest of the chapter depends on them not saying it.

Marginal Utility

Marginal utility is the extra utility from one more unit of a good — the derivative of \(U\) with respect to that good, holding the other fixed.

For our coffee-and-boba consumer with \(U = x^{0.8} y^{0.2}\):

Coffee

\(MU_x = \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial x} = 0.8\, x^{\,0.8-1}\, y^{0.2}\)

\(= 0.8\, x^{-0.2}\, y^{0.2}\)

Boba

\(MU_y = \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial y} = 0.2\, x^{0.8}\, y^{\,0.2-1}\)

\(= 0.2\, x^{0.8}\, y^{-0.8}\)

Power rule: bring the exponent down, subtract one. Each \(MU\) is decreasing in its own good — that negative exponent is diminishing marginal utility.

\(MU\) is a partial derivative — differentiate in \(x\), hold \(y\) fixed. Math Refresher →

MU is the extra utility from one more unit; as the unit shrinks toward zero the discrete change becomes the derivative - the deck's recurring "promote the difference quotient to a derivative" move. Note the notation switch to a partial: U depends on two goods, so MU_x differentiates with respect to x while treating y as a constant. That is exactly the ch2-partial refresher card, linked here for anyone rusty. Then the computation: the power rule brings 0.8 down and the exponent becomes 0.8 - 1 = -0.2, so MU_x = 0.8 x^(-0.2) y^(0.2); symmetrically MU_y = 0.2 x^(0.8) y^(-0.8). The negative own-exponent is diminishing marginal utility. These two MUs feed straight into MRS = MU_x / MU_y in section 4.3.

The four axioms

Four assumptions about how a consumer ranks. If they hold, the representation theorem (Debreu, 1954) guarantees a function \(U(x_1, x_2)\) exists with \(U(A) > U(B) \iff A \succ B\) — because the ranking behaves nicely, not because anyone measured happiness.

Notation

\(\succsim\) at least as good as
\(\succ\) strictly preferred to
\(\sim\) indifferent to

1. Completeness

For any \(A, B\): \(A \succsim B\), or \(B \succsim A\), or both. The consumer can always rank.

2. Transitivity

\(A \succsim B\) and \(B \succsim C\) \(\Rightarrow A \succsim C\). Internal consistency.

3. Monotonicity

More of every good (and strictly more of one) \(\Rightarrow\) strictly preferred. More is better.

4. Convexity

For \(A \succsim B\) and \(\lambda \in [0,1]\): \(\lambda A + (1-\lambda)B \succsim B\). Mixes are at least as good as extremes.

Each axiom does specific geometric work. We pick them apart one at a time.

The axioms are assumptions about the ranking, not about psychology - that is the sentence to land. The lineage worth naming out loud: Pareto in the 1900s, Hicks and Allen in the 1930s, Debreu in the 1950s; the throughline is that utility is an indexing tool, not a measurement of pleasure. Each of the four then does specific geometric work on the indifference map, which is how we take them apart over the next four slides.

Completeness — sounds innocuous, isn't

Faced with any two bundles, the consumer has an opinion — even if it's "indifferent."

What completeness rules out: “I have no idea.” Real users, faced with thousands of unfamiliar shows, often genuinely don't.

Platforms act as if preferences are complete — the algorithm has to rank one item above another somewhere on the page.

Without completeness, indifference "curves" can have holes. Calculus on a curve with holes is hard.

Transitivity — and the money pump

Transitivity. If \(A \succsim B\) and \(B \succsim C\), then \(A \succsim C\).

Sushi \(\succ\) Pasta and Pasta \(\succ\) Pizza \(\Rightarrow\) Sushi \(\succ\) Pizza. A ranking cannot loop back on itself.

Now break it. Suppose instead:

Pizza \(\succ\) Sushi  ·  Pasta \(\succ\) Pizza  ·  Sushi \(\succ\) Pasta

Every dish loses to something. Nothing is best — the ranking is a loop.

Three swaps and you hold sushi again — \$3 poorer. Nothing changed but your wallet, and the loop runs forever. That is the money pump, and transitivity is the axiom that rules it out.

Each arrow is a swap you would happily pay for — it hands you the dish you prefer. Follow them and you come back to where you started.

Monotonicity — “more is better” with caveats

Bundle \(A\) has at least as much of every good as \(B\), and strictly more of one \(\Rightarrow A \succ B\). Equivalently \(MU_1 = \partial U/\partial x_1 > 0\) and \(MU_2 = \partial U/\partial x_2 > 0\) wherever we work.

Geometric work: it forces indifference curves to slope downward. An upward-sloping curve would hold utility constant while handing you more of both goods — contradiction. We rely on this in §4.3 to sign the MRS.

What it sweeps under the rug: bads (pollution, second-hand smoke), bliss points (the 43rd taco), satiation. We work in the range where more is still better — and flipping a “bad” into a good restores the framework (§4.5).

Price is $0. If more were always better the table would clear in minutes. It doesn't — past the first copy, another intro-micro text adds zero marginal utility.

Monotonicity does one job on the picture: it forces indifference curves downward-sloping, which is what lets us sign the MRS in section 4.3. The free-books photo is the honest counterweight and the hook students remember. A department "FREE" table of economics books: price is zero, yet the pile sits. If preferences were globally monotone, a zero price would clear it instantly. It does not, because beyond the first copy the marginal utility of another identical textbook is about zero - negative once you count shelf space and hauling it home. That is satiation, and past the bliss point the good becomes a bad. Monotonicity assumes this away by restricting attention to the region where more is still better.

Convexity — averages weakly preferred to extremes

\((10, 0) \sim (0, 10)\) \(\Rightarrow\) the average \((5, 5) \succsim (10, 0)\).

Most consumers prefer variety to corners. Convexity captures it.

Geometric work: indifference curves bow toward the origin. Upper contour set is convex.

Where it fails: lock-in markets. Smartphone ecosystems — an all-Apple bundle and an all-Android bundle each deliver integration; the 50/50 mix sacrifices integration on both sides. U.S. v. Apple (2024) catalogs five lock-in mechanisms that produce exactly this shape.

Foreshadowing: the lock-in geometry returns in §4.5.5 with \(U = x_1^2 + x_2^2\) as the cleanest closed-form non-convex example.

§4.3  Indifference Curves & MRS

The ranking machine’s geometry and its slope

From a Utility Function to an Indifference Map

\(U = x_1 + x_2\)  —  \(x_1\) = strawberries 🍓,  \(x_2\) = blueberries 🫐

Step 1 — three bundles that each give \(U = 7\)

Step 2 — connect them \(\rightarrow\) one curve, \(\bar U = 7\)

Step 3 — one curve per level \(\rightarrow\) the map

An indifference curve is the set of all bundles giving the same utility: \((2,5)\), \((5,2)\), \((6,1)\) all yield \(U = 7\).

An indifference map stacks them — one curve per utility level. Farther from the origin = higher utility = preferred.

This utility is additive, so every curve is a straight line of slope \(-1\) — the perfect-substitutes case we formalize in §4.5. Cobb-Douglas curves bow.

The whole construction in one slide. Step 1: the utility function assigns every bundle a number; fix that number at 7 and plot the bundles that hit it - (2,5), (5,2), (6,1) all sum to 7. Step 2: they are collinear, so "connecting them" is literally the line x1 + x2 = 7 - that line IS the indifference curve. Step 3: do it again at 3, 5 and 9 and you have the map; the green arrow is the direction of higher utility, and curves never cross. Why Indifference Curves Cannot Cross

1. \(D = (4, 2)\) and \(F = (2, 4)\) lie on the same curve \(U_1\) ⇒ Joe is indifferent: \(D \sim F\).

2. Suppose a second curve \(U_2\) crosses \(U_1\) at \(F\) and also passes through \(E = (5, 2.5)\) ⇒ \(F \sim E\).

3. Transitivity: \(D \sim F\) and \(F \sim E\) \(\Rightarrow D \sim E\).

4. But \(E = (5, 2.5)\) has more of both than \(D = (4, 2)\) — one more basket of fries and half a burger ⇒ more is better ⇒ \(E \succ D\).

Contradiction. \(D \sim E\) and \(E \succ D\) cannot both hold — so the curves cannot cross.

The (impossible) case: \(D\) on \(U_1\), \(E\) on \(U_2\), both “tied” with \(F\). But \(E\) has more fries and more burgers than \(D\).

Proof by contradiction — the transitivity characteristic, made rigorous. Walk it slowly: D~F (same curve), assume a crossing curve gives F~E, transitivity forces D~E, but the picture shows E is up-and-to-the-right of D (more fries AND more burgers), so monotonicity forces E≻D. D~E and E≻D is a contradiction. The only way out: curves never cross. This is exactly why "no crossing" is on the characteristics list — it is transitivity + monotonicity working together.

The Marginal Rate of Substitution

Indifference curves describe tradeoffs. How much of one good will you give up for one more unit of another and stay equally well off? The slope answers it.

\[\mathrm{MRS}_{XY} = -\frac{dY}{dX}\]

— the slope of the indifference curve at a point

What the equation says, in words

“How many burgers will she hand over to get one more basket of fries — right at this bundle — and end up neither happier nor unhappier?”

\(dX\)  —  one more unit of \(X\) (a basket of fries)
\(dY\)  —  the units of \(Y\) given up (burgers) — negative along the curve

The slope of the curve at that point is the MRS.

Build it in order: indifference curves describe tradeoffs, then the question "how much Y for one more X?", then the slope answers it, and that slope is the MRS. The calculus form is MRS_XY = -dY/dX, the limit of the discrete ratio; the minus sign makes the MRS a positive number along a downward-sloping curve. One curve, one point, one tangent - that is all this slide claims. Point at the blue line and say it plainly: the slope of that tangent IS the marginal rate of substitution at that bundle. Then translate the formula out loud - dX is one more basket, dY is the burgers surrendered and is negative along the curve, and the minus sign is what keeps the MRS positive. Calculus Corner 4.1 — deriving the MRS

At \(A = (2,\,4)\) the slope is \(-2\): one more basket of fries costs her two burgers. What does that mean for her utility?

Total change in utility

\(dU = MU_{fries} \cdot dQ_{fries} + MU_{burgers} \cdot dQ_{burgers}\)

Along the indifference curve the change in utility is zero — she is just as well off: \(dU = 0\). Rearranging,

\(-MU_{burgers} \cdot dQ_{burgers} = MU_{fries} \cdot dQ_{fries}\)

and finally

\[\mathrm{MRS}_{XY} = -\frac{dQ_{burgers}}{dQ_{fries}} = \frac{MU_{fries}}{MU_{burgers}}\]

The general result

\[\mathrm{MRS}_{XY} = -\frac{dY}{dX} = \frac{MU_X}{MU_Y}\]

Any utility function, any two goods.

It generalizes. For any level set \(f(x_1, x_2) = c\), the slope is \(-\dfrac{\partial f/\partial x_1}{\partial f/\partial x_2}\). We use this exact trick again in Ch 7 to get \(\mathrm{MRTS} = MP_L/MP_K\), and in Ch 8 for the cost-minimizing tangency.

This is the single most important derivation in the chapter, and it is short. Press right or Space to reveal each step. Start at A = (2, 4), where the slope is -2. (1) Total change in utility from changing both quantities: dU equals MU_fries times dQ_fries plus MU_burgers times dQ_burgers. (2) Moving along ONE indifference curve, dU = 0, because she is equally well off - that is the whole trick. (3) Rearrange. (4) Divide through, and the MRS is the ratio of marginal utilities. Steps 5 and 6 open the right-hand panel: the same statement for any utility function and any two goods, and the observation that nothing here was special to utility - the slope of any level set is minus the ratio of partials, which is why the identical move returns for the MRTS in Ch 7 and the cost-minimizing tangency in Ch 8. Walkthrough 4.1 — Step 1 → Step 2, in Detail

\(x^{m} \cdot x^{n} = x^{m+n}\)  |  \(\dfrac{x^{m}}{x^{n}} = x^{m-n}\)  |  \(x^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{x^{n}}\) Math Refresher →

Form the ratio and cancel the \(\tfrac{1}{2}\)'s:

\(\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} = \dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2} = \dfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}\, x_1^{-1/2}\, x_2^{1/2}}{\tfrac{1}{2}\, x_1^{1/2}\, x_2^{-1/2}} = \dfrac{x_1^{-1/2}\, x_2^{1/2}}{x_1^{1/2}\, x_2^{-1/2}}\)

The \(x_1\) part — same base divided ⇒ subtract exponents:

\(\dfrac{x_1^{-1/2}}{x_1^{1/2}} = x_1^{(-1/2)\,-\,(1/2)} = x_1^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{x_1}\)

The \(x_2\) part — the \(x_2^{-1/2}\) in the denominator flips its sign, so we add:

\(\dfrac{x_2^{1/2}}{x_2^{-1/2}} = x_2^{(1/2)\,-\,(-1/2)} = x_2^{(1/2)\,+\,(1/2)} = x_2^{1} = x_2\)

\(\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} = x_2 \cdot \dfrac{1}{x_1} = \dfrac{x_2}{x_1}\)

The algebra behind Step 2, for students rusty on exponent rules. Three rules up top: x^m·x^n = x^(m+n) (multiply ⇒ add), x^m/x^n = x^(m-n) (divide ⇒ subtract), x^(-n) = 1/x^n (a power in the denominator flips sign). Then: (1) form the ratio — the 1/2 coefficients cancel. (2) x1 part: x1^(-1/2) / x1^(1/2) = x1^(-1/2 - 1/2) = x1^(-1) = 1/x1 — a negative exponent IS a denominator. (3) x2 part: dividing by x2^(-1/2) means subtracting a negative, i.e. adding: 1/2 - (-1/2) = 1, so x2^1 = x2. (4) Multiply the pieces: MRS = x2/x1. Emphasize the two trip-ups: "subtract a negative = add" and "negative exponent = put it in the denominator."

§4.4  Monotonic Transformations

The formal content of ordinal — cancellation, not philosophy

Monotonic Transformations — Why Bother?

What is it?

Apply a strictly increasing function \(g\) to a utility function: \(V = g(U)\). It re-labels the utility numbers but never changes which bundle ranks higher.

In plain English

Change the numbers on the scoreboard without changing who is winning. The ranking stays put; only the labels move.

Real-life example

Celsius → Fahrenheit (\(F = 1.8\,C + 32\)): every number changes, but "which day was hotter" never does. Same move: a 5-star vs a 100-point movie rating.

Why do we care?

Utility is ordinal — the exact numbers are arbitrary. So we may pick whichever scale makes the math easiest (e.g. take \(\ln\) of a Cobb-Douglas) without changing a single prediction.

The payoff, coming up next: the MRS — and therefore every demand curve in this book — is completely unchanged by a monotonic transformation.

Motivation before the formal claim. Four quick framings of one idea. (1) What it is: V = g(U) with g strictly increasing. (2) Plain English: relabel the scoreboard, keep the ranking. (3) Real-life: Celsius-to-Fahrenheit is the classic — F = 1.8C + 32 changes every number but preserves "hotter than"; a 5-star vs 100-point rating is the same move. (4) Why care: because utility is ordinal the numbers don't matter, so we can choose the most convenient representation — taking logs of Cobb-Douglas turns a product into a sum, exploited constantly in Ch 5. The key-result previews the next slide: the MRS is invariant, so every economic prediction is too.

Strictly increasing transformations represent the same preferences

Define \(V(x_1, x_2) = g(U(x_1, x_2))\), where \(g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) is strictly increasing (\(g'(U) > 0\)).

\(V\) represents the same preferences as \(U\): \(V(A) > V(B) \iff U(A) > U(B)\).

One-line proof: \(g\) is strictly increasing, so \(V(A) = g(U(A)) > g(U(B)) = V(B)\) iff \(U(A) > U(B)\). The ranking is preserved by definition of "strictly increasing."

Example transformations that all represent the same preferences as \(U\): \(\quad U + 7, \quad 2U, \quad U^3, \quad \ln U, \quad e^U, \quad \sqrt{U}\) (when \(U \geq 0\)).

Calculus Corner 4.2 — The chain-rule cancellation

Compute the MRS from \(V = g(U)\) using the chain rule:

\[\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_i} = g'(U) \cdot \frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i}, \qquad i = 1, 2.\]

Take the ratio — the \(g'(U)\) factor cancels in numerator and denominator:

\[\mathrm{MRS}^V = \frac{g'(U) \cdot MU_1}{g'(U) \cdot MU_2} = \frac{MU_1}{MU_2} = \mathrm{MRS}^U.\]

The cancellation, not the philosophy, is the operational content of "ordinal." Every observable object in consumer theory — demand curves, welfare measures, comparative statics — is invariant to the choice of \(g\).

Cobb-Douglas — the workhorse

A household spends about a third of its income on housing, two-thirds on everything else — and the split barely budges when rent or income moves. Some of both, traded smoothly, at a constant budget share:

\(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}, \qquad \alpha \in (0, 1)\)

The exponent \(\alpha\) is that budget share — here \(\alpha \approx \tfrac{1}{3}\) on housing. (Derived properly in Ch 5.)

Properties

Smooth, strictly convex, all four axioms hold. Bowed-toward-origin curves. Symmetric when \(\alpha = 1/2\). Closed-form MRS:

\(\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \dfrac{x_2}{x_1}\)

Try it — slide \(\alpha\) to morph the indifference map

\(\alpha\) = 0.50
← flat ICs (only \(x_2\) matters) symmetric steep ICs (only \(x_1\) matters) →
Start from the real consumer, then translate to a function - that is the move here. The household whose budget SPLIT between housing and everything else stays roughly stable regardless of prices or income is the picture; what makes it Cobb-Douglas is that the consumer wants some of both (an interior bundle, never a corner), trades off smoothly (not Leontief, not perfect substitutes), and holds a constant expenditure share on each good. That constant-share result is derived formally in Ch 5; here it is intuition, and BLS data backs it up for broad categories like housing - see Theory and Data 4.1 in two slides. Calculus Corner 4.3 — Cobb-Douglas MRS in closed form

From \(U = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}\):

\[MU_1 = \alpha x_1^{\alpha-1} x_2^{1-\alpha}, \qquad MU_2 = (1-\alpha) x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{-\alpha}\]

Taking the ratio and simplifying powers of \(x_1\) and \(x_2\):

\[\mathrm{MRS} = \frac{\alpha x_1^{\alpha-1} x_2^{1-\alpha}}{(1-\alpha) x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{-\alpha}} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \frac{x_2^{(1-\alpha)-(-\alpha)}}{x_1^{\alpha-(\alpha-1)}} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \frac{x_2}{x_1}\]

Walkthrough 4.3 — Housing vs everything else at \((8, 12)\)

🏠 A household's monthly budget. \(x_1\) = housing (rent units) takes about 1/3 of income; \(x_2\) = everything else takes about 2/3. Evaluate at \((8, 12)\): 8 units of housing, 12 of everything else.

\(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{1/3} x_2^{2/3}\) — consumer values \(x_2\) twice as much as \(x_1\)

Step 1 — identify parameters:

\(\alpha = 1/3\), \(1-\alpha = 2/3\)

Step 2 — plug into closed form:

\(\mathrm{MRS} = \dfrac{1/3}{2/3} \cdot \dfrac{12}{8} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{3}{2}\)

Step 3: \(\mathrm{MRS}(8, 12) = 3/4 = 0.75\)

Sanity check. Symmetric Cobb-Douglas at the same bundle gives \(\mathrm{MRS} = x_2/x_1 = 12/8 = 1.5\). Our asymmetric \(\alpha = 1/3\) cuts that in half — consumer cares less about \(x_1\), so willing to give up less \(x_2\) to get more \(x_1\).

Interpretation. At \((8, 12)\), the consumer would give up \(0.75\) units of \(x_2\) for one (small) more unit of \(x_1\). They'd want a price ratio \(p_1/p_2 \le 0.75\) to spend more on \(x_1\) at the optimum.

Theory and Data 4.1 — Cobb-Douglas in BLS data

A signature prediction of Cobb-Douglas: the consumer spends a constant fraction \(\alpha\) of income on \(x_1\) — independent of income, independent of prices. (We derive this in Ch 5.)

Approximately Cobb-Douglas

Housing share, transportation share — move within only a few percentage points of the average across BLS CES income quintiles.

Where it fails: food

Engel's law — food's share falls steadily as income rises. Named for Ernst Engel (Prussian statistician, 1857).

Cobb-Douglas is a useful approximation for some categories, a deliberately imperfect approximation for goods with strong income effects. Ch 6 builds the formal apparatus to talk about how demand depends on income.

Perfect substitutes (linear) U(x,y) = x + y — two goods that swap 1-for-1. A tangerine and an orange are pretty close substitutes; the consumer just cares about TOTAL citrus. U_a = 1+2 = 3, U_b = 2+2 = 4, U_c = 4+3 = 7. Indifference curves are straight lines with slope −1. Other examples: $1 bill + 4 quarters; identical brands of bottled water. Perfect substitutes — constant trade-off rate

Sometimes only the total matters — Duracell 🔋 or Energizer AAs, you just want four working batteries and the brand barely registers.

Every bundle on \(U = 6\) holds six working batteries — \((6,0)\), \((3,3)\), \((0,6)\) are all the same to her. One Duracell always trades for one Energizer.

\[U(x_1, x_2) = a x_1 + b x_2, \qquad a, b > 0\]

\(MU_1 = a, \quad MU_2 = b\)
\(\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} = a/b\) — the same everywhere.

A constant MRS is exactly what makes the curves straight and parallel, with slope \(-a/b\).

Ch 5 preview. Perfect substitutes \(\Rightarrow\) corner solutions, almost always: spend the entire budget on whichever good gives higher \(MU/p\). The tangency rule fails, because the indifference curve and the budget line have either the same slope everywhere or different slopes everywhere.

Plain-English first: Duracell and Energizer AAs - if you just want four working batteries, the brand split barely matters, you care about the total count. The signature is that every unit of x1 is worth a fixed number of units of x2. The figure makes it concrete: with a = b = 1 the MRS is 1, so every indifference curve is a straight line of slope -1. Walk the three dots on U = 6 - six Duracells, three of each, six Energizers - and ask which she prefers. None: only the total count matters. Higher lines are simply more working batteries. Then the Ch 5 consequence: with straight curves the optimum sits at a corner, and she buys whichever brand is cheaper per battery.

Walkthrough 4.4 — Store-brand vs national-brand sugar

The question

A shopper buys sugar: \(x_1\) = pounds of store brand, \(x_2\) = pounds of national brand, with \(U(x_1, x_2) = 2x_1 + 3x_2\).

Find the MRS — and check whether it changes as the shopper's basket changes.

Step 1 — partials:

\(MU_1 = 2, \quad MU_2 = 3\) — both constant

Step 2 — ratio:

\(\mathrm{MRS}_{1,2} = 2/3 \approx 0.667\)

Step 3: Same answer at \((1, 1)\), \((10, 1)\), \((1, 10)\), \((100, 100)\). The MRS does not depend on the bundle.

The consumer cares 1.5 times more about a pound of national-brand than store-brand. They'd give up only \(2/3\) of a pound of national-brand to get one extra pound of store-brand — regardless of how much they already have.

Optimization preview. If \(p_1/p_2 < 2/3\): spend all on \(x_1\) (store-brand cheap relative to value). If \(p_1/p_2 > 2/3\): spend all on \(x_2\). If \(p_1/p_2 = 2/3\) exactly: anywhere on the budget line is optimal.

Give them the question and 90 seconds before pressing through the steps: find the MRS, then test whether it moves as the basket moves. The answer - it never moves - is the whole signature of perfect substitutes. Interior optimum exists only on the knife-edge price ratio — non-generic. Almost every problem with perfect-substitute preferences delivers a corner.

Other examples: left + right shoes; peanut butter + bread; GPU + monitor. Adding more of just one side adds nothing.

Perfect complements (Leontief) U(F,W) = min(F, W) — two goods consumed in fixed proportions. Bicycle math: U_a = min(1, 2) = 1 (one bike built; 1 leftover wheel); U_b = min(2, 2) = 2 (two bikes, no leftover); U_c = min(4, 3) = 3 (three bikes; 1 leftover frame). Indifference curves are L-shaped at the kink F = W. The leftover units add zero utility — that\'s the perfect-complements signature.

Perfect complements — fixed proportions

The mirror image: goods that are useless without each other — a left and a right shoe. What matters is whichever you have less of.

Figure 4.10 — at \(A\), two of each. Adding a right shoe alone (\(B\)) leaves utility unchanged; only \(D\) — another left and right — reaches \(U_3\).

\[U(x_1, x_2) = \min(a x_1, b x_2), \qquad a, b > 0\]

L-shaped curves, with the corner along \(a x_1 = b x_2\). Off the corner the abundant good's marginal utility is zero — only the scarce good helps.

MRS at the corner is undefined (a kink). On the horizontal arm \(\mathrm{MRS} = 0\); on the vertical arm \(\mathrm{MRS} \to \infty\). Non-smooth utility.

Real near-complements: console + flagship game (Switch + Tears of the Kingdom), insulin pump + infusion sets, aircraft engine + maintenance contract.

The mirror of perfect substitutes. Plain-English: a left shoe and a right shoe - an extra left shoe alone does nothing, you are held back by whichever you have fewer of. The signature function is MIN, and utility tracks the scarcer good. Walk the figure by the points: A is two left and two right. Bundle B adds a right shoe only, so min(2,3) = 2 and B sits on the SAME curve - no gain from the unmatched shoe, which is the whole idea. To reach U3 the consumer needs D = three left and three right. The kink is where the MRS stops existing, and that matters in Ch 5: the optimum sits at the corner regardless of prices, so the tangency rule has nothing to bite on.

Walkthrough 4.5 — Hot dogs & buns at \(\bar U = 3\)

🌭 You’ve lived this: hot dogs come in packs of 10, buns in packs of 8 — so you can only build \(\min(10, 8) = 8\) complete hot dogs. Two lonely dogs, zero extra utility. Here \(x_1\) = hot dogs, \(x_2\) = buns.

\(U = \min(x_1, x_2)\), indifference curve at \(\bar U = 3\)

Three regions of the IC:

• Horizontal arm: \(x_1 \geq 3, x_2 = 3\). Adding more hot dogs doesn't help.
• Vertical arm: \(x_1 = 3, x_2 \geq 3\). Adding more buns doesn't help.
• Corner: \((3, 3)\). Both at 3.

MRS values:

• At \((5, 3)\) on horizontal arm: \(\mathrm{MRS} = 0\)
• At \((3, 5)\) on vertical arm: \(\mathrm{MRS} = \infty\)
• At corner \((3, 3)\): undefined (kink)

\(\mathrm{MRS} = 0\) on the horizontal arm captures the perfect-complements intuition: extra hot dogs are useless without matching buns.

Ch 5 preview. The optimal bundle always sits on the corner locus \(a x_1 = b x_2\), regardless of prices. The pricing question becomes "how far up the corner locus does the budget take us?" — not "where does \(\mathrm{MRS} = p_1/p_2\)?"

Application 4.3 — Console + flagship title

2017 — Nintendo launches Switch. Hardware sales modest until The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild ships alongside.

For a substantial slice of consumers, the console was the title. The whole purchase made sense only paired.

When the model fits

The L-shape is an idealization. Real consumers do trade off hardware and software at some margin (some buy hardware first hoping titles arrive). But the sharp non-linearity — having neither is much worse than having both — is a real feature.

A launch without a flagship complementary title routinely underperforms one with. Industry analytics keep estimating the lift, and they keep finding it.

The Curvature of Indifference Curves

(a) Almost straight → close substitutes

Decaf lattes and decaf coffees are nearly interchangeable. The curves bow only slightly — MRS changes, but slowly. Behavior is near-SUM.

(b) Very curved → strong complements

Tortilla chips and guacamole pull hard toward each other. The curves bow sharply — MRS changes fast. Behavior is near-MIN.

Perfect substitutes and perfect complements are the endpoints. Curvature is a dial, not a switch — most real goods land somewhere in between.

Press ↓ — curvature can also change within one consumer's map.

Frame it first: the curvature of an indifference curve is information. The straighter it is, the more the two goods substitute for each other; the more sharply it bows toward the origin, the more they complement each other. Figure 4.8 — the realistic picture. The perfect-substitutes (straight lines) and perfect-complements (L-shapes) slides just before this are the two textbook extremes; real goods live on the spectrum between them. Panel (a): decaf lattes vs decaf coffees — very close substitutes, so the indifference curves are almost straight (only gently bowed). Panel (b): tortilla chips vs guacamole — strong complements, so the curves bow sharply (but not perfectly L-shaped — you would still take more chips even with no extra guac). The takeaway: curvature is continuous. How sharply an indifference curve bends measures how substitutable two goods are, and that lives on a dial between the SUM and MIN extremes.

The Same Consumer Can Have Different Shapes

Figure 4.11 — 🍌 bananas vs 🍓 strawberries.

Initially, for low levels of utility \((U_A)\), bananas and strawberries might be substitutes — the curve is gentle, the consumer just wants fruit.

As utility increases \((U_B)\), the consumer might prefer a variety of fruit in their diet more than initially — the curve bows sharply, balance now matters.

Curvature can change along one consumer's map — preferences need not look the same everywhere.

Figure 4.11 — a subtle but important point: nothing in the axioms forces an indifference map to have the same curvature everywhere. Here one consumer's two curves differ. At low utility (U_A) the curve is gently bowed — bananas and strawberries act like substitutes, the consumer just wants fruit and is not fussy about which. At higher utility (U_B) the curve bows sharply — now the consumer wants a balanced variety of both, so the goods behave more like complements. The shape of an indifference curve is local information about the trade-off at that point; it can vary across the map for a single, perfectly rational consumer.

Your Turn — A Neutral Good (Sofia's YouTube vs TikTok)

Sofia watches \(Y\) hours of YouTube and \(T\) hours of TikTok. More YouTube ⇒ happier. TikTok is neutral — doesn't move utility. Find the IC map and the MRS at \((T, Y) = (1, 1)\).

Step 1 — vary \(Y\) at fixed \(T\):

\(B = (1, 2)\) has more YouTube than \(A = (1, 1)\) ⇒ \(B \succ A\). Higher \(Y\) sits on a higher IC.

Step 2 — vary \(T\) at fixed \(Y\):

\(C = (2, 1)\) gives Sofia more TikTok than \(A\), but \(T\) is neutral ⇒ \(C \sim A\). Same for \(D, E\) at \(Y = 1\).

Step 3 — the shape: ICs are horizontal lines. \(A, C, D, E\) all sit on \(U_1\); \(B, F\) sit on the higher \(U_2\).

MRS at \((1, 1)\) is the slope of \(U_1\), which is \(0\). \(\mathrm{MRS}_{T, Y} = 0\) — Sofia gives up zero YouTube for an extra hour of TikTok.

Horizontal ICs — the signature of a neutral good.

Walkthrough — the neutral-good case. Sofia likes YouTube (a "good") and is neutral on TikTok (a "neutral good"). Construction: (1) vary Y at fixed T — B beats A because more YouTube on a higher IC. (2) Vary T at fixed Y — C is equivalent to A because neutral T doesn't move utility; same for D, E. (3) Conclusion — ICs are horizontal lines parameterized by Y. MRS_{T, Y} = -dY/dT along a horizontal line equals 0. Sofia gives up zero YouTube to get an extra hour of TikTok. From a Real Consumer to a Quasilinear Function

1 — Start from a real situation

Think about cups of coffee ☕. The first cup of the day is a lifesaver; the second is nice; by the third you barely care. But the money you spend on everything else — each extra dollar is just as useful as the last.

2 — Why this is "quasilinear"

  • One good (coffee) has diminishing marginal utility — curved
  • "Everything else" is just money — constant marginal utility, perfectly linear
  • Hand the consumer \(\$20\) more and they don't buy more coffee — no income effect on the curved good

3 — Write it as a function

\[U(x_1, x_2) = v(x_1) + x_2\]

\(x_1\) = coffee, with \(v\) concave (\(v' > 0,\ v'' < 0\)); \(x_2\) = money for everything else, entering linearly.

Motivation before the Quasilinear workhorse slide — same three-step move as the Cobb-Douglas motivation: start from a real consumer, then translate to a function. (1) The real situation: cups of coffee have sharply diminishing marginal utility — the first matters a lot, the third barely at all — while "everything else" is just money, where each extra dollar is worth the same as the last. (2) Why that is quasilinear: one good is curved (diminishing MU), the other is linear (constant MU). The defining fingerprint — an extra $20 of income does NOT change coffee consumption; it all flows to "everything else," so there is no income effect on the curved good. (3) The function: U = v(x_1) + x_2, with v strictly concave (v' > 0, v'' < 0) and x_2 entering linearly. The next slide develops the MRS = v'(x_1) result and the vertical-translation geometry.

Quasilinear — one good linear, one good not

\[U(x_1, x_2) = v(x_1) + x_2\]

\(v\) smooth, strictly increasing, strictly concave: \(v' > 0\), \(v'' < 0\)

MRS depends only on \(x_1\)

\(MU_1 = v'(x_1), \quad MU_2 = 1\). \(\mathrm{MRS} = v'(x_1)\) — no \(x_2\) anywhere.

Geometry

Indifference curves are vertical translations of each other — same shape, just shifted up in \(x_2\).

Why it’s so useful: kills the income effect on \(x_1\). Ch 6's Slutsky decomposition becomes one-line clean. \(x_2\) absorbs all wealth changes.

Standard interpretation: \(x_1\) = "the specific good of interest"; \(x_2\) = "money for everything else."

The cleanest non-convex example

📱 Alex's smartphone world. \(x_1\) = hours a week on iOS apps, \(x_2\) = hours on Android. Going all-in on one platform compounds the value of everything in it; a 50/50 split strands Alex's messages, files and payments across two walled gardens that don't talk.

\(U(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + x_2^2\)  at  \(\bar U = 25\)

Three bundles — Alex's hours per week:

\((5, 0)\) all iOS: \(U = 25\)
\((0, 5)\) all Android: \(U = 25\)
Midpoint \((2.5, 2.5)\) split evenly: \(U = 12.5\)

Convexity test. Averages should be \(\succsim\) extremes. Here the midpoint scores \(12.5\) against \(25\) — exactly half. Mixing throws away half the value. Violated.

Level sets bow away from the origin. The dashed line joins two indifferent bundles — and its midpoint lands on a lower curve.

The convexity axiom, broken on purpose, with numbers clean enough to do in your head. Alex's ecosystem story first: all-in on one platform compounds the value of everything in it, while a 50/50 split strands data across two walled gardens - which is why utility grows faster than linear within a platform, and x1 squared plus x2 squared captures exactly that. Then the arithmetic: both corners give 25, the midpoint gives 12.5. Say the punchline plainly - mixing throws away half the value, which is the lock-in tax in one number. On the figure, point at the dashed chord: for convex preferences the midpoint would sit on a HIGHER curve; here it drops to a lower one. Close on Ch 5: tangency assumes convexity, so with these preferences the first-order condition can hand you a local worst and the real optimum is a corner.

Heads Up 4.4 — "Convex preferences" vs. "convex function"

A perennial terminology trap. They are inverses in this context.

Convex preferences

Upper contour set is convex; ICs bow toward the origin.
Math language: \(U\) is quasi-concave (or concave).

Convex function \(U\)

Level sets bow away from the origin (e.g., \(U = x_1^2 + x_2^2\)).
Represents non-convex preferences!

Key Takeaways

  1. Four axioms. Completeness, transitivity, monotonicity, convexity. Each does specific geometric work; each has a real-world failure mode.
  2. Utility is ordinal. Any strictly increasing transformation \(V = g(U)\) represents the same preferences and yields the same MRS. The cancellation, not philosophy, is the operational content.
  3. The MRS is a derivative. \(\mathrm{MRS} = MU_1/MU_2 > 0\). One equation bridges geometry and calculus — and we'll reuse it for MRTS in Ch 7.
  4. Four canonical families. Cobb-Douglas (workhorse, smooth), perfect substitutes (constant MRS), perfect complements (L-shape, kink), quasilinear (no income effect).
  5. Non-convexity is real. Lock-in markets — smartphone ecosystems, frequent-flyer status — produce non-convex preferences. Tangency rule fails; corners win.
  6. Bridge to Ch 5. The MRS is one half of the optimum; the price ratio is the other. They meet at the tangency: \(\mathrm{MRS} = p_1/p_2\).

The Netflix ranking and Spotify's Taste Profile both encode an ordinal ranking of bundles — nothing more. Today we wrote the page-and-pencil version. Ch 5 picks the optimum.
