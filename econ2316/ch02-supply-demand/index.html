7.1

What Does the Model Assume?

Three modeling assumptions

1. Homogeneous good — every Grade A large dozen interchangeable. The good is a commodity (eggs, wheat, copper, crude). Not a Picasso.

2. Single price + shared info — all participants pay/receive the same price; share info on prices, qualities, substitutes.

3. Many buyers and sellers — none large enough to move price alone. Chs 11–14 study what breaks when this fails.

All three together = a perfectly competitive market — an approximation, not a literal claim.

Three assumptions, and the next slide zooms straight into the first. Stress that they are approximations chosen for a question, not claims about the world — the Ch 1 subway-map point, now applied.

Homogeneous Goods

Not a claim about chemistry — a claim about buyers. A good is homogeneous when buyers will switch on price alone.

Different recipe, same market

Every brand blends its own additive package — Shell V-Power, Chevron Techron. The fuel is not chemically identical. Drivers buy on price and whichever exit comes first.

8.2

Answer: C today — and drifting toward A

Homogeneity is not a yes/no property. It is a matter of degree — and it moves.

At launch

Capabilities far apart. Different prices, different target users, different things each was good at. Buyers picked a model and stayed. Strongly differentiated.

Today

Performance clusters on most everyday tasks. Prices cluster. Interfaces converge. Buyers switch more readily — and increasingly on price. Drifting toward commodity.

As rivals converge, buyers become more willing to switch on price alone — and the good commoditises.

Which is exactly why firms spend so much to stay different (Ch 12), and why perfect competition (Ch 10) is the limit of this process, not its starting point.

The point students should leave with is that homogeneity is a DEGREE and a DIRECTION, not a label. Run the history out loud: at launch the frontier models differed sharply - in what they could do, what they cost, and who they were sold to - so buyers committed to one and stayed. Today they cluster on price, on interface, and on everyday-task performance, so switching is cheap and increasingly done on price. Same good, drifting from the aspirin case toward the gasoline case, inside three years. Two payoffs. First, it reframes the whole course: perfect competition (Ch 10) is where a market ENDS UP once rivals converge, not an unrealistic starting assumption - and monopolistic competition (Ch 13) is the middle of that road. Second, it explains firm behaviour they can see: the enormous effort spent on brand, ecosystem lock-in and proprietary features is a fight against exactly this drift (Ch 12). If someone answered A, tell them they are early rather than wrong. Do not quote specific model prices - they move monthly and the argument does not need them.

11.1

Building Demand from Willingness to Pay

Where the demand function \(Q^d(p)\) comes from: stack individual buyers' maximum prices, tallest to shortest.

Willingness to pay (WTP) — the maximum a buyer would pay for one more unit before walking away.

A function — and its inverse.

\(Q^d(p)\) input a price → get the quantity demanded — it's just an \(f(x)\). \(p^d(Q)\) invert it — input a quantity → get the price. That's inverse demand.

A pint of strawberries · 5 buyers · price \$4

Buyer WTP At \$4?
Alex\$7✓ buys
Beatriz\$6✓ buys
Chen\$5✓ buys
Devon ← marginal\$4= price
Esha\$3× walks

Sorted high → low, these WTPs are the demand curve. At \$4, four pints sell; Devon's WTP exactly equals the price, so \(p^d(4)=\$4\).

The height of \(p^d(Q)\) at each \(Q\) is a WTP — the area between that curve and the price you actually paid is consumer surplus (§2.5).

WTP links individual choice to the market demand function. Left column = the concept: WTP defined, then the function/inverse pair — \(Q^d(p)\) reads price→quantity, and (reveal) \(p^d(Q)\) inverts it to read quantity→price. Right column = the concrete 5-buyer strawberry table: sort the WTPs high-to-low and they ARE the demand curve; Devon is the marginal buyer at the \$4 price. Footer band = the bridge: the height of inverse demand at each \(Q\) is a WTP, which is the §2.5 consumer-surplus integrand. The three strawberry videos that follow ground it in a real product before we go algebraic. The two functions, with shifters

Demand

\(Q^d(p; \alpha_D)\)

Own price \(p\) and a vector of demand shifters \(\alpha_D\): income, prices of substitutes & complements, tastes, expectations, number of buyers.

Supply

\(Q^s(p; \alpha_S)\)

Same own price; supply shifters \(\alpha_S\): input prices, technology, number of sellers, expectations — and for our running example, the size of the laying flock.

Two derivatives, two meanings: \(\partial Q^d/\partial p\) is movement along (slope of demand curve, holding shifters fixed); \(\partial Q^d/\partial \alpha_D\) is shift (the whole curve moves). §2.4 hinges on this distinction.

Workhorse all chapter: linear \(Q^d = a - bp\), \(Q^s = c + dp\) with \(a, b, c, d > 0\).

Wrong

Right

Shock it → re-optimize → new mutual best response.

Equilibrium is at rest not because nothing happens but because each agent's action is already the optimal response to everyone else's. Information Is the Key

Two queues, identical except for one thing: whether you can see the other line.

Checkout lines — you can see them

Everyone joins the shortest. The lines even out — and no one can then do better by moving.

Peak hour? Longer lines, still nobody switching.

Nobody moves. Nobody regrets it. That is equilibrium, and you can watch it happen.

Highway lanes — you cannot

You judge your lane from the one car beside you — a terrible signal.

So drivers switch, switch back, and arrive no sooner.

Constant switching. Constant regret. Same problem, hidden information — and it never settles.

The signature of equilibrium is that nobody wants to move. Markets reach it when the information needed to choose is cheap to see — and churn when it is not. Ch 16 is the whole chapter on what happens when it is not.

This makes the previous slide observable. "Mutual best response" is abstract; "nobody is switching lines" is something they watched happen this week. Run it as a question first: "why do supermarket queues even out, but highway lanes never do?" Let them land on visibility themselves. Three points to stress. (1) Equal lines ARE the equilibrium - not a coincidence, and not anybody's plan. (2) At peak hour the queues are longer and it is STILL equilibrium; equilibrium does not mean good, it means no one can do better by moving, which is exactly the Ch 1 definition. (3) The highway is the counter-case: the good is the same, self-interest is the same, only the information differs - so the failure is informational, not behavioural. The lane illusion is real and documented (Redelmeier and Tibshirani, Nature 1999): you spend more time being overtaken than overtaking, so the next lane genuinely looks faster even when it is not. That is the hand-off to Ch 16, and to the surge-pricing slide next, where a visible price is what lets the market clear in real time.

Why markets move toward equilibrium

The condition \(Q^d(p^*) = Q^s(p^*)\) is just an equation. What forces the price toward \(p^*\)? Adam Smith's invisible hand.

Price above \(p^*\) — SURPLUS

\(Q^s > Q^d\). Inventories pile up → sellers discount → price falls. \(Q^s\) drops along supply, \(Q^d\) rises along demand → gap closes.

Price below \(p^*\) — SHORTAGE

\(Q^d > Q^s\). Buyers compete → price bid up. \(Q^d\) falls along demand, \(Q^s\) rises along supply → gap closes.

Symmetric, decentralized, robust. No central planner needs to know each buyer's WTP or each seller's cost — price itself carries the info, like a wave carrying the shape of the sea floor.

Surplus & Shortage — on the diagram

A symmetric asparagus calibration (\(Q^d = 100 - 20p\), \(Q^s = -20 + 20p\) — equal slopes, so \(E^* = (40, \$3)\) with a mirror-image gap). Move the price line off equilibrium and the gap is the imbalance.

Price too high — SURPLUS

At \(p = \$4\): \(Q^s = 60\) but \(Q^d = 20\).
Inventories pile up → sellers discount → price falls back toward \$3.

Price too low — SHORTAGE

At \(p = \$2\): \(Q^d = 60\) but \(Q^s = 20\).
Buyers compete → price bid up toward \$3.

Symmetric. Above \(p^*\): excess supply → price falls. Below \(p^*\): excess demand → price rises. Both forces vanish exactly at \(p^*\). The "invisible hand" is just the gap-driven price adjustment, drawn out.

Baseline equilibrium

\(4{,}500 - 150p = 150p - 1{,}500 \Rightarrow 300p = 6{,}000\)

Theory and Data — Does Surge Pricing Actually Work?

Two natural experiments from Uber's own data: the same model, tested against reality.

Surge OFF → the system breaks

New Year's Eve, NYC (Dec 31, 2014). A glitch left surge pricing off for a stretch. With the fare stuck at its normal level, riders far outnumbered available drivers — completion rates collapsed and waits spiked. A textbook shortage.

Surge ON → the system works

Ariana Grande concert, MSG (Mar 21, 2015). As the crowd poured out, surge kicked in. The higher fare drew more drivers and rationed rides to those who valued them most — completion rates held near 100 percent. The market kept clearing.

The model's prediction, in Uber's own data: let the price rise → the market clears; freeze it → shortage. That is an efficiency result — it still says nothing about whether the surge fare is fair.

Source: Hall, Kendrick & Nosko, "The Effects of Uber's Surge Pricing: A Case Study" (2015); covered in The Economist, "A fare shake" (May 14, 2016).

Theory meets data, and it closes the surge arc. The Hall-Kendrick-Nosko case study uses two events as natural experiments. (1) NYE 2014: a surge OUTAGE - the algorithm failed to raise prices - so the fare was effectively capped; the result was exactly the shortage the S/D diagram predicts: completion rates crashed, waits spiked. (2) The Ariana Grande concert: surge worked normally and completion rates stayed near 100 percent. The takeaway is the chapter's arrow-to-number and positive-vs-normative thread: the data confirm surge CLEARS the market (efficiency) but the study cannot tell us whether the price is fair - that is a value judgment. Equilibrium = the equation \(Q^d(p^*) = Q^s(p^*)\)

A market clears when the quantity buyers want at the prevailing price equals the quantity sellers want. With functions, that statement becomes an equation we solve.

For the linear system \(Q^d = a - bp\), \(Q^s = c + dp\):

\[ p^* = \frac{a - c}{b + d}, \qquad Q^* = \frac{ad + bc}{b + d}.\]

Pay special attention to the denominator \(b + d\).

Do we have to grind through this every time? Not really. We solve it once in this standard parametric form — then for any new market we just drop the parameters \((a,b,c,d)\) into the shortcut. No re-deriving. That frees us to spend our energy on the real elephant in the room : how equilibrium \((p^*, Q^*)\) moves when a shifter changes (§2.3).

The denominator \(b + d\) — the sum of the slopes — will keep reappearing in §2.3's comparative-statics formulas. Same arithmetic, multiple uses.

What do scalpers mean to the economy?

Scalpers (resellers, arbitrageurs) are the price-discovery mechanism when posted prices sit below market clearing. The question economists ask: do they create surplus or just redistribute it?

Pro: efficient allocation

The set reaches the buyer whose WTP is \$300, not the lucky Costco shopper whose WTP might be \$120. The \$191 of total surplus only exists if the trade happens . Scalpers complete trades the posted-price market would have missed.

Con: surplus capture

Of the \$191, the reseller takes \$96 (PS). Without scalpers, that same surplus might have gone to a lower-WTP buyer's CS . Same total surplus, different distribution — which offends fairness norms.

Takeaway 1 — efficiency vs distribution. Anti-scalping laws (concert tickets, COVID-era PPE, Pokémon resale bans) are distributional policies dressed in efficiency language. §2.2's invisible hand maximizes total surplus; the visible hand decides who gets what share. Wendy's, Costco, and Ticketmaster all face the same tradeoff.

Frame: scalpers ARE the §2.2 mechanism in action. Pro side — allocation efficiency: trade happens that wouldn't have. Con side — distribution: lucky Costco shoppers lose surplus to resellers. Anti-scalping laws favor fairness over efficiency. Connect back to Wendy's dynamic-pricing slide: same tradeoff, different actors. §2.6  Wrap-up

3 min · Where Ch 3 picks up

Key Takeaways

  1. Demand and supply are functions, not curves. Algebra beats eyeballing. Linear: \(p^* = (a-c)/(b+d)\).
  2. Surplus is an area. For linear demand the area is a triangle, so the geometry is exact — and real policies (NYC congestion pricing) get valued this way, in dollars.
  3. Comparative statics is one formula. Total-differentiate the market-clearing condition; sign both \(dp^*/d\alpha\) and \(dQ^*/d\alpha\) separately.
  4. Movement along ≠ shift. Two different partial derivatives. Co-movement direction of \(p\) and \(Q\) is the diagnostic.
  5. Linear is a starting point. Ch 3 generalizes via elasticity.
