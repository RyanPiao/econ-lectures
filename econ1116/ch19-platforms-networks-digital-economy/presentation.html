Before we model it

Types of Networks

Three ways people get connected — physical, virtual, and social.

Physical Networks — Built on Infrastructure

Connected by a physical structure you could point to.

  Fiber optics — the cables behind telephone & internet networks.

  Satellites — GPS and global communication networks.

  Transportation routes — airline hub-and-spoke systems.

Delta’s route map — hubs concentrate routes, so more cities are reachable per flight.

Massive fixed costs + economies of scale push these toward a few big players (Ch 11) — but the digital economy lives in the next two types.

Physical networks are the tangible layer: cables, satellites, runways. Two economics points to make: (1) they carry enormous fixed/sunk costs, which is why they tend toward a handful of providers (natural-monopoly tendencies from Ch 11); (2) the hub-and-spoke map is a visual preview of network density — a hub lets one airport reach dozens of destinations, so value rises faster than route count (the Metcalfe intuition two slides later). Use this to pivot: the chapter focuses on the virtual and social layers, where network effects are strongest and entry is cheapest.

Virtual Networks — Same Brand, Same Ecosystem

Connected by groups of people using the same brand of a good or service.

Gaming console & network

More players to match with, more developers making games for the platform.

Mobile phone & ecosystem

iMessage, App Store, AirDrop — the brand keeps your devices in one world.

Software & search engines

More users → more data → better results → more users.

Value rises with the installed base — direct and cross-side network effects. This is the chapter’s home turf.

Virtual networks are bound by a shared brand or standard rather than a wire. Consoles: same-side (more players) plus cross-side (more developers) — the textbook two-sided case. Phone ecosystems: Apple’s iMessage/App Store/AirDrop bundle is a virtual network plus switching costs (the green-bubble lock-in we hit later). Search: a data feedback loop — more queries train better results, which attract more queries, entrenching the leader. These are where network effects bite hardest and where today’s platform giants sit.

Social Networks — Connected Through People

Connected virtually, but also directly through contacts within the network.

Social-network apps

Instagram, TikTok, X — you join because your friends are already there.

Face-to-face clubs

Conferences and meetups — the same pull, offline (e.g., CES).

Community forums

Reddit & Discord — the content is the other users.

The purest direct network effect: value = who else is in your graph. Exactly the TikTok stickiness from the hook.

Social networks add person-to-person ties on top of the virtual layer. The defining feature: value comes almost entirely from the other users — an empty social app or a dead forum is worthless. This is the cleanest direct network effect and it loops straight back to the TikTok hook (you stayed for the people, not the features) and forward to tipping and lock-in. Note that offline clubs (CES, alumni groups) obey the same logic — networks aren’t only digital.

What Is a Network Good?

A network good is a good or service whose usefulness depends on the existence of a physical, virtual, or social network.

Examples you carry every day:

  Smartphones

  Streaming apps

  VR headsets

  Voice assistants

Strip away the network and the good loses its value — a phone with no one to call, a feed with no one to follow.

The definition that anchors the chapter. A network good is nearly worthless in isolation — its value is created by the network around it. Tie each example to a type from the last three slides: smartphones (virtual + social), streaming (virtual brand ecosystem), VR (virtual + emerging social), voice assistants (virtual ecosystem + data). This sets up the central question of Section 1: if value depends on others, what happens as the network grows? That is the network effect.

Section 1 · LO 19.1

Network Effects: Why Size Matters

The value of a product depends on how many other people use it.

The Mechanism: Network Externality

Each extra user creates a positive external benefit for everyone else — a network externality.

The twist: unlike a normal externality, it benefits all users — existing and potential — raising the whole network’s value and pulling in even more demand.

Diminishing returns: the 100th user adds far more than the 100-millionth.

Airbnb: more hosts attract more guests — and vice versa.

Because the benefit spills onto others, growth feeds on itself — the engine behind tipping points.

This is the engine. Frame the network effect as a positive externality: my joining makes the product better for you. The distinctive feature versus a standard externality — it lands on ALL users including future ones, which feeds back into more demand (the self-reinforcing loop behind tipping). Diminishing returns set up the demand curve two slides on: early users add huge value, late users little, which is why the network demand curve eventually bends down. The Airbnb diagram shows both cross-side (hosts↔guests) and same-side effects. Next we split the effect into its two flavors.

Two Flavors of Network Effect

Network effect — the value of a good to each user rises as more people use it. (Older texts: “network externality.”)

Direct — same side

Users benefit from more users like them. Every friend who joins WhatsApp makes it more useful to you.

Cross-side (indirect)

One side benefits from the other side. More Airbnb hosts attract more guests — and vice versa.

A gaming console has both: more players (direct) and more developers (cross-side).

The core distinction of the section. Direct = same-side (messaging, social). Cross-side = across two groups on a platform (riders/drivers, guests/hosts, users/advertisers). Many platforms have both, but which one dominates shapes strategy and whether the market tips. Note: more hosts actually compete with each other (same-side NEGATIVE), but the cross-side pull dominates.

Spotting the Effect in the Wild

WhatsApp — pure direct. Your experience improves every time a friend joins.

Airbnb — pure cross-side. Hosts don’t want more hosts; they want more guests.

PlayStation — both. More players to match with, more developers making games.

Why the distinction matters for strategy: with a direct effect, the platform must grow ONE pool fast; with a cross-side effect, it must coordinate TWO pools (the chicken-and-egg problem in Section 2). Ask students to classify Uber (cross-side: riders/drivers), LinkedIn (both), a dating app (cross-side, and notoriously hard to balance).

Example — Free Music, Then Sales

Aspiring musicians often release new songs free on streaming sites.

As a track gains listeners, each new fan makes it more likely to be shared, playlisted, and discovered — a network effect.

Popularity compounds — and sales (concerts, merch, licensing) rise with the fan network.

Give the good away to grow the network, monetize once it’s large — the same playbook as Google Photos.

A relatable network-effect example. The free release is deliberate: a zero price maximizes adoption, and adoption is what creates value here (more listeners → more shares → more discovery). Once the fan network is large, the artist monetizes adjacent goods — tickets, merch, sync licensing — whose demand scales with the fan base. Same structure as the penetration-pricing / Google Photos story: subsidize growth, harvest later. (If the SoundCloud brand glyph fails to load, it falls back to a blank icon — harmless.)

The External Benefit of a Network

Each new subscriber benefits everyone already on the network — a benefit their own demand curve ignores.

So demand shifts right (DP→DS): equilibrium subscribers rise QP→QS and price PP→PS.

The gap between DP and DS is the external benefit — value the network creates for others.

Rwanda’s drone network is this gap made vivid — the benefit reaches whole communities, not just the buyer.

Cash out the clip with the graph. Private demand D_P reflects only the value each subscriber gets for themselves; because a network good also benefits everyone else, social demand D_S sits to the right. The horizontal shift means more subscribers and a higher market-clearing price; the vertical gap at any quantity is the external benefit — value spilling onto others. Tie back to the clip: the buyer is the health system, but the benefit lands on patients, families, and whole villages — a textbook positive externality, and the reason networks are often subsidized to grow. Network Demand

Why a network good’s demand curve rises before it falls — and how firms price along it.

Fixed-Capacity Network Good

Vertical short-run supply. Capacity is capped by the firm’s fixed investment and expands only in large increments (S0 → S1) — classic case: an airline with a fixed number of planes.

Each capacity level has its own demand curve. A bigger network is worth more, so the network effect pushes demand out (D0 → D1) — partly offset by the price effect.

  HuskyHive: its servers & AI compute are the fixed capacity — only so many Huskies can be served at once. Scaling means adding a whole new cluster: one big lump.

Airlines add planes in lumps; HuskyHive adds server clusters in lumps — both face a vertical short-run supply that jumps, then holds.

When capacity is fixed (planes, server farms, spectrum), short-run supply is a vertical line and grows only in lumps. Each capacity level pairs with its own demand curve because a bigger installed base is worth more to every user. Expanding capacity (S0 to S1) sells more, but the network effect (demand out to D1) keeps price from collapsing. Figure: 1116 Worth Publishers.

The Unique Network Demand Curve

Value rises as more people subscribe, so the curve has two parts:

1. Upward (Core Users). Network effect > price effect. HuskyHive’s early Huskies seed the Q&A library and aren’t price-sensitive — as they pile in, price rises.

2. Downward (Casual Users). The network matures, the effect weakens (diminishing returns), price effect > network effect — HuskyHive’s exam-crammers join once the price drops.

HuskyHive climbs the curve on its core Huskies, then rides it back down by opening up to casual users.

The signature shape of a network good. Early on, adding users raises willingness-to-pay faster than the larger quantity lowers it (network effect > price effect), so the demand curve slopes UP through the core-user region. Once the network is large, diminishing network benefits let the ordinary price effect take over and the curve bends back DOWN through the casual-user region. The firm rides up the curve while building, then down it while harvesting. Figure: 1116 Worth Publishers.

Pricing Over the Network Life Cycle

1. New network. Price low to attract the first users.

2. Growing. Prices rise — a good used by more people is worth more.

3. Mature. Prices fall to sell a greater quantity.

The pricing path traces the network demand curve. Launch low to seed adoption (penetration pricing); raise price as the installed base and the network effect grow; then cut price once the network matures to reach the price-sensitive casual users. VR headsets illustrate it: the Rift DK1 launched at $300, usage climbed and plateaued, and nominal headset prices drifted down across 2016-2022.

Network Pricing in Practice — Google

1. Freemium. Free search (1998) builds the largest possible user network — the core-user phase.

2. Subsidize-and-extract. Ads (AdWords, 2000): users stay free, paying in attention; advertisers pay the cash. Two-sided — subsidize one side, extract from the other.

3. Charge the user. With a locked-in ecosystem, bill users directly too — Google One storage/photos, YouTube Premium & Music, Workspace email.

Google is the textbook network-demand-curve pricing path. Free at launch to build the largest possible search network (the core-user, network-effect phase), then monetized via AdWords once the network was huge. The "price" is implicit: attention and data, not a sticker price. This is the digital-platform version of riding up then harvesting the network demand curve.

Google — Bare Results → Monetized Search

As the network matured, bare organic results gave way to ad-laden results — the implicit price of a free, ubiquitous network good.

Subsidize → extract → then bill the user. Ads were only stage two. Once you live inside Gmail, Drive, Photos, and YouTube, Google also charges directly — Google One, YouTube Premium & Music, Workspace — just like a normal subscription market.

The "after" view shows how the harvest phase looks on the page: the same free search now surrounds organic results with paid ads and sponsored answers. Users still pay nothing at the till, but the network is monetized through their attention. This visual makes the opportunity-cost "price" of a free network good concrete.

Metcalfe’s Law: Value Compounds

Metcalfe’s Law: value grows with the square of users — n people make n(n−1)/2 possible connections.

Double the users → roughly 4× the value. That convexity is what makes a leader pull away.

The critique (Odlyzko–Tilly): you don’t talk to everyone — value is closer to n·log n. Still super-linear.

Metcalfe gives the math behind "size matters": pairwise connections scale as n². This convexity is why mergers in network markets are so valuable (per-user value rises with size) and why a leader compounds. Honest caveat: the n² law overstates it — most users don't connect to most others; Odlyzko & Tilly's n·log n (Zipf) is the empirically better fit. We revisit the exact numbers in the exit ticket.

Market Equilibrium

Where a network good settles — and why leadership flips so fast.

Market Equilibrium for a Network Good

  • Equilibrium on the downward slope: usually reached on the downward-sloping part of the network demand curve, after most network effects are realized (point d, where demand meets MC).
  • Volatility: not all network goods reach or sustain equilibrium — expansion and decline can be rapid.
  • Economies of scale: large fixed costs (software, infrastructure) + low marginal costs (one more user ≈ free) → big economies of scale, like monopolies and oligopolies.
Network goods settle on the DOWNWARD slope of the network demand curve (point d, demand = MC), after the network effect has mostly played out. The cost structure mirrors a monopoly/oligopoly: huge fixed costs to build the platform, near-zero marginal cost to add a user, so large economies of scale. fc_mc.png: data-center fixed cost vs a few dollars marginal cost per user.

Finding an Equilibrium

Equilibrium = the downward-sloping network demand curve meets MC (point d).

Tipping point (critical mass) — point b: the quantity at which network effects are strong enough to keep the network growing. It must expand from the origin to b to sustain growth.

Vicious cycle: fails to reach the tipping point → users leave → value falls for others → sharp decline and likely failure.

Virtuous cycle: past the tipping point → network effects push demand right → more value → expansion until equilibrium.

Equilibrium is where the DOWNWARD portion crosses MC (point d). The catch is getting there: the network must first claw from the origin past the tipping point b (critical mass). Below b, the network effect is too weak, churn feeds a vicious cycle toward collapse. Above b, the network effect dominates, a virtuous cycle pushes demand right to equilibrium. Whether a network lives or dies hinges on crossing b.

Tipping Point — Westmatch vs Facebook

Westmatch’s strategy

Romantic matchmaking · detailed questionnaires · algorithmic compatibility · per-university customization · monetize via licensing.

Facebook’s strategy

A specific community need · university connections · profiles & identity · social discovery · rapid growth & engagement.

Facebook chased network growth first and crossed the tipping point (virtuous cycle); Westmatch monetized early and never reached critical mass.

Two founders, two playbooks. Westmatch optimized the product and monetized via licensing but never hit critical mass. Facebook prioritized free, fast network growth and engagement, crossed the tipping point, and rode the virtuous cycle. The lesson: for a network good, growth past the tipping point beats early monetization.

Virtuous and Vicious Cycles — Real Cases

VICIOUS

Vicious: below the tipping point, users leave → value falls for the rest → collapse (Google+ → 2019 shutdown; MySpace, Friendster, Vine).

Virtuous: past the tipping point, scale compounds → more value → more users (Amazon Prime; ChatGPT & AI assistants).

VIRTUOUS

Same feedback loop — the sign flips at critical mass. Often winner-take-all.

The cycle made concrete, flanked by evidence. LEFT (vicious): Google+ and a graveyard of social networks that never crossed critical mass. RIGHT (virtuous): Amazon Prime's self-reinforcing scale and the explosive AI-assistant market led by ChatGPT. The curve in the middle is the mechanism: below the tipping point the network unravels; above it, network effects compound to equilibrium. Same loop, opposite sign, decided at critical mass.

Market Leaders Often Lose Dominance Quickly

  • Monopoly tendency: network goods often tip toward monopoly as users consolidate onto one platform.
  • But short-lived: even dominant firms like Friendster and MySpace declined fast once the network effect reversed. Have you even heard of them?
Network monopolies are real but fragile. The winner-take-most dynamic creates temporary monopolies (Netscape in browsers, MySpace/Friendster in social, Sega in consoles), but the same network effect that built them reverses the moment a better network appears, dominance evaporates fast. Today's dominant platform is one tipping point from the vicious cycle.

Misconception: “The Best Product Always Wins”

In network markets, the product that reaches the tipping point first often wins — even if a rival is technically better.

QWERTY (1870s)

Designed to slow typists down and stop jams — still dominates 150 years later. Pure lock-in.

Tesla NACS (2025)

Won the U.S. charging standard not on merit but on installed base: 7,300 stations, automakers with 75% of EVs adopted it.

Path dependence: history and timing, not just quality, decide the winner.

The canonical "wrong product wins" examples. QWERTY (David, 1985) is the textbook case of path dependence — an installed base of typists locked in a deliberately suboptimal layout. Tesla NACS is the fresh 2025 version: an installed base of chargers beat the "official" CCS standard. The lesson connects to tipping: being early and reaching critical mass can beat being better.

Section 2 · LO 19.2

Platforms & Two-Sided Markets

If the product is free, where does the money come from?

Platform vs. Pipeline

Platform — a business that creates value by matching two or more user groups, not by producing a good itself. An intermediary, not a maker.

Pipeline (traditional)

A carmaker buys steel → builds cars → sells them. Value flows one direction.

Platform (Uber)

Owns no cars, employs no drivers. It matches riders and drivers — scale with few staff.

Coase (Ch 9): firms exist to cut transaction costs. Platforms take that to the extreme — cut the cost of finding and trusting a match.

Pipeline vs platform is the mental model. The carmaker transforms inputs into output (Ch 9 production function). The platform produces nothing — it reduces the transaction cost of a match (Coase, Ch 9). That's why Uber/Airbnb scale globally with tiny headcounts and near-zero marginal cost per match. The asset is the network and the matching algorithm, not a factory.

Price Structure Beats Price Level

Rochet & Tirole (Tirole, 2014 Nobel): in a two-sided market, who pays matters more than how much.

Subsidize the price-sensitive side (free search, free maps), extract from the side that gets measurable value (advertisers).

Same logic: nightclub “ladies’ night,” credit-card rewards funded by 2–3% merchant fees.

The single most important platform-pricing idea. Google gives consumers search/Gmail/Maps free because consumers are price-sensitive and would flee a fee; advertisers pay because a targeted ad yields measurable sales. The "ladies' night" and credit-card examples make it vivid: subsidize the side that draws the other side. The optimum is an asymmetric price STRUCTURE, not equal prices.

The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

Riders won’t come without drivers; drivers won’t come without riders. A platform needs both sides at once — but each waits for the other.

Subsidize a side

Uber paid drivers guaranteed hourly rates before riders showed up.

Borrow a network

Threads launched off Instagram’s 2B users → 275M in months.

Start tiny

Airbnb began renting air mattresses in the founders’ apartment.

Solve the coordination problem and the cross-side flywheel takes over.

The chicken-and-egg (coordination) problem is the hardest part of launching a two-sided platform. Three classic solutions: (1) subsidize one side hard (Uber's driver guarantees); (2) leverage an existing network (Threads off Instagram — the fastest app to 100M ever); (3) bootstrap small and dense (Airbnb's air mattresses, then one city at a time). Once both sides reach critical mass, cross-side effects self-sustain.

Lock-In: Why You Don’t Leave

Sellers on Amazon

Review history & ranking don’t transfer. Running on two marketplaces (multi-homing costs) is rarely worth it — so they stay despite rising fees.

Consumers (psychological)

The iMessage “green bubble” — a trivial color cue — creates real social pressure to stay in Apple’s world.

Switching costs (Ch 12) are the glue that turns a network lead into durable market power.

Lock-in is what converts a transient network lead into lasting power. For sellers: non-portable reputation + multi-homing costs (the expense of maintaining presence on several platforms) effectively trap them. For consumers: switching costs are often psychological (green bubble) or data-based (your history, playlists, social graph). Connects back to Ch 12 switching costs and forward to the DOJ cases in Section 6.

Section 3 · LO 19.3

Platform Pricing & the Take Rate

The toll the platform charges on every match it makes.

The Take Rate: The Platform’s Cut

Take rate — the share of each transaction the platform keeps. Also: commission, rake.

Distinct from GMV (Gross Merchandise Volume = total value of transactions processed).

Amazon’s ~45% bundles commission + fulfillment + mandatory ads — far above its posted fee. Take rates rise once both sides are locked in.

The take rate is the platform's core revenue lever. Posted commission ≠ effective take rate: Apple's 30% is a clean number, but Amazon's effective ~45% bundles in FBA fees + advertising that sellers can't realistically avoid. The dynamic: as lock-in deepens, the platform can raise the take rate without losing the locked-in side — the FTC's central complaint against Amazon. GMV is the denominator; take rate × GMV = platform revenue.

Four Ways Platforms Price

Subsidize-and-extract

Free for one side, paid for the other. Google: consumers free, advertisers pay.

Freemium

Free basic tier builds the network & data; premium at $11.99 (Spotify, near-zero MC).

Peak-load (surge)

Higher prices at peak demand. Uber surge raised welfare +2.15% (Castillo 2025).

Intertemporal

$70 game now for the impatient → $30 later for the patient. Time-based price discrimination.

All four are variations on price discrimination (Ch 11) across sides, users, or time.

Four pricing patterns, all rooted in price discrimination (Ch 11). Subsidize-and-extract = the two-sided structure. Freemium = a version line where "free" also harvests data. Peak-load (surge) = allocating scarce supply to highest-value users; Castillo's Econometrica result shows it raises welfare, not just Uber's revenue. Intertemporal = sorting by patience (new-game $70→$30). Tie each back to the surplus-extraction logic of monopoly pricing.

Peak-Load (Surge) Pricing

Peak-load pricing: charge more when demand is highest — the firm segments the market by time. Higher Saturday-night vs matinee tickets, off-season Airbnb beach deals, ride-share surge at concerts.

Same capacity, demand-based price — Airbnb’s algorithm adds a seasonality + event factor ($300 → $550 on marathon weekend). Surge rations scarce supply to the highest-value users.

Peak-load pricing segments by time: with capacity fixed in the short run, raising price at peak demand (P_P, Q_P) above off-peak (P_0, Q_0) rations scarce supply to those who value it most and can raise welfare (Castillo 2025 on Uber surge). Airbnb's dynamic-pricing tool makes it concrete, layering a seasonality factor and an event factor (e.g., a marathon weekend) onto a base nightly rate. Same machinery as monopoly price discrimination across time.

The Streaming Wars Are Platform Economics

Subsidize-and-extract: Disney+ launched at $6.99 (below cost) to grab subscribers → raised to $18.99 by 2025.

Versioning: Netflix runs 3 tiers ($7.99 ads / $17.99 / $24.99) — the ad tier makes it a two-sided market too.

Bundling: Disney+/Hulu/ESPN+ and Comcast StreamSaver cut churn by packaging services.

Lock-in: Netflix +$1 to $18.99 with record-low 2.17% churn — your watchlist doesn’t transfer.

The oligopoly of Chapter 13 grew up into a set of locked-in content platforms.

Pull the streaming thread (running since Ch 4) into the platform frame. Content platforms have indirect network effects: more subscribers fund more content, more content attracts subscribers. Every pricing tool from this section appears: subsidize-and-extract (Disney+ launch price), versioning (Netflix tiers + the ad tier turning Netflix two-sided), bundling (StreamSaver), and lock-in (record-low churn despite price hikes = inelastic demand from your watchlist/recommendations).

Section 4 · LO 19.4

Competing in Network Markets

How do you build a new network — or defend an old one?

Teaser Strategies: Buy the Tipping Point

Teaser strategy — below-cost prices, freebies, or sign-up bonuses to attract early users and push past critical mass.

Google, 1998

Free, clean, no ads — built the user base, then monetized attention with text ads.

Credit cards

0% intro APR & cashback as the teaser — profitable on the 2–3% merchant fee regardless.

Verizon: 30 days free + a free phone + $300 to switchers — pay up front to capture the customer, recoup over years of lock-in.

Teasers are the launch-phase tool to climb the upward portion of the network demand curve and hit critical mass before rivals. Google (free search → ads) and Facebook (free social → ads) are the playbook. The economic logic: accept short-run losses because crossing the tipping point yields long-run network rents. Credit cards layer teaser pricing on the two-sided structure. Ride-hailing's early "free rides" were the same bet.

Lock-In: Defending the Castle

Lock-in — high switching costs keep users even when a better option exists. A moat and a source of market power.

Switch from iPhone to Android and you lose iMessage, iCloud photos, Apple Watch pairing, purchased apps. Each device you own raises the next switch’s cost.

The DOJ’s 2024 suit alleges Apple deliberately degrades cross-platform messaging to deter switching.

The fight: is the moat earned (a great product) or engineered (deliberate friction)? That line is where antitrust looks.

Three flavors of lock-in: tiered status · sunk-cost memberships · coalition rewards.

Lock-in is the defense phase — the complement to teasers. Apple's ecosystem is the canonical engineered moat: each additional device compounds the switching cost (network of complements). The DOJ 2024 complaint frames the green-bubble degradation as deliberate switching-cost inflation. Key normative point: lock-in is simultaneously a legitimate competitive advantage AND a potential antitrust harm — the line is whether it's earned or engineered.

Versioning: A Menu That Sorts Buyers

Versioning — offer tiers at different prices so buyers self-select by willingness to pay. (Second-degree price discrimination, Ch 11.)

ChatGPT’s ladder. Free → Plus ($20) → Pro ($200). The same model, gated into tiers, so heavy users self-select into the expensive plan.

The marginal cost of the premium tier is ≈$0 — the price gap is pure market segmentation, not cost.

Versioning = second-degree price discrimination (Ch 11 callback): the firm posts a menu and lets buyers reveal their type by choosing. Netflix tiers, Salesforce tiers, Tesla software-locked features. Because digital marginal cost ≈ 0, the price differences reflect WTP segmentation, not cost differences. Same self-selection machinery as the insurance screening menu in Ch 18 — here the goal is surplus extraction.

Bundling: Sell Them Together

A streaming firm sells two channels — a Movies pass and a Sports pass:

Their tastes diverge (each loves a different channel) — so one $20 bundle captures both. Bundling fails when tastes are aligned.

Bundling exploits negatively-correlated valuations: Aisha and Kaito disagree on which item is worth more, but both value the pair at $20, so a $20 bundle captures everyone. Separate pricing forces a worse trade-off (charge high and lose a buyer, or charge low and leave surplus). The exit-ticket / P&A Q5 has a variant. Caveat for the misconception slide: if both buyers valued A high and B low (positively correlated), bundling adds nothing.

You Try — Bundling Problem

Maya values game X at $70, game Y at $30.  Ben values game X at $30, game Y at $65. Marginal cost = $0. The firm can sell X and Y separately (single price each) or as a bundle. (No price discrimination.)

Selling separately

  1. Most the firm can earn on game X = $____
  2. Who buys game X?
  3. Most the firm can earn on game Y = $____
  4. Who buys game Y?
  5. Most total profit selling separately = $____

Bundling the two games

  1. The firm can sell the bundle for $____
  2. How much total profit does the firm earn?
  3. How much additional profit from bundling?
Work it live. Separately: X sells best at $30 (both buy) = $60, or $70 (only Maya) = $70 -> best $70, only Maya buys. Y: $30 (both) = $60, or $65 (only Ben) = $65 -> best $65, only Ben. Separate total = 70 + 65 = $135... but each consumer only buys their high game, so the firm picks the single price per game that maximizes revenue: X at $70 (Maya) and Y at $65 (Ben) = $135. Bundle: Maya values the pair at $100 (70+30), Ben at $95 (30+65) -> set bundle at $95, both buy = $190. Additional profit from bundling = 190 - 135 = $55. (Adjust to your exact rounding; the point is bundling beats separate when tastes diverge.) Names kept short and easy to say.

A platform that controls one layer (the OS) can leverage it into an adjacent market (the browser) via bundling. DOJ sued 1998; 2001 settlement forced API-sharing.

Exactly the concern driving today’s cases against Google and Apple — and the EU’s interoperability rules.

The recurring pattern of network markets: control of one layer of the stack (Windows) leveraged into a neighboring market (browsers) through bundling/tying. Microsoft drove IE from ~0 to 90%+ and the court found illegal maintenance of the OS monopoly. The 2001 behavioral remedy (API-sharing, oversight) is the direct precedent for the Google remedies 25 years later (Section 6). It's why the DMA mandates interoperability ex ante.

Section 5 · LO 19.5

Data as an Economic Input

When the service is free, you — your data — are the product.

The Data Flywheel

Data flywheel — more users → more data → better algorithms → more users. A self-reinforcing loop.

Meta, Q2 2025: advertiser net-dollar retention hit 130% (the average advertiser spent 30% more) as ML targeting improved on more data.

~$170B in 2025 ad revenue — nearly all from monetizing user data.

The data flywheel is the network effect's cousin: instead of users valuing other users directly, the platform converts user data into a better product that attracts more users. Meta's 130% NDR is the flywheel in a number — advertisers pay more because targeting keeps improving. The flywheel creates economies of scale in DATA that act as a barrier to entry — the "data moat."

Data Moats — and Their Limits

Economies of scale

Building a model costs a fortune; processing one more user’s data costs almost nothing. Favors incumbents — a data moat.

Diminishing returns

The 10-millionth search query improves Google far less than the 10-thousandth. Past some scale, the moat thins.

Data moat — proprietary data that improves the product and raises entry barriers. Powerful, but not infinite.

Two opposing forces. Economies of scale in data (Ch 9) entrench incumbents: huge fixed cost to build a model, ~0 marginal cost per extra user's data. But data also has diminishing returns — once you have enough, more adds little — which is why some economists argue moats are thinner than they look (open-source data, synthetic data, transferable talent erode them). This tension is exactly what the Google remedy is testing.

Data Becomes a Commodity

As AI models hunger for training data, communities discover they’re sitting on a goldmine:

Reddit

$203M cumulative licensing — ~$60M/yr from Google, ~$70M/yr from OpenAI for 17 years of human posts.

Stack Overflow

Traffic fell 76% as AI assistants replaced it — so it pivoted to licensing its verified knowledge base.

The communities built deep data moats — now among the most valuable inputs of the AI era.

Data is now a tradeable factor of production. Reddit's IPO disclosed $203M in licensing — its 17 years of human conversation is exactly what frontier models need. Stack Overflow's story is the cautionary flip: AI cannibalized its traffic (−76%), so it monetized the asset directly. Raises the P&A question: is the data rival or non-rival? (Non-rival — licensing to many doesn't use it up — which is why exclusive deals command premiums.) And the ethics: the users who created it aren't paid.

Privacy as a Cost — & the New Antitrust

Privacy is a real cost

Personalized service vs. surveillance. FTC (2024–25): 250+ firms used surveillance pricing. Users underweight the long-run cost of data extraction.

Economist at Work — Lina Khan

2017 Yale paper: low prices can hide market power. As FTC chair (2021), she sued Amazon & won a $2.5B Prime settlement.

A 2025 Cowles paper shows a monopolist platform systematically distorts privacy — the case for “privacy by default.”

Two ideas. (1) Privacy is an economic cost users bear and underprice — surveillance pricing (250+ firms) personalizes prices to your data; the Cowles 2025 result proves a monopolist distorts privacy allocations absent a default, motivating "privacy by default" (the opt-out logic from Ch 18 nudges). (2) Lina Khan's "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" reframed antitrust: the consumer-welfare standard (low prices = fine) misses platform power. Her FTC won the $2.5B Prime "Iliad Flow" settlement — the Ch 18 hook, now in context.

Section 6 · LO 19.6

Regulating the Digital Economy

How do you regulate a monopolist that gives its product away free?

The New Antitrust: US v. Google

Aug 2024 — the finding

Google is an illegal monopolist: 90% of search held by an “illegal web of exclusionary contracts” — $26B+ to Apple alone to stay the default.

Sep 2025 — the remedy

No more exclusive defaults; share the search index & click data with rivals; rules extend to the Gemini AI app.

The court moved preemptively into AI — stopping a search monopoly from becoming an AI monopoly. A second 2025 ruling found Google also monopolized ad-tech.

The defining case of platform antitrust. The harm wasn't high prices (search is free) but foreclosure: paying $26B+/yr for default placement starved rivals of the queries that train search. The remedy is behavioral, not structural — and crucially attacks the DATA MOAT directly (forced data-sharing), testing whether that restores competition. The court's extension to Gemini shows regulators learned the browser-wars lesson: stop leverage into the next paradigm early.

The European Approach: DMA & DSA

Ex ante regulation: the DMA (2024) names big platforms “gatekeepers” and sets rules before harm — not after a court case.

Apple forced to allow third-party app stores & alt payments. April 2025 fines: Apple €500M, Meta €200M. Cumulative EU tech fines > €6B.

US = ex post litigation (slow, case-by-case). EU = ex ante rules (fast, but risks over-regulating).

The core US-vs-EU contrast (Review Q6). EU's DMA is ex ante: pre-set obligations on designated gatekeepers, predictable and fast, but risks banning productive conduct. US is ex post: litigate proven harms, flexible but glacial (Microsoft, Google took years) and only acts after damage. Apple's malicious compliance (the Core Technology Commission that re-extracts revenue) shows the cat-and-mouse nature of ex ante rules.

History Rhymes: AT&T & Microsoft

AT&T (1984)

Controlled ~80% of U.S. landlines. Structural breakup + mandated interconnection → competition, lower prices, long-distance vanished as a market.

Microsoft (1998–2001)

Leveraged Windows to crush Netscape. Behavioral remedy: share APIs, accept oversight — the model for the Google remedy today.

Essential facility / interconnection: force the dominant firm to grant rivals access to shared infrastructure.

Two precedents bracket the remedy spectrum. AT&T = STRUCTURAL (break it up) + interconnection — proof that breakup can work even for network industries; but telecom was physical wires with clear geography. Microsoft = BEHAVIORAL (API-sharing, oversight) — the direct template for Google's data-sharing remedy. The hard question (Review Q7): digital "infrastructure" is data and algorithms that replicate globally overnight, so defining the market and the remedy is far harder than splitting phone regions.

Should Network Goods Be Regulated?

Government aims at low prices, choice, and quality — without hindering productivity. Monopolies still face potential competition; one way to promote it is preventing exclusive access through interconnection.

Essential facilities

An input needed to produce or consume the product — the gateways developers must use to reach users.

Interconnection

Physical linking of networks to essential facilities — prevents a dominant firm from blocking competitors.

The pro-competition lever for network goods isn't always breakup — it's denying exclusive control of an essential facility. App stores (Google Play, Apple App Store) are gateways developers can't avoid; interconnection forces the dominant network to let rivals plug in (the AT&T precedent). The DMA's interoperability mandates are the modern version. Frame the trade-off for the dilemma slide ahead.

Standards and Compatibility

  • An industry standard — a common format shared by all competitors (e.g., USB plugs) — eases compatibility and use.
  • Firms fight standards wars to become the standard and “own” the network — often via heavy marketing, not productivity.
  • Network compatibility lets rival standards coexist — e.g., editing the same spreadsheet across Windows & Mac via Google Docs and Excel.
Standards are network coordination devices. A shared standard (USB) maximizes the network by making everything compatible; but the prize of owning the standard triggers costly standards wars (Blu-ray vs HD-DVD, Lightning vs USB-C, DirectX vs alternatives) where firms burn resources on marketing rather than products. Compatibility/translation layers (Google Docs reading .xlsx) let standards coexist and blunt lock-in — a market-based alternative to mandated interconnection.

Apple in the Antitrust Spotlight

DOJ, 2024. Alleges Apple maintains a smartphone monopoly by engineering switching costs — degrading cross-platform messaging, super-apps, smartwatches, and cloud streaming.

EU DMA. Forces the App Store open — sideloading, third-party payments, and interoperability — treating it as an essential facility.

The core question: is the ecosystem a product people love, or a moat that blocks rivals? The remedy is interconnection, not breakup.

Apple is the live test of the interconnection vs breakup debate. The DOJ 2024 complaint frames Apple's ecosystem advantages as deliberately engineered switching costs (green bubbles, watch lock-in, blocked super-apps/cloud gaming). The EU DMA goes further ex ante, mandating sideloading and third-party payment as if the App Store were an essential facility. The normative line is whether lock-in is earned (a great product) or engineered (an anticompetitive moat) — the same question as the browser wars, 25 years on.

The Regulation Dilemma

Every fix has a cost: break up a platform and you may destroy the network value; force data-sharing and you may erode privacy; cap prices and you get shortages (Ch 7).

Markets self-correct? MySpace, Friendster, Netscape all fell. Contestability + new entry may discipline platforms.

Or too slow? Network effects + switching costs + data moats may make self-correction too rare to wait for.

Misconception: “Big tech are natural monopolies — just break them up.” Network effects create winner-take-all tendencies, not inevitability.

The honest closing tension — this is a genuine trade-off, not a slogan. Poorly designed intervention can do more harm than the failure it targets (the California 2000-01 electricity price caps → blackouts, Ch 7). The empirical question: do contestability and the threat of entry (MySpace/Friendster/Netscape all died) discipline platforms, or do network effects + switching costs + data moats make self-correction too slow? Bridges to Ch 17's institutions. No clean answer — that's the point.

Can Poor Regulation Be Worse Than None?

Regulation aims to stop the exploitation of essential facilities — but a clumsy fix can cost more than the problem. Regulatory cost example: wireless-contract exclusivity with phone makers limits consumer choice.

Disaster case — California electricity crisis (2000–01). The state’s price cap acted as a price ceiling (Ch 7).

Shortages. Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied — the rolling blackouts.

Misallocation & search costs. Resources go to the wrong uses; consumers waste time chasing the scarce good.

Quality decay. With incentives capped, the controlled product deteriorates over time.

Poorly designed rules can inflict greater harm than the failure they target — deadweight loss + shortages. Weigh benefits vs costs; sometimes no regulation is better. Can a network be efficient and secure without regulation?

The cautionary counterweight to the regulation slides. Regulation has real costs (exclusivity contracts limiting choice), and badly designed regulation can be catastrophic: California's 2000-01 retail price cap functioned as a price ceiling, producing the textbook trio — shortages (blackouts), misallocation + search costs, and quality deterioration — plus large deadweight loss (Ch 7). The takeaway is not anti-regulation but cost-benefit humility: evaluate carefully, and accept that for some network goods the least-bad option is light or no regulation. Ends on the open question to set up the debate.

Appendix: A Network Without a Center — Blockchain

Blockchain — a decentralized, distributed ledger. Instead of one company’s server, the data lives on thousands of nodes worldwide, each holding a cross-checked copy. A network good with no owner.

Security by decentralization. Every node cross-references the ledger, so altering it means hacking thousands of copies at once.

Uses. Crypto (Bitcoin) today; tracking supply chains, health records, and secure elections next.

Self-governed. Rules are set by users, not government — raising money-laundering & illegal-use concerns.

Governments can ban certain uses, but cannot regulate the blockchain itself. Networks now power communication, remote work & study, and global commerce — and they’ll keep raising living standards.

The forward-looking coda: a network good can run with no central authority at all. A blockchain replaces the single-server database with a distributed ledger replicated across thousands of nodes; consensus + cross-referencing make tampering require attacking the whole network at once, which is the security property. Beyond Bitcoin, the applications are supply-chain provenance, health records, and tamper-evident voting. The economic twist: it is largely self-regulated (rules by users), which both removes a central point of failure and creates enforcement gaps (laundering, illicit use) governments can only address at the use layer. Close on the chapter's theme — networks are now infrastructure for modern life and will keep improving living standards.

Appendix: The Economics of AI Products

Extreme fixed cost: GPT-4 ~$78M, Gemini ~$191M, Anthropic $12B+ in cumulative compute.

Near-zero marginal cost: inference fell 280× (Nov 2022 → Oct 2024). Train once, serve forever.

High barrier + infinite scale → natural oligopoly. But open-source (Llama, Gemma) is a public good (Ch 17) that erodes the moat.

AI is the chapter's cost structure taken to the extreme (Ch 9): colossal fixed cost to train, ~0 marginal cost to serve (inference down 280×). That LRAC shape (falling forever) suggests natural oligopoly — only the deep-pocketed compete. The open question (P&A Q10): does AI tip like search, or stay differentiated like streaming? Open-source models (Llama, Gemma) are non-rival, non-excludable public goods (Ch 17) that could keep the market from tipping. A fitting capstone — every chapter's tools in one market.

4. Teasers, lock-in, versioning & bundling build and defend network positions.

5. Data flywheels are self-reinforcing moats — powerful, but with diminishing returns.

6. Regulating platforms means balancing network value against unchecked power — no clean answer.
