Section 1  ·  LO 7.1a

Price Ceilings

When the government says “not above this”

The Cobra Effect — CFPB Late-Fee Cap (2024)

2024 cap: late fees $32 → $8 for the largest issuers. Vacated April 2025; Senate bills tried to codify it in 2026.

The Cobra Effect

A policy designed to help consumers ends up hurting the same people.

  • Weaker deterrence → more late payments, worse credit scores.
  • Cost-shifting → higher APRs & fewer rewards. On-time payers subsidize.
  • Tighter lending → subprime denials, lower limits.
  • Higher minimum payments → cash-flow strain on stretched households.

Standard binding ceiling: shortage + DWL.

“Cobra Effect” story: British colonial India offered a bounty for dead cobras; people bred cobras for the bounty, and when the program was canceled the bred cobras were released — ending with MORE cobras than before. The frame applies to the CFPB cap: a rule meant to protect consumers ends up raising their borrowing costs and shrinking their credit access. The four bullets ARE the banking industry's economic argument from the 2025 lawsuit. Same diagram, same logic as rent control; the huge DWL triangle visually shows how far below equilibrium $8 sits.

Price Ceiling

Price ceiling: a legal maximum price.

Below equilibrium

BINDING — forces price down → creates a shortage (Qd > Qs)

Above equilibrium

NON-BINDING — market clears at E as if the ceiling weren’t there

Misconception: “Any ceiling affects the market.” A $200 ceiling on something selling for $150 is like a 200 mph speed limit — in place, but nobody notices.

Hammer the binding/non-binding distinction now — it returns for floors, tariffs, and quotas. The 200 mph analogy lands well.

NYC Rent Control — A Shortage in Five Forms

When a binding ceiling stops the price from rationing, something else has to.

~1 million rent-stabilized NYC apartments; 2024 increase cap 2.75% vs 3.8% inflation. Vacancy rate (2023): 1.41% citywide, 0.98% rent-stabilized — lowest since 1968. The shortage resurfaces as five other costs:

Shortage

Qd > Qs. Tenants stay put for decades; new arrivals can’t find a unit.

Non-price rationing

Connections, lotteries, “know-someone” waitlists replace price.

Black markets

“Key money,” illegal sublets — resale above the legal cap.

Quality erosion

Deferred maintenance: landlords can’t recoup repair costs via rent.

Supply shrinks

Condo conversions, withdrawals; NYC permits −4.8% in 2024.

The shortage never disappears — it just stops showing up as a high price and starts showing up as a long waitlist, key money, or a deteriorating apartment.

Make the abstraction concrete. The diagram (next slide) shows Q_d > Q_s; this slide shows what that gap LOOKS like in New York. Reveal the five cards one at a time. The closing callout reinforces the core principle: price is a rationing device; cap it and rationing doesn’t stop, it just moves to uglier mechanisms.

Price Ceiling on the Diagram

Eq: 10K units at $2,500. With ceiling $2,000: only 7.5K offered, but 13.3K demanded → shortage of ~5.8K units, and a small DWL triangle.

Walk through the diagram once. Key move: the ceiling is HORIZONTAL at $2,000. Trace it to supply → that's the quantity traded (supply rations). Trace to demand → that's how many people WANT to rent at $2,000. The gap is the shortage. The DWL triangle is the 2,500 lost units between Q_s and Q_eq.

Welfare Effects: Transfer vs. Destruction

Two very different things happen:

1. Transfer

7,000 tenants who keep their apartments pay $500/month less. That surplus moves from landlords to tenants.

Not destroyed — just redistributed.

2. Destruction (DWL)

3,000 apartments disappear from the market. The surplus those trades would have created is gone — not transferred to anyone.

Plus: misallocation — remaining units don’t go to who values them most.

Political confusion: tenants celebrating low rent are celebrating a transfer. The real efficiency cost is the apartments that no longer exist.

This is the conceptual centerpiece for Sections 1-4. Every wedge has a transfer piece (cancels out) and a destruction piece (DWL). Make sure students separate them.

Poll — Hurricane Gasoline

A hurricane spikes gasoline prices from $3 → $8/gallon. The state imposes a price ceiling of $3.50. Which is the biggest consequence?

A. Everyone gets cheap gas — great policy

B. Stations sell out fast — first-come, first-served

C. Supply from outside the area doesn’t arrive

D. Both B and C — shortage and blocked supply response

Click your answer. 60 seconds.

Expected: D. The deeper insight is C — the price signal is what tells truckers in Atlanta to drive water/gas to Tampa. Ceilings shut off that signal. Use this to motivate the alternative-policy discussion (vouchers, FEMA distribution).

Poll Results

Answer: D. Shortages and blocked supply response. The price would have signaled trucks in Atlanta to drive gas to Tampa. The ceiling silences that signal — so the shortage is worse and lasts longer.

Better policy: vouchers for low-income drivers + let prices rise. Preserves the signal, protects the vulnerable.

Reveal the bigger lesson: ceilings don't just create shortages, they also prevent the market's normal self-correction. This sets up the "alternative policy design" arc that returns in Sections 5 and 8.

Insulin Cap — Elasticity Decides the DWL

  Real-world correction

The first-cut math predicted ~$1 B/yr DWL. The actual market reaction is far smaller. Why?

  • Demand: diabetics need insulin to live — nearly perfectly inelastic.
  • Supply: only 3 makers (Lilly, Novo, Sanofi) — capacity highly inelastic.
  • Quantity barely moves; the cap is mostly a transfer from firms to patients.

Inelastic S & D ⇒ tiny DWL, huge welfare redistribution.

Compare to rent control: elastic supply → the same kind of cap creates a large shortage. Same diagram, very different DWL.

Insulin is the textbook case for inelastic-on-both-sides. Demand: a diabetic without insulin dies in days — no substitute. Supply: only three firms worldwide make it (Lilly, Novo, Sanofi). A price cap mostly REDISTRIBUTES surplus from manufacturers to patients with only a small deadweight-loss triangle. Compare to rent control 4 slides ago: elastic supply, so the same ceiling created a large shortage. Same diagram, opposite welfare conclusion — the punchline is “elasticity decides the DWL.” This slide previews tax-incidence (Section 3) where the same elasticity rule reappears.

Price Floor

Price floor: a legal minimum price. Binding when set above equilibrium → creates a surplus.

The most-debated price floor in America: the minimum wage.

  • Federal floor: $7.25/hr since 2009 — 17 years frozen.
  • State variation: WA $17.13; AL/LA/MS/SC/TN: no state minimum.

Simple model prediction

If equilibrium wage = $10 and floor = $15:
more want to work, fewer firms hire.
The gap = unemployment.

Mirror image of ceiling. Floor binds when ABOVE equilibrium — surplus instead of shortage. The minimum-wage example is the heart of empirical micro — brace for the nuance ahead.

Card & Krueger: A Phone Call That Won a Nobel

1994 Natural Experiment

NJ raised minimum wage $4.25 → $5.05. PA didn’t. Card & Krueger called 410 fast-food restaurants before and after.

Finding: no employment decline.

Why? Several possible mechanisms.

C&K were cautious. Leading hypotheses from later literature (Manning 2003; Cengiz et al. 2019; Azar–Marinescu–Steinbaum 2020):

  • Monopsony — concentrated employers were paying below competitive; min wage moves it toward competitive (preview, Ch 14).
  • Firms absorb cost via lower profits or higher menu prices.
  • Reduced turnover lowers training costs.

2021 Nobel Prize in Economics: Card shared it for inventing the "natural experiment" approach that now dominates empirical economics. Cengiz et al. (2019) replicated across 138 increases — same conclusion.

This is the most counter-intuitive finding in the chapter for many students. Stress: Card did NOT disprove S&D. He showed the COMPETITIVE labor market model doesn't fit fast-food — monopsony does. The empirical method matters as much as the result.

Minimum Wage Policy

  Case Study

Raise the minimum wage from $10 → $15.

  • Labor supply: workers can't suddenly opt out — inelastic.
  • Labor demand: short-run firm staffing is hard to change — also inelastic.
  • Quantity barely moves: Q changes only from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_2\).

Inelastic S & D ⇒ modest employment loss, big wage gain for those still employed.

The minimum-wage policy debate hinges on elasticities. If both supply AND demand are inelastic, raising the wage from $10 to $15 produces only a tiny employment change — mostly a transfer from employers to workers, with small deadweight loss. This is the empirical view from Card & Krueger forward: short-run labor demand for low-wage work appears highly inelastic. Compare to the linear textbook diagram (page 20): same $10 → $15 cap, but with elastic curves it gave 200K unemployment and $500K DWL. With realistic elasticities, both numbers shrink dramatically.

California’s $20 Fast-Food Wage

AB 1228 (April 1, 2024)

Chains with 60+ locations: $16 → $20/hr — a 25% jump.

One of the largest sectoral wage hikes in US history.

Clemens-Edwards-Meer (NBER)

  • Employment: −2.7–3.2% (~18,000 jobs)
  • Menu prices: +3.3–3.6%
  • 98% of operators raised prices

Lesson: moderate floors may not cut jobs (monopsony slack); large floors visibly do. ~500K kept jobs got a big raise — an equity vs. efficiency call we’ll settle in §7.5.

Key teaching arc: small minimum wage hikes don't show big disemployment (Card-Krueger). Big ones do (CA $20). That's not a contradiction — it's the nonlinearity that emerges when you push past the monopsony slack and into the competitive zone. Both findings can be true at different scales.

Sugar: A Price Floor That Costs You at the Candy Aisle

US has supported sugar prices since the 1930s. Domestic sugar: ~$0.42/lb; world price: ~$0.20/lb.

Mechanism: price supports + import quotas (more in §7.5).

Winners: a few thousand domestic sugar growers.
Losers: 330M consumers, candy makers, bakers, and the sugar refining workforce that lost jobs to Mexico.

Hershey’s → Mexico. Brach’s → Mexico. Life Savers → Canada.

A textbook example of how concentrated benefits + diffuse costs — the "logic of collective action" — keeps an inefficient policy alive for 90 years.

Concentrated benefits (a few growers in FL/LA) plus diffuse costs ($10/year per family) explain why bad policies survive. Echo this when we hit tariffs and political economy in §7.5.

Federal minimum has been $7.25 since 2009. Prices rose ~40%. The real wage fell about 30% without a single political vote.

Should the minimum wage be indexed to inflation, like Social Security benefits?

Arguments FOR indexing

Section 3  ·  LO 7.2

Tax Incidence

Who actually pays?

The Equivalence Principle

The person who writes the check is not necessarily the person who bears the burden.

$1 tax on the BUYER

Buyer pays $X + $1 at the pump. The market shifts demand down.

$1 tax on the SELLER

Seller sends $1 per gallon to the government. The market shifts supply up.

Identical outcome. Same wedge, same buyer-price, same seller-price, same Q. The economic burden is set by elasticities, not by legislation.

This is the most important slide in the chapter for many students. Tax laws have to put the tax on SOMEONE — but the economics doesn't care. Tell them this is why "tax cuts for the rich" rhetoric is sometimes wrong: the corporate income tax is split between workers, consumers, and shareholders depending on elasticities.

The Buyer-Share Formula

$$\text{Buyer’s share of tax} \;=\; \frac{E_s}{E_s \,+\, |E_d|}$$

The more inelastic side bears the larger share — it can’t walk away.

Cigarettes: Es = 2.0, |Ed| = 0.5

Buyer share = 2.0 / 2.5 = 80%

Luxury yachts: Es = 0.5, |Ed| = 2.0

Buyer share = 0.5 / 2.5 = 20%

1990 luxury tax killed the US boat-building industry. Congress repealed it in 1993.

Drill the formula: numerator is the OTHER side's elasticity. Common mistake is flipping it. Use the symmetry: if both elasticities are equal, 50/50 split. As supply becomes infinitely elastic, buyer pays 100%. As demand becomes infinitely elastic, seller pays 100%.

Two Markets, Two Burden Splits

Same $1.50 tax, very different burdens. The shape of the curves — not the legislation — sets who pays.

Make the visual point: in the left panel, the blue rectangle (buyer burden) is large because demand is steep (inelastic). In the right panel, the red rectangle is large because supply is steep (inelastic). The dashed line is the supply-with-tax curve; the wedge between the original supply and the dashed line is the per-unit tax.

Philadelphia Soda Tax (2017)

The tax

1.5¢/oz on sweetened beverages. Statutorily on distributors.

  • Sugary drink sales: −33% citywide
  • Lower-income households: −50%
  • Child dental decay: −22–34%

Incidence in practice

Most of the distributor tax was passed through to consumers in shelf prices.

Cross-border shopping surged as elasticity rose (suburbs untaxed).

Revenue: $75M (2022) → $73M (2023).

Equity worry: the heaviest burden fell on lower-income consumers with fewer shopping alternatives.

Two lessons: (1) statutory incidence (distributor) ≠ economic incidence (consumer). (2) Geographic substitutes matter — demand became more elastic over time as people drove to the suburbs, so producers had to absorb more of the tax later.

The FICA Payroll Tax — $1.2 Trillion in Incidence

15.3% payroll tax (Social Security + Medicare). By law: 7.65% on employer, 7.65% on employee. Congress called it “equal sharing.”

What the elasticities say

Labor supply is highly inelastic (most people work at most wages). Labor demand is more elastic.

Actual burden

Workers bear roughly all 15.3%. The “employer’s half” shows up as wages that never rose.

Largest applied lesson in tax incidence. The legal split is theater — elasticities run the show.

This usually surprises students. Ask: "Have you ever had a job where the boss said 'sorry, your wage is lower this year because we have to pay the employer FICA share'?" Probably not — because the wage was just lower from the start. The pass-through to workers is invisible because it happened before the offer letter.

Behavioral Twist — Tax Salience

Chetty et al. (2009): grocery store field experiment.

Control

Sticker price only. Tax added at register.

Treatment

Tax-inclusive price on the shelf.

Demand fell 8% more when the tax was visible on the shelf.

Statutory incidence can matter — when consumers don’t fully process taxes added later.

Important caveat to the equivalence principle. Behavior matters when consumers can't or don't compute the tax. Hidden taxes (sales tax at register) get under-responded-to; visible taxes (gasoline excise baked into the pump price) hit harder. Connect this to Ch 18 behavioral economics.

Poll — Who Bears the Carbon Tax?

A $50/ton carbon tax raises gasoline prices. Demand for gasoline is inelastic (Ed ≈ 0.3) in the short run. Supply is elastic (Es ≈ 1.5).

Who bears most of the burden?

A. Refineries — they pay the tax legally

B. Consumers — demand is inelastic

C. Split 50/50 by law

D. Foreign oil exporters absorb it

Click your answer. 60 seconds.

Expected: B. Using the formula: buyer share = E_s/(E_s+|E_d|) = 1.5/1.8 = 83%. The inelastic side (consumers) bears most.

Poll Results

Answer: B. Buyer share = Es / (Es + |Ed|) = 1.5 / 1.8 = 83%. Consumers eat the tax.

Reveal: this is exactly why distributional design matters. Canada's Climate Action Incentive turns a regressive carbon tax into a progressive transfer by rebating ~90% of revenue per capita. Hold this thread; it returns in Ch 16.

Section 4  ·  LO 7.3

Deadweight Loss of Taxation

The efficiency price of raising revenue

DWL Logic — Malik & Elena

Before the tax

Malik values cleaning at $120. Elena’s cost is $80. They agree on $100. Surplus: $20 + $20 = $40.

After a $50 tax

Malik would have to pay $150 for a service he values at $120. Trade doesn’t happen.

Revenue: $0. DWL: $40.

DWL = the surplus from trades that no longer happen. Not transferred. Just gone.

The Malik-Elena example is the cleanest possible illustration. The whole $40 of surplus evaporates because a $50 tax wedge exceeds the $40 gain from trade. Generalize: every tax kills the marginal trades where buyer's value just exceeds seller's cost.

DWL Grows With the Square of the Tax

Policy rule: tax many goods a little, not one good a lot. A 5% tax on ten goods << a 50% tax on one good.

Feldstein (1999): the US income tax destroys ~30¢ for every $1 raised — about 12x Harberger’s 1954 estimate, because behavior responds.

Crucial intuition: DWL is a TRIANGLE. Base = tax size, height = quantity change which is itself ~ tax. So DWL ∝ tax². Doubling the tax quadruples DWL. Connect to Laffer logic on the next slide.

The Laffer Curve

Tax rate 0% → revenue $0. Tax rate 100% → nobody works → revenue $0. Somewhere in between is the rate that maximizes revenue.

Useful framing, but it’s a hypothesis, not a prescription. The empirical question is: where on the curve are we?

A 2012 survey of top economists: 96% disagreed that a US income-tax cut would raise tax revenue. We’re on the left side.

NY cigarette tax: $5.35/pack. Missouri: $0.17. Smuggling >$1B/year — classic Laffer at work for a single state.

Laffer is great pedagogy and bad headline. Yes there exists a revenue-maximizing rate. No, the US isn't above it. Most evidence places the optimum income-tax rate at 50-70% for top earners; the federal top rate is well below.

Section 5  ·  LO 7.4

Subsidies, Tariffs, and Quotas

Wedges that go the other way — and across borders

Subsidies — The Mirror Image of a Tax

A subsidy drives the same wedge as a tax — but the gov pays instead of collects.

Buyers pay less, sellers receive more, and Q rises above the efficient level. The DWL is from over-production.

Subsidies look generous but they create the same kind of efficiency loss as taxes — just from too many trades instead of too few. The "DWL from overproduction" idea returns in §7.5 when we discuss whether it's actually a loss when externalities are at play.

EV Tax Credits — The Inframarginal Problem

The policy

IRA: up to $7,500 per new EV. Sales +50% in 2023. First three months of point-of-sale: ~$600M consumer savings.

Stanford/SIEPR finding

75% of recipients would have bought an EV anyway. Only 25% are marginal — behavior changed.

Cost per behavioral change: ~$30K per additional EV (vs. $7.5K sticker subsidy). Most of the budget went to people who didn’t need pushing.

This is the classic subsidy design problem: you can't tell who's inframarginal vs. marginal in advance, so you pay everyone. Cleaner targeting (income caps, trade-in requirements, used-EV credits) helps but doesn't solve it.

Tariffs on Imports

$200 tariff → domestic price rises to $1,000. Producers gain. Government collects revenue. Consumers lose more than that — 2 DWL triangles.

Walk the diagram: free trade Q_s = 60, Q_d = 100, imports = 40. With tariff: Q_s = 75, Q_d = 90, imports = 15. The green rectangle (gov revenue) is gain to the country. The TWO gray triangles are loss to the country. Net loss = $4B (Problem 6 numbers).

“Liberation Day” Tariffs (April 2025)

Announced April 2, 2025

  • 10% baseline on nearly all countries
  • China: 34% → later 125%
  • EU: 20%, Vietnam: 46%
  • April 9: most paused to 10% for 90 days

Yale Budget Lab

Consumer prices: +3.0%. Average household cost: ~$4,900/year.

Shoes +87%, apparel +65%.

Regressivity (Nov 2025 distributional analysis): bottom decile pays 2.4% of income; top decile, 0.8%. A 3:1 ratio.

Amiti-Redding-Weinstein (NY Fed) found nearly 100% pass-through of the 2018 tariffs to US importers/consumers. Foreign exporters didn't absorb. So "foreign countries pay" is empirically false. The "reciprocal" formula (trade deficit/imports/2) has no economic justification — arithmetic, not economics.

Tariffs vs. Quotas — Same Wedge, Different Beneficiary

TARIFF

  •   Raises domestic price
  •   Domestic producers gain
  •   Government collects revenue
  •   2 DWL triangles

QUOTA

  •   Raises domestic price
  •   Domestic producers gain
  •   No gov revenue — "quota rents" to license holders
  •   2 DWL triangles

If foreign governments allocate licenses, U.S. consumers lose twice — once at the cash register, once when rents leave the country.

This sets up the cap-and-trade analogue in Ch 16. Auctioned permits behave like tariffs (revenue to gov); free permits behave like quotas (rents to permit holders). Same design choice; same distributional consequence.

Policy Wedge Winners Losers Gov $ Source of DWL

Same diagram, six policies. The DWL grows with elasticity in every case.

Section 6  ·  LO 7.5

Efficiency vs. Equity

When is the leaky bucket worth carrying?

Okun’s Leaky Bucket

Every intervention fails an efficiency test: each creates DWL. Does that mean “never intervene”? No.

Arthur Okun: transferring money from rich to poor is like carrying water in a leaky bucket. The bucket leaks — but the water that arrives may still be worth the trip.

Minimum wage

Dube: poverty elasticity −0.22 to −0.46. 10% increase → 2–5% less poverty.

Tariffs (2025)

3:1 regressivity ratio + DWL. A particularly expensive redistribution tool.

Drug price caps

Billions transferred to patients; CBO: ~13 fewer drugs over 30 years.

Three different leaky buckets. Different answers depending on how much you weight equity vs. efficiency.

This is where the chapter pivots from positive economics ("what happens") to normative ("what should we do"). Economists can quantify the DWL. Whether the leakage is worth tolerating depends on social weights — that's a political call.

The Benefit Cliff — Where EMTR Exceeds 100%

A worker’s raise

$25,000 → $28,000 (a $3,000 raise).

  • SNAP: −$1,500
  • EITC: −$600
  • Medicaid: −$4,000

Total loss = $6,100 on a $3,000 raise.

Effective marginal tax rate

EMTR ≈ 203%

The worker is worse off after the raise. Federal statutory rate at this income: 10–12%.

Welfare design failure. Programs phase out independently, stacking penalties on top of each other. The market signal “work more” is overridden by program design.

This is the equity-efficiency story made concrete. Each program (EITC, SNAP, Medicaid) is individually well-intentioned. Stack them and they punish work at the very income range where work-incentives matter most. Reform proposals: smoother phase-outs, integrated benefit cliff calculators (mathematica.org), basic income.

Key Takeaways

1. Every intervention is a wedge. Six policies, one diagram — price ceiling, floor, tax, subsidy, tariff, quota.

2. Statutory ≠ economic incidence. The check writer is not the burden bearer.

3. The inelastic side pays. Same rule for taxes, tariffs, and subsidies.

4. DWL grows with elasticity AND with tax2. Doubling the tax quadruples the loss.

5. Equity vs. efficiency is a value judgment. Economists quantify the leakage; citizens decide if the water is worth the trip.
