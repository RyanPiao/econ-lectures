Section 1 · LO 15.1

Land and the Theory of Rent

David Ricardo, 1817 — a 200-year-old model that still prices Manhattan, Saudi oil, and Shohei Ohtani.

What Is “Land”?

Key Concept · Land

Natural resources used in production — farmland, mineral deposits, water, airwaves, and physical sites. Land is “the gifts of nature” — what production starts with rather than makes.

Anything in nature production draws on: the lot a coffee shop sits on, the airwaves a station broadcasts over, the river a dam taps. (First defined Ch 3, §3.1.1.)

Land is one of the four factors of production (Ch 3). The economist's "land" is a catch-all for natural resources nobody manufactured — not just dirt, but mineral deposits, water, spectrum, and physical sites. Today we focus on what makes it special as a factor: its supply.

Land’s Defining Trait: It’s Scarce

The most important feature of land: it is extremely scarce and its supply normally cannot expand.

Fenway Park is boxed in by the densest part of Boston. You can’t make more land where it’s wanted most.

The only escape is a new frontier — which is why SpaceX talks about colonizing the Moon and Mars.

This is the property that drives the whole chapter: a fixed supply. When you can't expand supply, all the action is in price. The "alternatives" point is the genuine exception — the only way to truly add land is a new frontier (reclamation at the margin, or eventually off-planet); even SpaceX's Mars rhetoric is, economically, about creating new land because supply is fixed on Earth.

Location, Demand & the Fixed Supply of Land

Desirability drives land prices — firms want proximity to their product markets or input suppliers. But supply can’t respond…

Starbucks near Tufts Medical Center — prime location, $3.65 grande (vs $2.95).

You can’t manufacture more beachfront — supply is perfectly inelastic (a vertical line, S₀).

Price is set entirely by demand: D₀→D₁ lifts rent r₀→r₁, quantity never moves. (Coffee farmers replant; land can’t.)

Merged "location & demand" with "fixed supply → vertical curve." Because land's supply is perfectly inelastic, a more desirable location just shifts demand right (D₀→D₁), lifting rent (r₀→r₁) with quantity fixed — all adjustment runs through price. The Starbucks premium ($3.65 vs $2.95, Ch 11) is the same idea on the output side. Bridges to the real Fenway lease prices next.

Scarce Land → Steep Prices (Right Here in Fenway)

84–100 Peterborough St: 3,700 SF at $50/SF/yr = $185,000/yr (~$15.5K/month) — the price of a fixed location pressed by demand.

A real listing a few blocks from campus. Because the supply of Fenway storefronts is fixed, demand alone sets the lease rate — $50 per square foot per year. This is economic rent in the wild — the concrete payoff to the location-and-demand idea, and the bridge into Ricardo's rent theory.

Rent Theory — Who Was David Ricardo?

David Ricardo studied the wheat market to understand economic rent and land value, focusing on:

1. Corn Laws & food prices — tariffs raised food prices, letting him trace the effect on land rents, wages, and profits.

2. Land productivity & rent — rent is tied to land quality, especially as demand pushes farming onto less fertile land.

3. Distribution of wealth — rising prices made landlords richer, raising the split among landlords, capitalists, and workers.

These insights became his theory of differential rent — the model we’re about to walk through.

Ricardo (1772–1823), classical economist and MP, author of the 1817 "Principles of Political Economy and Taxation." He used the wheat market — under England's Corn Laws — as the laboratory for his theory of differential rent. The next slide turns that theory into the parable of three settlers.

The Parable of Three Settlers

First settlers claim the best meadows (100 bushels). Land is abundant → they pay no rent.

As settlers arrive, every meadow is taken. Fatima can farm free scrubland (60 bushels) — or rent a meadow.

She’ll pay up to 100 − 60 = 40 bushels. Rent = the gap over the marginal land.

Ricardo (1817) explained why English landlords were getting rich as food prices rose. His twist: rent does NOT determine the price of wheat — the price of wheat determines rent. High wheat prices make scrubland worth farming, which makes the meadow's productivity advantage more valuable, which drives up rent. Causality runs price → rent.

Rent = Productivity Gap Above the Marginal Land

The marginal land (scrubland, 60) is the least productive plot still in use. It sets the baseline.

Rent on any parcel = its productivity − the marginal land’s. Better land → bigger gap → higher rent.

If a new technology makes wasteland productive at 80 (now the marginal land), rents on every better plot RISE: meadow rent jumps from 40 to 100−80... wait, to 100−80? No: meadow 100 − new marginal 80 would be 20. Careful — the textbook problem uses meadow 200 / scrub 120 / wasteland 0→80, where meadow rent rises 80→120 because the new marginal land (80) sits ABOVE the old (which left use). The teaching point: rents are set by the gap to the WORST land still farmed.

Two Definitions to Lock In

Marginal land

The least productive land still in use. Its productivity is the baseline against which all other rents are measured.

Economic rent

Payment to a factor above the minimum needed to keep it in its current use. When supply is perfectly inelastic, the entire payment is economic rent.

Distinguish economic rent from everyday "rent" (your monthly apartment check). Your apartment payment = economic rent on the scarce LOCATION + a normal return on the landlord's BUILDING investment (which had an opportunity cost). Only the location piece is pure Ricardian rent.

Differential Rent — Why Landlords Love the Common

Same business, two locations: a full-service restaurant on Boston Common pays ~$17,000/mo; a comparable one in Brookline pays ~$8,500. That gap is differential rent.

A spot beside a beautiful, protected park is scarce and desirable — so it commands a premium that flows straight to the landowner.

The twist: landlords around the Common have every incentive to lobby city authorities to keep it as a park that can’t be commercialized — preserving the scarcity that inflates their own rents.

Same logic as London’s Green Belt — protecting open space quietly hands a rent windfall to nearby owners. (We’ll return to it.)

Differential rent in the wild: the ~$17K vs ~$8.5K rent gap is the premium on a scarce, prime location next to a protected amenity. The political-economy point: because the park's "no-commercialization" rule keeps surrounding land uniquely desirable, incumbent landowners benefit from preserving it — a private incentive to lobby for a public good that happens to protect their rents. Direct parallel to the Green Belt slide and to Ricardo's differential rent (rent set by the location-quality gap).

Economic Rent Is Everywhere

Any factor whose supply can’t expand to meet demand earns rent — not just dirt.

Manhattan land — 22.8 sq mi, unchanged since 1626. A parking lot sold for $30M; the price is pure rent.

Shohei Ohtani — $700M Dodgers deal vs ~$5M next-best. The ~$65M/yr gap is rent on a vertical talent supply.

cars.com — valued at $872M. There is exactly one. Entire value is economic rent.

A neurosurgeon — $800K, would stay for $200K. The $600K gap is rent on extreme scarcity of skill.

The more inelastic the supply, the larger the share of payment that is rent.

Common thread: whenever supply can't easily expand to meet rising demand, the factor earns rent. Ask of any "too-high" price — concert tickets, beachfront, star salaries — "is the supply fixed?" If so, you're looking at economic rent.

OPEC and the Rent on Oil

Meadowland = Saudi crude

Cheap, easy to extract — sometimes under $10/barrel. Huge rent when world prices are high.

Scrubland = oil sands & deepwater

Feasible but expensive — over $50/barrel. Only profitable when prices are high.

OPEC restricts output — like banning farming on some grassland. It raises the price and the rent on its cheap “meadows.” Saudi rents fund ~60% of the government budget.

Saudi production cuts of ~1 million bpd (2023–2025) aimed to hold prices above $80. The "ban farming on the grassland" framing is exactly Ricardo — artificial scarcity raising the rent on the inframarginal (cheap) barrels.

Ricardo in 2025: Medallions & Spectrum

NYC taxi medallions

Capped at a fixed number in 1937. As the city grew, prices hit $1.3M (2013). Pure rent on artificial scarcity.

Uber/Lyft entered without medallions — effectively expanding supply. Values crashed to $80K.

Radio spectrum

Genuinely fixed by physics. FCC auctions licenses; winning bids are rent on a natural resource.

T-Mobile paid $3.5B for spectrum in 2024. Like Manhattan, it can’t be manufactured.

A 200-year-old model still explaining markets Ricardo never imagined.

Medallions are the clearest modern Ricardian rent — and the clearest demonstration that "expanding the supply of land" (Uber) destroys the rent overnight.

Think Like an Economist

London’s Green Belt (1947) bans most development on a ring around the city — the equivalent of banning farming on the grassland.

Who benefits? Existing homeowners inside the boundary — their property values rise.

Who pays? Renters and first-time buyers facing artificially high prices.

A 200-year-old model lighting up a thoroughly modern policy debate.

Discussion prompt: artificial scarcity (the Green Belt) creates economic rent for incumbents at the expense of newcomers — the same distributional pattern as taxi medallions. Is the open-countryside benefit worth the housing-cost transfer?

Common Misconception: Can’t We Just Make More Land?

“If land is fixed, how do you explain Boston’s Back Bay or Dubai’s Palm Islands?”

Boston built its own land. The hilly Shawmut Peninsula (<800 acres) had its three Trimount hills leveled in the 1800s; the gravel filled the Mill Pond, South Cove (→ Chinatown), and the Back Bay (1857–82, ~700 acres). A peninsula became a ~89 sq-mi city.

But it’s slow and astronomically expensive — Dubai’s Palm Jumeirah cost $12B over six years.

And it creates land in one spot — you can’t make another Times Square. Desirable locations stay fixed, so Ricardo’s vertical supply curve holds.

Merged: Boston's landfill history + the reclamation misconception. The point isn't that land can never be created — Boston's Back Bay and Dubai's Palm prove it can — it's that creating desirable LOCATIONS is slow, hugely expensive, and one-off, so the supply of any given location is fixed for practical purposes and Ricardo's vertical curve holds. Source: historyofmassachusetts.org, "How Boston Lost Its Hills."

Can We Ever Add Land? — Improving Land

Though generally inelastic, usable land can be increased at the margin — irrigation, clearing jungle, draining swamps, terracing mountains, and land reclamation (new land from the sea).

Examples: Hong Kong Disneyland & Dubai’s artificial islands — built on reclaimed sea floor.

But: these projects are slow and astronomically expensive — and can’t make another Times Square.

The honest caveat to "land is fixed": at the margin, humans do create usable land — Hong Kong, Dubai, Boston. But it's expensive, slow, and creates land in one spot, so it doesn't expand the supply of desirable LOCATIONS. Reinforces the misconception slide just before it.

Why Firms Cluster: Agglomeration

Industrial agglomeration: related firms cluster, pushing local rents up — but the benefits outweigh the cost.

Marshall’s external economies of scale:

  • A shared pool of specialized workers
  • Dense networks of suppliers · rapid knowledge spillovers

Silicon Valley tech · Detroit autos · Hollywood film — the rent flows to whoever owns property in the right place.

Despite the most expensive commercial real estate on earth, thousands of tech firms pack into a narrow SF–San Jose strip because the agglomeration benefits are self-reinforcing. The three clusters — Silicon Valley, Detroit's Big Three, Hollywood — all show the same pattern: clustering bids up local land rents. Ricardo would recognize it immediately.

Background: Where Does the Money Come From?

A firm needs money to buy capital (to produce more, or more efficiently). It doesn’t print it — it borrows from the financial system, which is funded by savers like us.

👤

You / Households

Savers supply funds in the factor market

🏦

Banks & Markets

Channel savings into loans, bonds, equity

🏭

Firms

Get the money → buy capital

The price that connects them is the interest rate: we earn it for lending our savings; the firm pays it to fund its capital. (We map this market in §15.3–15.4.)

The crucial framing students miss: the interest rate the firm "pays" isn't abstract — it flows to households who supplied the savings in the factor market. We are the suppliers of financial capital; the firm is the demander. This sets up financial markets (§15.3) and the loanable-funds market (§15.4), and explains why the interest rate is THE cost of capital in the investment decision.

Is It Worthwhile? Two Tools

1. Present Value (PV)

Discount every future return back to today’s dollars, add them up, and compare to the upfront cost.

Invest if PV(returns) > cost

2. Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

Find the break-even rate that makes PV(returns) exactly equal the cost — the project’s own rate of return.

Invest if IRR > market interest rate

Two sides of the same coin — both give the same yes/no, and the interest rate is the hurdle. Let’s run the numbers on Amazon’s robots.

Roadmap slide before the worked example. PV compares dollars (discount future returns, beat the cost); IRR compares rates (project return vs market rate). They always agree. The next several slides build MRP_K (the benefit side), then present value, the Amazon PV table, rate sensitivity, and IRR.

Labor or Capital? Where the Next Dollar Goes

PV and IRR tell you whether a project clears the bar. But a firm also chooses where its next investment dollar goes — more workers or more machines? It compares the return from each side.

Labor — MRPL

Extra revenue from one more worker. Hire until MRPL = wage (w).

Capital — MRPK

Extra revenue from one more machine. Invest until MRPK = cost of capital (r).

The next dollar goes wherever the bang-per-buck is bigger. At the best mix, the last dollar on each input earns the same return:

\( \dfrac{MRP_K}{r} \;=\; \dfrac{MRP_L}{w} \)

Both MRPs slope down (diminishing returns) — so the firm keeps reallocating until the returns balance.

Continues page 28: PV/IRR answer "is this project worth it?"; this slide answers "labor or capital?" The allocation rule is equalize MRP per dollar across inputs — spend the next dollar where MRP/price is higher until MRP_K/r = MRP_L/w. Both MRPs diminish, which is what makes an interior mix optimal. Next we zoom into the capital side: MRP_K vs the interest rate.

Physical Capital

Manufactured products used to make other goods & services — cranes, machinery. Buying them is called investment.

MRPK = demand for capital. The extra revenue from one more unit of capital — downward-sloping due to diminishing returns.

Cost of capital = opportunity cost = the interest rate (borrowing cost, or forgone savings).

Investment level: firms invest until MRPK = cost of capital.

The capital side of the previous slide, drawn out. Capital demand = MRP_K, downward-sloping from diminishing returns. The firm keeps buying capital until the return on the last unit (MRP_K) equals its cost (the interest rate). When r falls, the firm slides down MRP_K and invests more — the link the loanable-funds market will make concrete.

Time Value of Money

Goal: compare investments with different income streams — payoffs that arrive at different times. Money you save earns interest, so it’s worth more later.

 Discuss: how do you connect a dollar today to a dollar a year from now?

Today → Future: compound

\( FV = PV\,(1+r)^t \)

Future → Today: discount

\( PV = \dfrac{FV}{(1+r)^t} \)

The bridge between today and the future is the factor (1 + r) — multiply to go forward, divide to come back.

Ask the question FIRST (let them propose multiplying/dividing by 1+interest), then reveal the two formulas. The (1+r) factor is the whole idea: compound forward, discount back. This is the tool for comparing income streams that arrive at different times.

Why a Dollar Today Wins: Discounting

$100 today > $100 in the future — today’s $100 can earn interest (and future dollars lose value to inflation).

Discounting = the process of calculating the present value of future income (running the compounding in reverse).

$100 compounding at 10% a year — each step is ×1.10:

The ladder makes (1+r)^t concrete: $100 at 10% becomes $161 in five years. Discounting is just walking back down the ladder — $161 five years out is worth $100 today. Connects to the next two worked examples.

Present vs. Future: Earning Interest

Example: what is the future value of $1,000 lent for one year at 5%?

\( FV = PV(1+i)^n \)
\( FV = 1000\,(1+0.05)^1 \)
\( FV = 1000\,(1.05) \)
\( FV = \$1{,}050 \)

Forward direction: multiply by (1+i). $1,000 today, lent at 5%, becomes $1,050 in a year — the $50 is the interest you earn.

Present vs. Future: Discounting

Example: what is the present value of getting $1,000 one year from now at 5%?

\( PV = \dfrac{FV}{(1+i)^n} \)
\( PV = \dfrac{1000}{(1+0.05)^1} = \dfrac{1000}{1.05} \)
\( PV = \$952.38 \)

Backward direction: divide by (1+i). $1,000 a year from now is worth only $952.38 today — that's the most you'd pay now to receive $1,000 next year at 5%. This is the discounting that drives every capital, bond, and education decision in the chapter.

How Fast Future Dollars Shrink

At 5%, $1 in 30 years is still worth ~23¢.

At 20%, that same dollar is worth less than 0.5¢.

Higher interest rates punish the distant future — which is why rates govern long-horizon investment.

This is the visual intuition behind why a rate hike kills long-dated projects (a 30-year robot payoff, a new factory) far more than short-dated ones. Connect forward to why the Fed's 2022 hikes shelved automation projects.

The Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

IRR = the rate at which PV of returns exactly equals the upfront cost. Here ≈ 15%.

Decision rule: invest if IRR > market rate. At 5% the 15% IRR clears easily; at 20% it fails.

PV and IRR are two sides of one coin — one compares dollars, the other compares rates.

Both methods give the same invest/don't answer. IRR is handy because it's a single number you can compare to whatever the market rate happens to be that year.

Section 4 · LO 15.4

Interest Rates & the Credit Market

The interest rate is just a price — set where the supply and demand for funds meet.

The Loanable Funds Market

Savers → supply money. Slopes up: higher rates reward saving.

Borrowers → demand money. Slopes down: lower rates make more projects pay.

They meet at the equilibrium interest rate — the price firms weigh for every investment.

Rate ↑ → cost of borrowing ↑ → money demanded ↓

Rate ↓ → cost of borrowing ↓ → money demanded ↑

Ch 4 supply-and-demand applied to money. Savers supply, borrowers demand, the interest rate clears the market. The two-row logic is the movement ALONG the demand curve: a higher rate raises the cost of borrowing, so less is demanded.

The Market Sets the Firm’s Investment

The firm is a price-taker in the credit market. The market sets the rate i₀; the firm invests I₀ where MRPK = i₀. More saving → S₀→S₁ → rate falls to i₁ → the firm slides down MRPK and invests more (I₁).

The linkage students need: the loanable-funds market (Panel A) sets the economy-wide interest rate, which each firm takes as given (Panel B) and uses as the hurdle on its MRP_K. When more saving lowers the rate (S0→S1, i0→i1), every firm slides down its MRP_K curve and invests more. This is why a savings glut or a Fed cut boosts investment.

What Moves the Equilibrium Rate?

Shifts in supply (saving)

  • Uncertainty → save more (COVID 2020) → rates down
  • Aging population draws down savings → rates up
  • Govt deficits absorb funds — crowding out

Shifts in demand (investment)

  • Tech optimism (AI boom) → demand up → rates up
  • Recessions → firms postpone → rates down
  • The Fed sets short rates directly
Crowding out is the key vocabulary item: heavy government borrowing reduces the supply of funds available to private borrowers, pushing rates up and squeezing private investment.

The Low-Rate Decade & Its Reversal

2010–2021: ~0.8% avg. Cheap capital fueled AI, cloud, automation; VC flooded into Uber, Airbnb, OpenAI. Mortgages fell below 3%.

2022–2023: hiked to 5.3%. VC fell 35%, housing sales plunged, automation projects shelved.

The interest rate is the connective tissue linking every factor market.

Source: FRED FEDFUNDS. The same rate that prices capital also moves land values and — through automation — the demand for labor. This single chart ties §15.2, §15.3, §15.4, and Ch 14 together.

Economist at Work: The Fed’s Dual Mandate

Congress gives the Fed two jobs: maximize employment and stabilize prices.

When inflation hit 9.1% (June 2022), the Fed raised rates fast — deliberately slowing investment and hiring to ease price pressure. By late 2024 inflation was ~2.7%, and cuts began.

Eight meetings a year — each decision ripples through every factor market in this chapter.

The trade-off is real and painful: higher rates cool demand by making capital expensive, which slows hiring (capital–labor link). This is the bridge from micro factor markets toward the macro course.

The Capital Investment Rule

Labor: hire until W = MRP

Capital: invest until the return on the last unit = the interest rate

The low-rate decade (2010–2021, ~0.8%) made huge waves of AI and automation profitable. When the Fed hiked to 5.3% in 2022–2023, many of the same projects were shelved. The interest rate shapes the pace and direction of technological change itself. Track today’s rate — the Effective Federal Funds Rate: newyorkfed.org/markets/reference-rates/effr

Section 3 · LO 15.3

Financial Markets: Where the Money Comes From

Firms invest when the return beats the interest rate — but first they have to raise the cash.

Five Channels to Raise Capital

1. Bank loans — small/medium firms, predictable cash flow

2. Bonds — borrow directly from many investors

3. Stocks / IPO — sell ownership shares

4. Venture capital — finance risky startups

5. Private equity — buy & restructure mature firms

Each channel matches a stage and risk profile: loans for steady cash flow, bonds for big established firms, stocks for growth, VC for unproven startups, PE for turnaround targets. The next slides take them two at a time.

Debt Capital: Bank Loans

Bank loans — borrow from one bank, repay with interest, usually pledge collateral. The most common source of outside capital for most firms.

Commercial & industrial loans — small-business loans granted on credit history, business potential, and collateral.

Limitations — banks are conservative: loan amounts are capped, rates rise for riskier borrowers, and unproven ideas are often denied.

Bank debt is the default first stop. The catch: banks lend against proven cash flow and collateral, so young or risky firms get small loans, high rates, or a flat no — which pushes larger or riskier firms toward bonds and equity.

Debt Capital: Corporate Bonds

When bank borrowing is insufficient or too restrictive, firms issue bonds — IOU certificates promising to repay over time to thousands of investors at once.

Three parts: face value (par, repaid at maturity) · coupon rate (periodic fixed interest) · maturity date (months to decades, even perpetual).

Yield = coupon ÷ current price. Yield rises with risk — riskier bonds must pay more. Advantage: risk is spread across many investors. Catch: a bond is a fixed claim against the firm, and future issuance depends on its credit rating.

Bonds scale a loan across many lenders, lowering the rate. Both loans and bonds are DEBT — fixed repayment, no ownership dilution. Yield = coupon/price moves inversely with price and rises with risk. Press ↓ for the bond market.

The Bond Market

The bond market is where firms issue bonds and bondholders invest — lending money in exchange for interest.

US firms issued over $1.5 trillion in bonds in 2024 — the bond market is roughly twice the size of the stock market.

A secondary market lets bondholders sell before maturity, which makes buyers more willing to lend in the first place. The sheer scale (2x the stock market) is why corporate borrowing costs hinge on bond demand and credit ratings.

Equity Capital: Stocks & IPOs

Stocks sell partial ownership (equity). Shareholders are owners; an IPO is the first public sale to raise capital. Market cap = the firm’s stock-value worth; capital gains = price rises. Traded on NYSE/NASDAQ.

Advantages: raise big capital, no repayment, limited liability for losses.

Disadvantages: lose ownership & profits (dividends), mandatory disclosure, outside influence on management.

SpaceX’s eventual IPO is among the most anticipated ever. Press ↓ for a cautionary tale.

Equity = selling ownership, no repayment obligation but you dilute control and share profits as dividends. SpaceX is the headline "when will it IPO?" story. The down-slide is the risk counterweight: stocks can stay underwater for decades.

Why Would SpaceX Go Public?

SpaceX is privately held — valued around $350B (2024). An IPO would sell shares to the public to raise enormous capital.

What would they fund with it? Capital-hungry bets: Starship development, the Starlink satellite network, and the Mars program.

Why wait? Going public means quarterly disclosure and shareholder pressure — which can clash with Musk’s very long-horizon Mars goals.

A hypothetical SpaceX ticker — the firm trades the capital of an IPO against the freedom of staying private.

The IPO decision in one example: raise huge capital for Starship/Starlink/Mars vs the cost of public disclosure and short-term shareholder pressure. This is the equity advantages/disadvantages tradeoff made concrete — and why a cash-rich, mission-driven firm with patient private investors might delay. stock_price.png is a hypothetical "if SpaceX were public" ticker.

Stocks Are Risky: The Cisco Lesson

Buy Cisco at the 2000 dot-com peak (~$80) and you waited 20+ years just to break even — even though Cisco was a great company.

Holding all those years, the opportunity cost was huge — you missed the internet, social-media, and mobile booms.

A great company at the wrong price is a bad investment. Now think about today’s AI valuations…

The Cisco cautionary tale: paying the bubble price meant 20+ years to recover nominal value, plus the opportunity cost of every other trend you missed. Ties straight to §15.2 PV (a stock is worth the PV of future earnings; the 2000 price implied earnings that never came) and previews behavioral bubbles (Ch 18). The AI parallel makes it current.

The Rise and Fall of Meme Stocks

Easy access: apps like Robinhood — no fees, gamified — brought millions of retail investors into the market.

GameStop, Jan 2021: r/WallStreetBets coordinated a short squeeze — $17 → ~$500 in weeks with no change in fundamentals — then it crashed −82%.

The market is risky: social-media hype can rip price from the present value of earnings (§15.2). No guaranteed profit — gains for some, losses for many. (Behavioral econ, Ch 18.)

The meme-stock episode is the counterexample to efficient markets — herd behavior overriding PV fundamentals, amplified by zero-fee gamified trading. GameStop spiked on a short squeeze, not earnings, then collapsed. Ties to the Cisco lesson (price vs value) and previews Ch 18 behavioral economics.

Measuring Risk: The “Fear Index”

Investors gauge market risk with the VIX — the “fear index.” It measures the volatility traders expect over the next 30 days.

Low VIX (~12–20) = calm, confident markets.

High VIX = fear: it spiked near 80 in the 2008 crisis and the 2020 COVID crash. A check on how risky the moment feels.

The VIX (CBOE Volatility Index) is the market's expected 30-day volatility implied by options prices — nicknamed the "fear index." Calm markets sit ~12-20; panic sends it to 40, 60, 80+. It's the standard quick read on perceived market risk and a natural companion to the Cisco "stocks are risky" point.

Equity Capital: Venture Capital

Venture capital — equity funding for risky start-ups with high-growth potential: seed money in exchange for a share of ownership.

Staged funding (money grows each round): Seed → Angel → Series A → B → C → Mezzanine → IPO.

Most bets fail; a few 10× winners carry the fund. OpenAI raised $6.6B in 2024 at a $157B valuation — with zero profit.

The staged model matches capital to a startup's evolving risk — Apple's garage, Facebook's dorm. Each round raises more at a higher valuation. AI VC exceeded $100B globally in 2024.

Equity Capital: Private Equity

Private equity (PE) firms raise capital to buy companies that are not publicly traded — often mature firms that are struggling — take a large (often majority) stake, improve performance, and sell for a profit years later.

They may replace management and restructure. Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Carlyle manage trillions.

Controversial: defenders say it imposes financial discipline; critics say it loads firms with debt and cuts jobs.

PE targets underperforming mature firms (vs VC's young startups). Leveraged buyouts: buy with borrowed money, restructure, exit in 3-7 years. The discipline-vs-debt-and-layoffs debate is the live controversy.

Capital structure = the firm’s mix of debt (loans, bonds) and equity (stocks, VC, PE). The optimal mix balances debt’s tax advantage against equity’s flexibility.

When rates rise, debt channels get expensive and investment slows; when stock markets boom, equity is easy. VC dried up 35% in 2023 then roared back in 2024 — the availability of each channel shapes how much firms invest.

Section 5 · LO 15.5

Entrepreneurship & Creative Destruction

Ingredients don’t cook themselves. Someone has to combine them and bear the risk.

The Fourth Factor of Production

The entrepreneur combines land, labor, and capital and bears the risk of failure — in exchange for the possibility of profit.

The reward is economic profit — above the opportunity cost of all inputs. In competition it tends to zero (Ch 10) unless you create something new.

Profit is also a signal: high returns tell other entrepreneurs where to send resources. AI profits in 2023–2025 triggered a stampede.

Profit is not just a reward — it's an information system steering resources toward their highest-valued uses. The extraordinary early-AI returns signaled "build here," pulling in startups, talent, and capital.

Schumpeter’s Creative Destruction

The essential fact of capitalism isn’t price competition — it’s innovation competition. New products destroy old ones (Schumpeter, 1942).

Kodak: 145,000 jobs (1988) → bankrupt 2012.
Blockbuster: 9,000 stores (2004) → one left by 2014.
Netflix: 13,000 staff, 325M subscribers.

Wealth and disruption in equal measure — managing the transition (Ch 14) is the policy challenge.

Instagram had 13 employees when Kodak went bankrupt. Streaming killed video rental; smartphones killed GPS units, cameras, MP3 players; ride-hailing killed the taxi medallion. Each wave destroys jobs in the old industry and creates them in the new.

Removing Weeds: From Labor to Labor + Capital

The problem: weeds and grass compete with crops for water, light, and nutrients — cutting yields.

Old way — pure labor: hoeing weeds by hand was slow, back-breaking, and labor-intensive.

Then — labor + capital: mechanical tilling with tractors substitutes capital for some labor. Faster, but still costly — the real leap came from chemistry.

The pre-herbicide era: weeds were fought with hand labor, then partly mechanized with tractors (capital substituting for labor). It was still expensive and imperfect. The Chemical Leap: Herbicides & Roundup Ready

1940s: selective herbicides (2,4-D) kill broadleaf weeds, not grass crops — the first shift from labor → chemical (capital) input.

1970s: Monsanto’s glyphosate (Roundup) — a non-selective herbicide that kills almost every plant.

1990s: “Roundup Ready” GMO seeds (new capital/tech input) — crops engineered immune to Roundup.

Productivity boom: plant the seed, spray the whole field, kill only the weeds → low-labor no-till farming.

Roundup (1970s)

Roundup Ready seed (1990s)

Rent-theory lens: this is Ricardo’s “improving land” — technology that makes each acre far more productive. The question rent theory asks: who captures the extra output?

The chemical revolution: seed + herbicide are COMPLEMENTARY capital inputs that replaced labor and raised land productivity enormously — the "improving land" idea from §15.1 in modern form. The cliffhanger — who gets the surplus from more productive land? — sets up the monopoly slide.

The Price of Productivity: Building Monopoly Power

Building the monopoly: Monsanto patented both Roundup and the Roundup Ready seed — a tied system where farmers needed both.

Controlling inputs: “Technology Use Agreements” forbade seed-saving — a practice farmers had followed for millennia — turning independent producers into yearly customers.

Half 1 of the monopoly story: patents on the complementary seed + herbicide create a tied system, and the no-seed-saving contract turns one-time buyers into recurring customers. Next: how that market power was enforced, and who pays.

Abusing Market Power — The Rent-Theory Price

Abusing market power: private investigators, a 1-800-ROUNDUP snitch hotline, and lawsuits that bankrupted farmers; herbicide drift killed neighbors’ crops — join or face ruin.

The rent-theory price: the productivity gain that could have raised land rents and farm profits is captured by the patent monopolist — farmers pay more every year for seed + chemicals. Market power shifts from many small farmers to one corporation that owns the critical input.

Listen — NPR Planet Money: “A Pesticide, A Pigweed and a Farmer’s Murder” (2017)

Half 2: enforcement (snitch hotline, lawsuits) + drift pressure, and the rent-theory payoff — the surplus from more-productive land is captured by the input monopolist via patents and the seed-saving ban, not the farmer/landowner. Patents: The Incentive to Innovate

Why spend millions on a new drug if rivals can copy it instantly? IP rights grant a temporary monopoly: patents (~20 yrs), copyrights, trademarks.

A drug may cost $2B to develop; most candidates fail. The winners must fund the whole pipeline — why a patented drug runs $100K/yr and the generic $500.

The tension (Ch 11): patents reward risk — but they concentrate market power and create deadweight loss. How long should the monopoly last?

The core trade-off: without IP protection, no one funds risky R&D; with it, you get a temporary monopoly (high prices, deadweight loss) in exchange for the innovation. Drug development is the sharpest case — $2B and mostly failures, so winners must be very profitable.

Patents in Practice

The patent cliff: Humira was the world’s best-selling drug for a decade under patent. US biosimilars entered in 2023 and prices began falling — the monopoly is temporary by design.

At scale: the USPTO granted ~327,000 utility patents in 2024 — roughly 3× the 1995 count.

When IP isn’t enough: for COVID vaccines, speed beat exclusivity — Operation Warp Speed put ~$18B of public money behind development.

Patents in the real world: the Humira cliff shows the design (temporary), the USPTO count shows scale, and Warp Speed shows the limit — when public urgency outruns private incentives, governments fund R&D directly.

The New Wave: AI — Complement or Substitute?

An enormous bet

OpenAI $6.6B, Anthropic $7.3B; Google/Microsoft/Meta/Amazon committed $200B+ to AI in 2024–25 — betting AI is the next general-purpose technology.

Complement or substitute?

A power saw complements a carpenter; an ATM substitutes for a teller. AI coding assistants (+30–55% productivity) do both, depending on the task.

Closes the loop to Ch 14: new capital changes the marginal product of labor. The complement-vs-substitute question is THE open question for AI's effect on wages. Next slide: the long-run optimistic pattern — new job categories.

New Work: Technology Creates Jobs

Historically capital is net positive for labor — the car killed blacksmith jobs but created millions more. About 60% of US jobs in 2018 were in occupations that did not exist in 1940.

The internet created: app developer, UX designer, data scientist, cloud & cybersecurity engineer — none existed in 1990.

The AI wave is creating: prompt engineer, ML engineer, AI ethicist/auditor, model-training & data-labeling roles.

Whether AI extends that pattern or breaks it is the open question (Ch 16 picks up the spillovers).

Sources: Autor, Chin, Salomons & Seegmiller, “New Frontiers… 1940–2018,” QJE 2024 (nber.org/papers/w30389) · Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, “Generative AI at Work,” NBER 2023 (w31161)

The optimistic long-run case: technology creates whole new job categories (60% of 2018 jobs are post-1940 occupations). The internet and AI examples make it concrete. But "extends or breaks the pattern" is genuinely open — sets up Ch 16 externalities/spillovers.

Innovation in Unlikely Places: Ski Dubai

A $400M indoor ski resort in one of the hottest deserts on earth (2005). It seemed absurd.

Over a million visitors a year. The entrepreneurial insight: demand for winter sports existed where no supply did. Combine capital + land + labor → a market that didn’t exist.

Schumpeter would approve: the entrepreneur's role is to combine factors in a way no one had attempted, creating a market where none existed. Cooling tech (capital) + mall space (land) + instructors (labor).

Key Takeaways

1. Land is fixed → vertical supply, price set by demand. The whole payment is economic rent.

2. Capital is intertemporal. Use present value; invest when the return beats the interest rate.

3. Five financing channels — loans, bonds, stocks, VC, PE — make up a firm’s capital structure.

4. The interest rate clears the loanable-funds market — the price of capital, the tissue linking all factor markets.

5. Entrepreneurs bear risk for profit — and drive growth through creative destruction.

6. Human capital obeys the same PV logic — a degree is an investment in yourself.

The unifying thread: every factor market prices the return on the last unit against its cost. Land adds fixed supply (rent); capital adds time (present value); entrepreneurship adds risk (profit). The interest rate ties them together.
