What Is a Market?

Market — any arrangement bringing buyers and sellers together to exchange goods, services, or resources.

Competitive market — many buyers and many sellers, each too small to influence the market price on its own.

Three assumptions behind a competitive market

1. Many buyers + sellers

No single one is big enough to move the market price.

2. Price takers

Buyers and sellers accept the going price — they decide how much , not what price.

3. Identical products

Buyers treat the products of different sellers as interchangeable .

Prices as signals. When eggs got scarce, the price told consumers “find alternatives” and farmers “rebuild your flocks now.” No committee met — the price mechanism did the work.

Three assumptions on one slide: many buyers + sellers, price-taking, identical products. The next slide gives concrete examples that fit this model; the slide after zooms into the identical-products assumption with gasoline (holds) and aspirin (breaks). "Competitive" is a precise word in economics — most markets we'll study in Ch 4–10 satisfy these three; Chs 11–12 are about what happens when assumption #3 breaks.

Six Kinds of Markets — What Counts as the “Price”?

Same supply-and-demand model. Different name for the price.

Goods markets

Gas stations, grocery stores, flea markets.

Price = $ per unit.

Labor markets

LinkedIn, Indeed, hiring fairs, recruiters.

Price = wage.

Financial markets

Loanable funds, banks, bond markets.

Price = interest rate.

Stock exchanges

NYSE, NASDAQ, crypto platforms.

Price = per-share value.

Currency markets

Foreign exchange (FX) trading.

Price = exchange rate.

Futures markets

Commodity contracts for later delivery.

Price = locked in today for tomorrow.

⚡ Electricity — price = $/MWh

Wholesale auctions run by grid operators (ISO-NE, PJM, ERCOT, CAISO). Generators submit bids — “I’ll supply X MWh at $Y” — stacked low-to-high; everyone accepted gets paid the highest cleared bid (uniform pricing).

Day-ahead set 24 h before delivery; real-time re-prices every 5 min. $20/MWh on a mild Sunday → $9,000 during a Texas heat wave.

🎯 Prediction markets — price = probability

Kalshi, Polymarket — contracts pay $1 if an event happens, $0 if not. The price is the crowd’s estimated probability. $0.65 on “Fed cuts rates” → the market gives it a 65% chance.

Jan 2026: $701.7 M traded per day . Markets don’t just allocate goods — they aggregate information.

Six market types from the textbook (goods / labor / financial / stock / currency / futures), each with its own name for "price." Then two spotlights at the bottom — both illustrate that the same supply-and-demand model works for "prices" that don't look like dollar amounts: • Electricity = a literal auction market. Generators bid, grid operator stacks low-to-high, uniform-price clearing, day-ahead + real-time tiers. ISO-NE average wholesale ~$35/MWh in mild conditions, ERCOT spiked to $9,000/MWh during Feb 2021 cold event. • Prediction markets (Kalshi, Polymarket) = the price IS the probability. $701.7M daily trading (Jan 2026), up from $15.8B annual in 2024 to $63.5B in 2025. Used by traders, journalists, and increasingly by the Fed itself to read crowd expectations. Same model, very different "price" definitions.

Willingness to Pay (WTP)

The maximum a buyer will pay for one more unit — the building block of demand.

Willingness to pay (WTP) — the maximum price a buyer would pay for one more unit before walking away.

Two readings of every demand curve.

1. At price P → how much will buyers want?

2. At quantity Q → what is the marginal buyer’s WTP for one more?

Hold this. We use the WTP reading in Ch 5 (consumer surplus = WTP − price).

A pint of strawberries: 5 buyers

Buyer WTP (max $) Buys at $4?
Alex $7 ✓ Yes
Beatriz $6 ✓ Yes
Chen $5 ✓ Yes
Devon $4 ✓ Marginal
Esha $3 ✗ Walks

At $4, 4 pints sold. Devon is the marginal buyer — her WTP exactly equals the price.

WTP is the link between individual choice and the market demand curve. Each buyer has a max price; stack them tallest to shortest and you get a "staircase" demand curve. As n grows, the staircase smooths into the familiar downward-sloping curve. The marginal buyer at every price is the one whose WTP exactly equals it. This is the foundation for consumer surplus (Ch 5) — WTP minus price, summed over all buyers who transact. The next three slides use real strawberry footage to anchor the abstraction.

Same Soda. Different WTP.

Your willingness to pay isn’t a property of the product — it’s a property of the situation . The market signal (price) reflects WTP here , not what the thing is “worth” in the abstract.

Grocery store

A wall of alternatives, brand after brand.

Soda price: ~$1.50 / can

You see Coke, Pepsi, Dr Pepper, generics, sparkling water, juice — one shelf over. Your WTP is capped by the next-best alternative . Competition holds price near production cost.

Movie theater

One experience. One bundle. No exit.

Soda price: ~$7.50 / cup

Two hours into a movie, your WTP is anchored to the experience — popcorn + drink + film as a complement bundle. And no alternatives to slip out to. The theater can charge 5×.

Same molecule. 5× the price. WTP varies with setting (alternatives, complements, mood, urgency) — and the market signals each setting’s WTP through its own equilibrium price. Different stores, different equilibria, all from the same demand-and-supply machinery we’re about to build.

Same product, two settings, 5× price gap. Two mechanisms at work: (1) Substitutes available → demand is elastic → WTP capped by next-best alternative → low price (grocery). (2) Complements bundled (movie + popcorn + soda) + zero in-venue substitutes → demand is inelastic → high WTP → high price (theater). This previews two later concepts: elasticity (Ch 6) and price discrimination (Ch 11). For now: the takeaway is that the "market signal" carries setting-specific information; you can't talk about a good's price without specifying where and when.

How is the relationship between price and quantity sold?

Demand isn't an assumption. It's an empirical question we test the same way physicists test gravity.

Hypothesis

If prices keep rising, will sales fall?

• Yes → relationship is negative
• No → relationship is positive

Data

Consumer behavior varies with price.

Collect (price, quantity) pairs — holding shifters fixed.

Analyze

Plot the relationship.

A scatter → a curve → a function.

Conclude

Reject or refine the hypothesis.

• Yes?
• No?

Frame demand as an empirical question, not an assumption. Law of Demand: All else equal, as price ↑, quantity demanded ↓.

Two forces behind every row:

  • Substitution effect: Avocados expensive? Switch to hummus.

The right column shrinks by 20k buyers per $0.50 step. The next slide unpacks why — the 20k who drop out at each price are the ones whose subjective value falls in that band.

The market-level pattern: 6 prices, 6 quantities. Walk down the table row by row — same drop of 20k buyers each step. Two textbook explanations: substitution (switch to a related good) and income (real purchasing power falls). The next slide opens up the row-by-row drop and shows the individual-level mechanism: subjective value.

Every purchase is a vote cast by your subjective value.

Subjective Value:

WTP isn’t a property of the avocado. Hosting brunch for 6? You’ll pay $3.50. Just ate guac yesterday? $1 is your max. Jane, who buys avocados weekly, lands at $2.

How that builds the schedule:

At $3, 20k buyers have a high-enough WTP to transact (people like “You hosting brunch”). Drop the price to $2 and Jane joins — 60k. At $1, “You-just-ate-one” joins — 100k.

The Lesson:

The schedule’s 120 → 20 decline isn’t the product changing. It’s individual subjective values stacking tallest-to-shortest.

Two pedagogical beats keyed to the demand schedule numbers. (1) Same product, different person/state → different WTP: you-hosting-brunch $3.50, you-just-ate $1, Jane $2. (2) Stacking individual WTPs tallest-to-shortest IS the 120k → 20k decline in the schedule — each $0.50 step drops 20k buyers because their WTPs land in that band. The Demand Curve

Every point answers: "At this price, how much will buyers want?"

"P on the vertical axis, Q on the horizontal — Marshall convention from the 1890s. Pick a price, trace to the curve, drop to Q. That's how you read it."

Movement Along the Curve — Reading the Line

Holding everything else constant (ceteris paribus) , the entire line is your prediction tool: pick a price → read off the quantity demanded.

When price changes — same curve, new point

Price drops $2 → $1.50: slide from A to B along the same curve.

Price changes → movement along the curve. The curve itself doesn’t budge.

Hold everything else fixed — income, tastes, related-good prices, expectations, # of buyers. That’s ceteris paribus .

The line is the prediction tool — for any price in the market, the curve tells you how much buyers will want.

Pro tip — vocabulary trap: “Quantity demanded” is a single point on the curve — it changes when the price changes. “Demand” is the entire curve — it only changes when a shifter changes (next section). Mix these up on an exam and you’ll lose points.

Three beats: (1) price moves you along the SAME curve; (2) ceteris paribus is the assumption that lets us draw the curve as a stable line; (3) the line is the prediction tool. The pro tip is the vocabulary trap students will hit on every exam — "quantity demanded" (point) vs "demand" (curve). Poll-1 on the next slide tests exactly this distinction with the avocado price drop $2 → $1.50. The correct answer is "increase in quantity demanded" (B), because the price of the good itself changed.

Section 2

Shifts in Demand

LO 4.2 & 4.3 — What moves the curve vs. moving along it

1. Income ↑ → Demand for Normal Goods ↑

Income roughly doubles → vacation trips per year roughly double too. The entire curve shifts right.

Demand for vacations shifts right

Vacations, dining out, organic produce, gym memberships — all normal goods.

Normal good: income ↑ → demand ↑ (curve shifts right ). E.g. median U.S. household income rose ~12% real terms 2015–2024 → restaurant + travel spending grew faster than groceries.

First of two income slides. Normal good = more income → more demand. Vacation is intuitive — when you make more money, you take more trips. The whole curve shifts right (at every price, buyers now want more). Notice the price of vacations didn’t change — what changed was income. That’s a shifter, not a movement along.

1. Income ↑ → Demand for Inferior Goods ↓

When income rises, value meals lose customers — budgets shift toward fancier alternatives. The curve shifts left.

Demand for value meals shifts left

Value meals, bus rides, ramen, generic brands, used clothing — classic inferior goods.

Inferior good: income ↑ → demand ↓ (curve shifts left ). McDonald’s value-menu visits per household fall as income rises — substituted for sit-down dining.

Counter-intuitive but real. "Inferior" doesn’t mean bad quality — it means demand FALLS as income rises. Bus tickets, used clothing, value meals — all common examples. Price of value meals didn’t change; what changed was income. The whole curve shifts left.

2. Related Goods — Substitutes

When the price of one good rises, demand for its substitute rises. PS5 hikes? Xbox demand climbs.

🎮 PS5 — price rises (movement along)

PS5 price ↑ → PS5 quantity demanded ↓ (move up the same curve).

🎮 Xbox — demand shifts right

🎮

Xbox Series X

At every Xbox price, more buyers want one → whole curve shifts right .

Direction summary: P PS5 ↑ ⇒ movement along D PS5 (Q d PS5 ↓)  |  D Xbox shifts right (Q d Xbox ↑ at every price).

Two charts side-by-side because two different goods are doing different things. The PS5 chart shows movement ALONG (its own price changed). The Xbox chart shows a SHIFT (the price of a related good — its substitute — changed). Direction: substitutes move OPPOSITE for one (price up) → SAME direction for the other (demand up).

2. Related Goods — Complements

When the price of one good rises, demand for its complement falls. PS5 hikes? Demand for PS5 games drops.

🎮 PS5 console — price rises

Same movement-along we saw for substitutes — PS5 price ↑ → Q d ↓.

🕹️ PS5 games — demand shifts left

🕹️

PS5 game disc

Fewer PS5 owners → smaller game-buying base → D games shifts left .

Direction summary: P PS5 ↑ ⇒ movement along D PS5 (Q d PS5 ↓)  |  D games shifts left (Q d games ↓ at every price).

Complements: goods consumed TOGETHER. PS5 + games, gas + SUVs, peanut butter + jelly. When the complement's price goes up, fewer of them sold → smaller market for the partner good → its demand shifts LEFT. Same structure as substitutes: one moves along, the other shifts. Opposite directions of effect.

3. Tastes — Dubai Chocolate

TikTok virality (2024–25) made pistachio-kataifi-filled chocolate a global sensation. Then the trend cooled.

Pistachio prices doubled globally as makers rushed to copy.

Demand: shifts right, then back left

Same product, same price — demand depends on whether it’s on FYP this week.

Tastes: when buyers’ preferences shift, the curve shifts — even with no price change. Viral booms typically pull demand right; fading attention pulls it back.

Tastes is the most volatile shifter — TikTok can move millions of buyers in a week. Dubai chocolate is the canonical 2024 example: pistachio-kataifi-filled bars went from niche to globally backordered. Then by mid-2025 the trend cooled, demand drifted back left. Pistachio prices doubled in 2024 as Lindt, Mars, Cadbury all rushed to copy.

3. Tastes — The Biological Shift (GLP-1s)

Ozempic / Wegovy regulate appetite & blood sugar. Households using GLP-1s cut grocery spend 5–11% — mostly snacks.

🍪 Cookies — demand shifts left

GLP-1 users eat less. Snacks, sweets, and sugary drinks see demand contract.

🥛 High-protein yogurt — demand shifts right

Smaller portions = need more protein per calorie. Greek yogurt & cottage cheese boom.

One drug class, two opposite shifts. Estimates: snack-category demand ↓ 8–15% in GLP-1 households; protein dairy ↑ double-digits since 2023 (Walmart, Kroger earnings notes).

Tastes shifters don’t have to come from culture — they can come from biology too. GLP-1s alter the hormonal signal for hunger, so demand for snacks legitimately FALLS and demand for protein dense foods RISES. Two markets, opposite shifts, same cause. This is showing up in food-company earnings calls already (PepsiCo, General Mills, Conagra all reference GLP-1 headwinds).

4. Expectations — When the EV Tax Credit Expired

Federal $7,500 EV credit ended Sept 30, 2025. Buyers pulled purchases forward to lock in the savings.

📊 U.S. EV sales — quarterly

Q3 spike — pull-forward demand. Q4 crash — buyers already converted.

↑ Before Sept 30

“Buy now or pay $7.5k more in Oct” → whole curve shifts right .

↓ After Sept 30

Effective price ↑ $7,500 → move up the curve, Q d ↓.

Expectations of a future price change move demand today. The Q3 spike isn’t a one-time anomaly — it’s the demand curve responding to an expected price hike. Cox Automotive: 9.8% Q3 EV market share, then 5.3% Q4.

Expectations is the most forward-looking shifter. Buyers don’t just respond to today’s price — they respond to what they think tomorrow’s price will be. The Sept 2025 EV credit sunset is a clean natural experiment: Q3 2025 EV sales hit record highs (Cox Automotive reported 9.8% of new-car sales), then Q4 collapsed to 5.3% as buyers had already pulled their purchases forward. Same product, same model lineup — only the expected future price changed.

5. Number of Buyers

More buyers in the market → demand shifts right. Fewer buyers → demand shifts left.

📅 Sept move-in week, Boston

30,000+ students arrive → futons, microwaves, ramen, Ubers, IKEA runs all see demand shift right .

🏭 Local manufacturer closes

🏭 → 🚪

Lordstown GM (2019): 1,600 jobs gone → town-wide drop in demand.

Restaurants, haircuts, gym memberships, used-car lots — the whole local market shrinks.

Number of buyers is the slow-moving but powerful shifter — immigration, population aging, regional booms and busts. The demand curve in 2030 will look different from 2025 mostly because the buyers are different.

Two examples, opposite directions. Move-in week is the clean RIGHT shift — same Boston, same prices, just 30k more buyers in town. The Lordstown GM closure (March 2019, ~1,600 direct jobs + thousands of related) is the canonical LEFT shift: a closing plant pulls income out of an entire town, demand for restaurants/haircuts/gyms all shift left simultaneously. Number of buyers is also why immigration and population aging matter for econ forecasting — slow but huge over decades.

Demand Shifters — Summary

Five shifters, two directions. Anything other than the good’s own price that changes how much buyers want.

1

Income — income ↑ shifts normal goods right , inferior goods left .

2

Related goods — P substitute ↑ shifts D right ; P complement ↑ shifts D left .

3

Tastes — viral trends, biological shifts (GLP-1s) can move D either way.

4

Expectations — expect future P ↑ → D shifts right today.

5

Number of buyers — more buyers right , fewer left .

Two possible shifts

Any of the 5 shifters can move D₀ in either direction.

The rule: when something other than the good’s own price changes, the whole curve shifts. When the good’s own price changes, you move along the curve. Mix these up on an exam and you’ll lose points.

Summary slide. Five shifters (left list) + the two directions they can move the curve (right diagram). The center black D₀ is the original demand; the red D₂ on the left is "demand decrease"; the green D₁ on the right is "demand increase." Any of the five shifters can move D₀ in either direction depending on the specifics (e.g., income ↑ moves normal-good D right but inferior-good D left). Closing reminder: shifters move the curve; the good's own price moves you ALONG the curve.

The #1 Exam Mistake

Movement Along

The price of this good changed.

Curve stays put. You move to a new point on the same curve.

Shift of the Curve

Something other than the price changed.

The entire curve moves right or left.

The test: "Did the price of this good change?" → Movement along   |   "Did something else change?" → Curve shifts

Misconception: "An increase in demand means more people bought it."

Not necessarily. An increase in demand means the curve shifted right — buyers want more at every price.

"This is the #1 mistake on every economics exam. We're testing it three times today — in Poll 1, this discussion, and Poll 2. If you master this, you're ahead of most students."

Law of Supply: All else equal, as price ↑, quantity supplied ↑.

Why? Higher prices make production more profitable → existing sellers produce more, new sellers enter.

The symmetry: Demand slopes ⬇ down (higher prices discourage buying). Supply slopes ⬆ up (higher prices encourage selling).

"Notice the opposite pattern from demand: price up, quantity up. These opposing forces are what make markets work."

The Supply Curve

Every point answers: "At this price, how much will sellers offer?"

"Upward-sloping — the mirror image of demand. Both curves together give us the market."

Costs ↑ → supply shifts left

2. Technology

Better tech → supply shifts right

4. # of Sellers

More sellers → supply shifts right

5. Gov't Policy

Tax → left; Subsidy → right

6. Related Production

Lime prices ↑ → avocado supply ↓

Same rule applies: Price of this good changes → movement along. Anything else → curve shifts.

Discussion: Price Gouging

5 min — Open Debate

Bloom's L5: Evaluate

After a hurricane, bottled water and generators spike in price. Some states cap prices with "anti-price-gouging" laws.

Using the S&D model: what happens to the quantity available if prices are capped below equilibrium? Who benefits? Who is harmed?

Pro-cap: Low-income families can't afford $10 water; spikes feel exploitative

Anti-cap: Capped prices → no incentive for suppliers to rush in → shortages persist

"This is where economics meets ethics. The model shows capping prices creates shortages. But is that the whole story? We'll formalize this in Topic 7 with price ceiling diagrams."

Debrief: The Price Mechanism

When water prices spike, the price signal simultaneously tells consumers "conserve" and suppliers "rush more in."

Cap the price, and you mute that signal . The market loses its ability to self-correct.

Topic 7 preview: We'll draw price ceiling diagrams and measure the welfare consequences — deadweight loss, misallocation, and black markets.

"Whether we SHOULD let prices rise in emergencies is normative. How the mechanism WORKS is positive — and that's what this chapter equips you to analyze."

Key Takeaways

1. Markets coordinate through prices. Scarce → price ↑ → rations demand, incentivizes supply.

2. Demand ↓, Supply ↑. Law of demand + law of supply create the tension that makes markets work.

3. Shift vs. movement is everything. Price changes → move along. Everything else → curve shifts.

4. Equilibrium = where curves cross. Surpluses push P ↓, shortages push P ↑. Markets self-correct.

5. Comparative statics is your prediction tool. Identify the shift, trace the new equilibrium. When both curves shift, one variable is ambiguous.

"Five sentences that capture the whole chapter. If you remember nothing else, remember #3 — shift vs. movement is the key."
