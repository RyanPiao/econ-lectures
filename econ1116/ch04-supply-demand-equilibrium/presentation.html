What Is a Market?

Market — any arrangement bringing buyers and sellers together to exchange goods, services, or resources.

Competitive market — many buyers and many sellers, each too small to influence the market price on its own.

Three assumptions behind a competitive market

1. Many buyers + sellers

No single one is big enough to move the market price.

2. Price takers

Buyers and sellers accept the going price — they decide how much , not what price.

3. Identical products

Buyers treat the products of different sellers as interchangeable .

Prices as signals. When eggs got scarce, the price told consumers “find alternatives” and farmers “rebuild your flocks now.” No committee met — the price mechanism did the work.

Three assumptions on one slide: many buyers + sellers, price-taking, identical products. The next slide gives concrete examples that fit this model; the slide after zooms into the identical-products assumption with gasoline (holds) and aspirin (breaks). "Competitive" is a precise word in economics — most markets we'll study in Ch 4–10 satisfy these three; Chs 11–12 are about what happens when assumption #3 breaks.

Six Kinds of Markets — What Counts as the “Price”?

Same supply-and-demand model. Different name for the price.

Gas stations, grocery stores, flea markets.

LinkedIn, Indeed, hiring fairs, recruiters.

Loanable funds, banks, bond markets.

Price = interest rate.

NYSE, NASDAQ, crypto platforms.

Price = per-share value.

Currency markets

Foreign exchange (FX) trading.

Price = exchange rate.

Futures markets

Commodity contracts for later delivery.

Price = locked in today for tomorrow.

⚡ Electricity — price = $/MWh

Wholesale auctions run by grid operators (ISO-NE, PJM, ERCOT, CAISO). Generators submit bids — “I’ll supply X MWh at $Y” — stacked low-to-high; everyone accepted gets paid the highest cleared bid (uniform pricing).

Day-ahead set 24 h before delivery; real-time re-prices every 5 min. $20/MWh on a mild Sunday → $9,000 during a Texas heat wave.

🎯 Prediction markets — price = probability

Kalshi, Polymarket — contracts pay $1 if an event happens, $0 if not. The price is the crowd’s estimated probability. $0.65 on “Fed cuts rates” → the market gives it a 65% chance.

Jan 2026: $701.7 M traded per day . Markets don’t just allocate goods — they aggregate information.

Six market types from the textbook (goods / labor / financial / stock / currency / futures), each with its own name for "price." Used by traders, journalists, and increasingly by the Fed itself to read crowd expectations. Same model, very different "price" definitions.

Willingness to Pay (WTP)

The maximum a buyer will pay for one more unit — the building block of demand.

Willingness to pay (WTP) — the maximum price a buyer would pay for one more unit before walking away.

Two readings of every demand curve.

1. At price P → how much will buyers want?

2. At quantity Q → what is the marginal buyer’s WTP for one more?

Hold this. We use the WTP reading in Ch 5 (consumer surplus = WTP − price).

A pint of strawberries: 5 buyers

Buyer WTP (max $) Buys at $4?
Alex $7 ✓ Yes
Beatriz $6 ✓ Yes
Chen $5 ✓ Yes
Devon $4 ✓ Marginal
Esha $3 ✗ Walks

At $4, 4 pints sold. Devon is the marginal buyer — her WTP exactly equals the price.

WTP is the link between individual choice and the market demand curve. Each buyer has a max price; stack them tallest to shortest and you get a "staircase" demand curve. As n grows, the staircase smooths into the familiar downward-sloping curve. The marginal buyer at every price is the one whose WTP exactly equals it. This is the foundation for consumer surplus (Ch 5) — WTP minus price, summed over all buyers who transact. The next three slides use real strawberry footage to anchor the abstraction.

Willingness-to-Pay (WTP)

The maximum amount a consumer is willing and able to pay for a good.

Subjective Value:

WTP isn’t fixed. If you missed lunch, your WTP for a sandwich might be $10. After eating one, your WTP for a second drops to $4. Jane, who ate earlier, has a WTP of only $6.

The Lesson:

Demand starts with the individual’s perception of value, not the product’s inherent cost.

Three pedagogical beats. (1) Same person, different state → different WTP: you-hungry $10, you-full $4. Diminishing marginal value. (2) Different people, different WTPs: Jane (not hungry) at $6 lands between. (3) Stack all individual WTPs tallest-to-shortest and the staircase smooths into the market demand curve. Drives home that demand is a behavioral fact built up from millions of individual valuations — not a property of the good itself. This replaces the older "schedule table" because the bar visualization makes the individual → market aggregation visible; the table comes back later as the avocado walk-through.

5 Demand Shifters

1. Income

Normal goods: income ↑ → demand ↑
Inferior goods: income ↑ → demand ↓

2. Related Goods

Substitutes: Uber surges → Lyft demand ↑
Complements: Gas ↑ → SUV demand ↓

3. Tastes

TikTok trend → demand surges overnight. Avocado toast tripled U.S. consumption.

4. Expectations

Expect prices to rise → buy more now. Expect recession → pull back spending.

5. Number of Buyers

Population growth, immigration, new market access → demand shifts right.

The #1 Exam Mistake

Movement Along

The price of this good changed.

Curve stays put. You move to a new point on the same curve.

Shift of the Curve

Something other than the price changed.

The entire curve moves right or left.

The test: "Did the price of this good change?" → Movement along   |   "Did something else change?" → Curve shifts

Misconception: "An increase in demand means more people bought it."

Not necessarily. An increase in demand means the curve shifted right — buyers want more at every price.

"This is the #1 mistake on every economics exam. We're testing it three times today — in Poll 1, this discussion, and Poll 2. If you master this, you're ahead of most students."

Law of Supply: All else equal, as price ↑, quantity supplied ↑.

Why? Higher prices make production more profitable → existing sellers produce more, new sellers enter.

The symmetry: Demand slopes ⬇ down (higher prices discourage buying). Supply slopes ⬆ up (higher prices encourage selling).

"Notice the opposite pattern from demand: price up, quantity up. These opposing forces are what make markets work."

The Supply Curve

Every point answers: "At this price, how much will sellers offer?"

"Upward-sloping — the mirror image of demand. Both curves together give us the market."

Discussion: Price Gouging

5 min — Open Debate

Bloom's L5: Evaluate

After a hurricane, bottled water and generators spike in price. Some states cap prices with "anti-price-gouging" laws.

Using the S&D model: what happens to the quantity available if prices are capped below equilibrium? Who benefits? Who is harmed?

Pro-cap: Low-income families can't afford $10 water; spikes feel exploitative

Anti-cap: Capped prices → no incentive for suppliers to rush in → shortages persist

"This is where economics meets ethics. The model shows capping prices creates shortages. But is that the whole story? We'll formalize this in Topic 7 with price ceiling diagrams."

Debrief: The Price Mechanism

When water prices spike, the price signal simultaneously tells consumers "conserve" and suppliers "rush more in."

Cap the price, and you mute that signal . The market loses its ability to self-correct.

Topic 7 preview: We'll draw price ceiling diagrams and measure the welfare consequences — deadweight loss, misallocation, and black markets.

"Whether we SHOULD let prices rise in emergencies is normative. How the mechanism WORKS is positive — and that's what this chapter equips you to analyze."

Key Takeaways

1. Markets coordinate through prices. Scarce → price ↑ → rations demand, incentivizes supply.

2. Demand ↓, Supply ↑. Law of demand + law of supply create the tension that makes markets work.

3. Shift vs. movement is everything. Price changes → move along. Everything else → curve shifts.

4. Equilibrium = where curves cross. Surpluses push P ↓, shortages push P ↑. Markets self-correct.

5. Comparative statics is your prediction tool. Identify the shift, trace the new equilibrium. When both curves shift, one variable is ambiguous.

"Five sentences that capture the whole chapter. If you remember nothing else, remember #3 — shift vs. movement is the key."
