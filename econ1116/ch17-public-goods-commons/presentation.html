Section 1 · LO 17.1

Classifying Goods: Two Questions, Four Boxes

Whether a market works depends on two simple properties.

Two Definitions to Lock In

Excludability

Can the supplier prevent non-payers from consuming it? A movie ticket: yes. A streetlight: no — you can’t stop someone seeing by its glow.

Rivalry

Does one person’s use reduce what’s left for others? A burrito: yes (eat it, it’s gone). A podcast: no — your listening doesn’t diminish mine.

Markets work beautifully when a good is both excludable and rival. Relax either one and trouble starts.

These two yes/no questions are the entire chapter's scaffolding. Excludability is about the SELLER's power to charge; rivalry is about whether the good gets "used up." Private goods (pizza) are yes/yes — that's everything from Ch 4–13. The other three boxes are where markets struggle.

The Two-by-Two Matrix

Private — rival + excludable. Markets nail these.

Club — excludable, not rival. Firms profit (Netflix, tolls).

Common resource — rival, can’t exclude. Overused (§17.3).

Public — neither. Underprovided (§17.2).

Note the diagonal pattern of failure: the two non-excludable boxes (top-right, bottom-right) are where markets break — common resources get OVERused (too much), public goods get UNDERprovided (too little). Non-excludability is the common culprit. Club goods work because excludability lets firms charge even though the good isn't rival.

The Lines Are Blurrier Than They Look

ChatGPT, pre-2024 — paywall = club good (excludable, non-rival).

ChatGPT free tier at peak hours — anyone signs up (non-excludable) but caps bite as compute gets scarce → common resource.

Open-weight models (Llama 4) — download once, non-rival & non-excludable → public good... until you need a $40k GPU to run it.

Common misconception: “A good is permanently public or private.” No — goods move between boxes as tech and policy change. NYC’s roads became excludable overnight when the $9 toll began.

The matrix is a snapshot of current institutions, not a fixed label. The same underlying thing (a road, an AI model) can sit in different boxes depending on pricing and capacity. This flexibility is the lever policy pulls: congestion pricing literally moves a road from common-resource to club-good by adding excludability.

Think Like an Economist: Boston Common

On a quiet Tuesday, Boston Common is a public good — your stroll doesn’t reduce anyone else’s.

On a packed July 4th — blankets edge to edge, trash overflowing — it becomes rival. Now it’s a common resource.

Where on the matrix does it sit? Does your answer depend on the day?

The point: rivalry is often a matter of CONGESTION. Many "public goods" are really congestible — non-rival until crowded, then rival. Roads, parks, bandwidth, national forests. This is exactly why congestion pricing exists. Let students argue both sides for ~60 seconds.

Your Turn — Classify the Good

A fireworks display over the Charles River. Anyone can watch from anywhere, and your watching doesn’t block mine. What kind of good is it?

A. Private good
B. Club good
C. Common resource
D. Public good
Answer: D. Non-excludable (can't stop anyone from looking up) and non-rival (your view doesn't diminish mine) = public good. This is the textbook example of why fireworks are almost always publicly funded or sponsored — no firm could sell tickets to the sky. Contrast with a stadium concert (excludable = club/private).

Section 2 · LO 17.2

Public Goods & the Free-Rider Problem

When everyone benefits whether they pay or not, who pays?

The Free-Rider Problem

The tendency to enjoy a non-excludable good without paying for it — because your consumption can’t be prevented whether you contribute or not.

Each person’s contribution is tiny relative to total cost...

...and each captures only a sliver of total benefit. So everyone waits for someone else.

Result: the good is underprovided — or not provided at all.

The mirror of the commons problem coming in Section 3. Public goods are UNDERprovided (too little); common resources are OVERused (too much). Both stem from non-excludability. The free rider isn't being stupid — given everyone else's choices, free-riding is individually rational. That's what makes it a genuine market failure, not a moral failing.

Why Markets Underprovide: The Fountain

200 neighbors value a fountain at $100 each → $20,000 total benefit.

Contractor quotes $7,000. Clearly passes the cost-benefit test.

Pass the hat: only $3,000 comes in. “My $35 won’t make or break it.” No fountain.

Everyone reasons the same way — "if others pay I still enjoy it, and my share is too small to matter" — so contributions fall far short of the $7,000 needed, even though the project would make everyone better off. This is the core inefficiency. The fix (government taxes everyone $35) appears two slides on.

The Same Logic at National Scale

National defense — perfectly non-rival & non-excludable. US spent $886B in FY2024. No firm could charge for it.

NATO free-riding — for decades the US over-paid while allies skipped the 2% target. Only after Ukraine did 23 of 32 finally meet it.

GPS — built & run by the US military, free to all. ~$1.4 trillion/yr in value (NIST). Rideshare, shipping, farming all free-ride.

Basic research — the seed of medicine & new materials. Government funds ~25%; firms prefer patentable applied R&D.

Free-riding scales from a block party to an alliance of nations. The threat had to become real before most allies stopped free-riding.

NATO is the cleanest real-world free-rider story: each ally benefits from the American umbrella whether it pays or not, so most underpaid for decades. GPS is the cleanest "government creates massive value" story — $1.4T/yr from a system no private firm would build because it can't charge for the signal. Basic research: 2026 budgets proposed cutting NSF 54% — a live debate about underproviding a public good.

Demand for a Public Good: Sum Vertically

Private good: add quantities horizontally. Everyone buys their own amount.

Public good: everyone consumes the same unit → add willingness to pay vertically.

Efficient where social WTP = MC → 5 days. But neither Leo nor Rachel alone would pay $5.

The key conceptual flip from Ch 4. Because the good is non-rival, the social value of one more unit is the SUM of what everyone would pay for that same unit — so we stack WTP vertically. The efficient point (social WTP = MC, 5 days) is unreachable through voluntary payment because each person's private WTP sits below MC. That gap is exactly the free-rider problem, drawn.

Can It Be Fixed? Private vs. Public

Private patches — social pressure ("if everyone gave $3..."), warm-glow giving, and selective incentives (tote bags, member-only hikes — Olson 1965).

But imperfect: Wikipedia’s 97% still free-ride; firms fund little basic science.

Government solution — tax everyone $35, collect $7,000, build the fountain. Each pays less than their $100 value → everyone wins.

Warm-glow utility = you get private satisfaction from the act of giving itself, independent of the outcome — that's why donation drives work at all. Selective incentives (Olson): bundle an excludable private perk with the public good. Government's tool is compulsion: make payment mandatory via taxation, which is the only thing that reliably defeats free-riding at scale — defense, schools, GPS, NIH/NSF grants.

Section 3 · LO 17.3

Common Resources & the Tragedy of the Commons

Public goods: too little. Common resources: too much.

Hardin’s Parable (1968)

Tragedy of the commons — a shared resource gets overused because each user gets the full private benefit but bears only a fraction of the cost.

A pasture open to all. Each herder gains the whole value of one more cow; the overgrazing cost is spread across everyone.

Each adds another cow — and the pasture collapses for all.

Hardin's 1968 Science essay. The structure: private benefit concentrated, cost socialized. It's the same wedge as a negative externality (Ch 16), but with NO market at all — just open access. Note Hardin himself argued only privatization or top-down control could fix it; Ostrom later proved him incomplete (coming up).

The Commons Is Everywhere (2024–26)

Atlantic cod — collapsed ~1992, 32-yr moratorium, 40,000 jobs. Reopened 2024 — still not recovered.

Pacific bluefin tuna — strict international quotas rebuilt the stock a decade early; 2025 US quota +80%.

Colorado River — over-allocated since 1922; seven states deadlocked as 2026 guidelines expire. Each acts in self-interest.

Orbital space — 10,000+ satellites; cascading-collision (Kessler) risk threatens access for everyone.

A congested road is a commons too — until a toll makes it excludable. That’s the Ch 16 link.

The cod vs tuna contrast is the spine of the section — same kind of fish, opposite outcomes, decided by governance. Groundwater (Ogallala, Central Valley) is the invisible version — the tragedy plays out underground until wells run dry. Orbital debris is the newest commons. Tie the road example back to last chapter's congestion pricing.

Why Rational Individuals Ruin It

Each fisher thinks: “If I leave fish behind, someone else just catches them. I’m better off fishing more.”

Marginal private benefit of one more unit > marginal private cost — because the depletion cost is external.

It’s the prisoner’s dilemma from Ch 13 — “defect” (overuse) dominates, so everyone overuses.

When everyone plays their dominant strategy, the resource collapses — even though all would be better off cooperating.

Explicitly connect to Ch 13: each fisher's dominant strategy is to overfish regardless of what others do, so the Nash equilibrium is collective overharvest. This is why the climate free-rider problem (end of chapter) is structurally the same game played by nations. The numerical version is next.

The Fishing Commons, by the Numbers

Social optimum ≈ 40 hrs: catch peaks at 240 fish (24 each).

But if the other 9 fish 4 hrs, you gain by fishing 6. Everyone does → 80+ hrs.

Past max sustainable yield — exactly what sank the Grand Banks cod.

Walk the table: 20hr→160, 40hr→240 (peak), 60hr→240, 80hr→160, 100hr→100. The individual's marginal catch from extra hours stays positive even as TOTAL efficiency falls — that wedge between private and social return is the engine of the tragedy. Grand Banks: total allowable catch was set above scientific advice year after year because each community lobbied for its share.

Three Fixes — and Ostrom’s Third Way

1. Regulation — quotas, taxes, tradable permits (ITQs: Iceland 1984, New Zealand).

2. Privatization — one owner internalizes depletion. Works for grazing land; fails for open ocean.

3. Community self-governance — users write & enforce their own rules. Maine lobster co-ops: trap limits, V-notching, social sanctions — thriving 100+ years.

Elinor Ostrom (Nobel 2009) documented 800+ successful community commons — refuting Hardin’s “only markets or government” claim.

Ostrom's 8 design principles: clear boundaries, proportional costs/benefits, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, recognized self-governance rights, nested enterprises. Key enabler: small, stable groups can monitor each other and sanction cheaters — impossible in vast anonymous settings like the open ocean. That's the dividing line for when Hardin's prescription (privatize/regulate) is still better.

When Cooperation Fails: The Plastics Treaty

Plastics Treaty (Aug 2025) — collapsed

8M tons of plastic enter the ocean/yr. 170+ nations; oil producers blocked binding limits. Thousands of producers, few substitutes, huge stakes.

Montreal Protocol — succeeded

Phased out CFCs. Few producers, ready substitutes, modest compliance cost relative to benefit.

The design of cooperation matters as much as the intent. Same prisoner’s dilemma — very different payoffs.

A global commons is the hardest case: no one owns the ocean, so each nation's waste imposes costs on all others — the PD played by 170 countries. Montreal worked because the conditions favored a deal (small N, cheap substitutes). Plastics has the opposite conditions. This previews the climate free-rider problem at the end of the chapter.

Your Turn — Save the Lobster

Maine’s lobster fishery has thrived for a century with no federal manager — fishers set trap limits and enforce them socially. This is closest to which solution?

A. Government regulation
B. Privatization (single owner)
C. Ostrom-style self-governance
D. Do nothing — open access
Answer: C. The defining feature is that the USERS themselves design and enforce the rules through monitoring and social sanctions — Ostrom's third way, not top-down regulation (A) or a single private owner (B). It works because the community is small and stable enough to watch each other — Ostrom's monitoring + graduated-sanctions principles in action.

Section 4 · LO 17.4

Cost-Benefit Analysis: How Governments Decide

No prices to follow — so estimate costs and benefits directly.

The Four Steps of CBA

1. Identify all costs & benefits — direct and the indirect ones easy to overlook.

2. Monetize everything — even clean air & saved time. The hard, controversial step.

3. Discount future flows to present value (Ch 15) — the rate can flip the verdict.

4. Compare & decide — do it if net benefit > 0; rank rivals by net benefit per dollar.

Deceptively simple rule, genuinely hard execution — Steps 2 and 3 are where the fights happen.

Step 3 is from Ch 15: a lower discount rate makes far-future benefits loom larger, which is why climate and big infrastructure are so sensitive to the rate chosen. Step 2 is the ethics minefield — you have to put a number on a statistical life and a clean river. Both choices are technical AND political.

A Real CBA: NYC Congestion Pricing

Benefits: −11% traffic, +23% faster commute, −22% PM2.5, −7% crashes, +7.7% transit. $550M+ revenue → $15B transit capital.

Costs: $9/entry, hitting lower-income drivers hardest; higher delivery costs; fierce political backlash.

Aggregate math: benefits >> costs. But CBA can’t say if the distribution is fair.

A federal judge ruled the attempt to rescind it "arbitrary and capricious" (March 2026). The teaching point: CBA tells you the pie got bigger, not how the slices are divided. The same toll that is efficient in aggregate is regressive in dollar terms — the efficiency-equity tension from Ch 5 returns. Last chapter we saw the toll as a Pigouvian price; here we see how the city justified it.

Valuing the Invaluable

Contingent valuation

Survey: “What would you pay to prevent an oil spill?” Used to assess Exxon Valdez damages. Risk: hypothetical ≠ real.

Hedonic pricing

Infer value from markets: homes near parks cost more; risky jobs pay more (Ch 14) → the value of safety.

EPA value of a statistical life

$11.8M

Social cost of carbon

$190 / ton

The VSL is NOT the value of a specific person — it's aggregate willingness to pay for small mortality-risk reductions, scaled across the population. These two numbers (VSL $11.8M, SCC $190 from Ch 16) drive billions in regulatory CBA — power plants, fuel standards, water rules. Whoever sets these numbers sets policy, which is why they're politically contested.

Section 5 · LO 17.5

Government Failure & Public Choice

What if politicians are just as self-interested as everyone else?

Public Choice: The Symmetry Assumption

Government failure — intervention that fails to improve efficiency, or makes it worse, because politicians’ & bureaucrats’ incentives diverge from the public interest.

Market failure justifies intervention — but only if government does better. Public choice applies the self-interest model to politics.

The intellectual move: don't compare a flawed market to a PERFECT government — compare flawed to flawed. Public choice (Buchanan, Tullock) assumes voters, politicians, and bureaucrats all respond to incentives, just like consumers and firms. That symmetry is what generates the predictable failure modes on the next slides.

Rent-Seeking

Spending resources to capture a transfer from government rather than create value (Ch 11).

Spend $10M lobbying for a tariff worth $50M? The $10M is pure waste — deadweight loss.

US federal lobbying, 2024: a record $4.4 billion. Pharma alone: $451.8M. Realtors (top org): $86.3M.

The waste isn't the transfer itself (that's a wash — one party's loss is another's gain). It's the REAL RESOURCES — lawyers, lobbyists, executive time — burned fighting over the transfer instead of producing anything. Multiply across thousands of interest groups. Same deadweight-loss logic as monopoly (Ch 11), now applied to politics.

Regulatory Capture

A regulator created to serve the public comes to be dominated by the industry it regulates — writing rules that benefit the industry at consumers’ expense.

George Stigler (Nobel 1982) formalized it. Classic case: FAA & Boeing after the 737 MAX crashes.

The “revolving door” — regulators take industry jobs and back — quietly rewards lax enforcement.

Capture happens because the industry has concentrated, intense interest in the rules while the public's interest is diffuse — the same asymmetry that drives rent-seeking. The revolving door is the mechanism: career incentives make regulators reluctant to antagonize their future employers. Sets up the concentrated-benefits/diffuse-costs slide.

The Median Voter Theorem

Majority rule on one issue → the median voter’s choice wins (Black 1948, Downs 1957).

Candidates converge to the center — extremes get ignored.

The median voter has no reason to weigh anyone else’s value → the efficient level is hit only by luck.

Five voters wanting $0/$10k/$20k/$30k/$100k — $20k beats every alternative head-to-head, so the median wins regardless of how intensely the $100k voter cares. That's the limitation: majority voting counts noses, not intensity of preference, so it ignores the total-social-benefit-=-total-cost condition for efficient public-good provision. Explains centrist convergence in two-party systems.

Concentrated Benefits, Diffuse Costs

A sugar tariff costs each household ~$10/yr (you shrug) but hands a few producers millions (they lobby furiously). The tariff survives — even though total cost > total benefit.

Rational ignorance — your one vote won’t decide anything, so learning the issue isn’t worth it. Organized interests fill the void.

Buy a car: you bear the full cost, so you research hard. Vote on a bond: payoff to research ≈ zero.

This is arguably public choice's single most important insight — it explains the persistence of sugar tariffs, the Jones Act, ethanol mandates, farm subsidies. The asymmetry: beneficiaries are few and organized, payers are many and inattentive. Rational ignorance isn't stupidity; it's a rational response to a near-zero probability of being decisive.

So When Does Government Work?

The question is never “is government perfect?” It’s “is government failure less costly than the market failure it fixes?”

Clearly worth it: defense, basic research, environment, public health. Infrastructure saves ~$700/household/yr (ASCE).

Often not: subsidies to connected industries, rules written by the regulated. Captured programs.

Not anti- or pro-government — institutions matter. Transparency & accountability shrink failure; Ostrom’s principles apply to democracy too.

The balanced landing: government failure is real but doesn't prove markets do better — it's always a comparative judgment. CBO estimates ~5% return on incremental public capital. The lesson is institutional design: the same features that make Ostrom's commons work (monitoring, accountability, aligned incentives) reduce government failure. Sets up the threads.

Two Threads: Climate & AI as Public Goods

A stable climate

Non-rival, non-excludable → a global public good. Every country wants others to cut — the free-rider problem with 190 players.

Open-source AI & its “public bad”

Llama/Gemma: billions in shared value. But AI misinformation is a non-excludable harm — #1 short-term global risk (WEF 2025).

Information quality may be the most important — and most threatened — public good of the digital age.

Climate is BOTH an externality (Ch 16) and a public-good/free-rider problem (Ch 17) — which is why it's so intractable. AI flips the public-good frame: open models are a positive public good, while misinformation is a "public bad" (non-excludable, harmful). Bridges to Ch 18 (information/behavioral) and Ch 19 (platforms).

Key Takeaways

1. Two questions — rivalry & excludability — sort every good into private, club, common, or public.

2. Public goods are underprovided by the free-rider problem; the standard fix is tax-funded provision.

3. Common resources are overused — the tragedy of the commons. Fix: regulation, privatization, or Ostrom self-governance.

4. Cost-benefit analysis guides provision — but monetizing & discounting are contested, and it’s silent on equity.

5. Government failure — rent-seeking, capture, median voter, rational ignorance — means intervention isn’t a free fix.

6. The real question: is government failure less costly than the market failure? Institutions decide.

Next time: Chapter 18 — Information, Uncertainty & Behavioral Economics. When we don’t know — or don’t act rationally.

The unifying idea: non-excludability is the thread — it underprovides public goods and overuses commons. Every solution (taxes, permits, property rights, community rules, CBA) is a way to supply the coordination the market can't. And government is one tool among several, not a guaranteed fix.
