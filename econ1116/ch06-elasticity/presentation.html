Section 1

Price Elasticity of Demand

Measuring how much consumers respond

What is Elasticity?

The law of demand tells us that consumers respond to price changes. Elasticity measures how much.

It is the degree of responsiveness of one variable (quantity) to changes in another (price or income).

Key Insight

Think of it as a sensitivity test.

Necessity / Inelastic

Price +10% → quantity drops −1%  (stuck)

Luxury / Elastic

Price +10% → quantity drops −20%  (flexible)

Anchor the concept before any math. Gasoline = inelastic (you still need to get to work); plane tickets = elastic (vacations can be skipped, postponed, or substituted with road trips). The “sensitivity test” framing carries through every elasticity in this chapter — price, cross-price, income, supply.

Math Refresher — What does a ratio mean?

A ratio is just a comparison of two numbers: how big is \(A\) relative to \(B\)? Write it \(A/B\) (read “\(A\) to \(B\)” or “\(A\) per \(B\)”).

The idea

\(\dfrac{A}{B} = 2\)

“\(A\) is twice \(B\).”

\(\dfrac{A}{B} = 0.5\)

“\(A\) is half of \(B\).”

\(\dfrac{A}{B} = 1\)

“\(A\) and \(B\) are equal.”

Apple \$1, water bottle \$2: ratio \(= 2/1 = 2\) — water is 2× the apple.

MBTA fare hike (July 2025)

New subway fare \(p_{\text{new}} = \$2.90\)
Old subway fare \(p_{\text{old}} = \$2.40\)

Ratio \(\dfrac{p_{\text{new}}}{p_{\text{old}}} = \dfrac{2.90}{2.40} \approx \mathbf{1.208}\)

“The new fare is 120.8% of the old fare” — or equivalently, “\$1.21 of new per \$1.00 of old.”

Order matters. \(2.90/2.40 = 1.208\) is “new per old.” Flipping it — \(2.40/2.90 \approx 0.828\) — says the old fare was 82.8% of the new. Same data, different ratio, different statement.

Anchor: students need to read ratios as comparisons, not raw numbers. The 1.208 is meaningful only because we know what's on top and bottom. This sets up elasticity, which is a ratio of two percentage changes — order matters there too.

Math Refresher — Percentage change

\(\displaystyle \%\Delta = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100\%\)

MBTA — continued

Numerator: \(p_{\text{new}} - p_{\text{old}} = 2.90 - 2.40 = \mathbf{\$0.50}\)
Denominator: \(p_{\text{old}} = \mathbf{\$2.40}\)

\(\%\Delta p = \dfrac{0.50}{2.40} \approx 0.2083 = \mathbf{+20.83\%}\)
(a price increase)

Why “old” in the denominator?

We want the change relative to the starting point — \$0.50 from \$2.40 is meaningfully different from \$0.50 from \$50.

Sign convention

\(\%\Delta > 0\) rise · \(\%\Delta < 0\) fall · \(\%\Delta = 0\) no change

Building block of elasticity: \(\;E_d = \dfrac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P}\) — two percentage changes divided by each other. We'll refine this with the midpoint method on the next slide.

Connect explicitly: the MBTA fare ratio (previous slide) was the building block; the percentage-change formula here is the operational version. When we write elasticity as \(\%\Delta Q / \%\Delta P\), each numerator and denominator is a (new − old) / old calculation. Real-world: MBTA published ridership data shows weekday subway boardings dropped from ~510K to ~500K after the hike — students can estimate the implied elasticity on their own.

Price Elasticity of Demand

$$E_d = \left|\frac{\%\;\Delta\; Q_d}{\%\;\Delta\; P}\right|$$

Absolute value — always positive (law of demand guarantees negative ratio)

Eggs: 10% price ↑ → 1% quantity ↓

E d = 0.1 — inelastic

Streaming: 10% price ↑ → 15% subscribers ↓

E d = 1.5 — elastic

Key intuition: elasticity puts the RESPONSE (quantity) on top and the CAUSE (price) on the bottom. It's a ratio of percentages, not absolute numbers — that's what makes it comparable across goods.

Your costs (including the opportunity cost of your time) are rising — you may want to raise prices.

Issue 2 — Competition

A high level of competition may require you to lower your prices.

The law of demand asks:

If we increase the price — how many customers will we lose?  ·  If we decrease the price — how many will we gain?

Frame the chapter as a founder's problem. Two real pressures — rising costs and competition — pull price in opposite directions. The law of demand says price up → consumers down (and vice versa), but it doesn't tell us how many we'd lose or gain. That's elasticity. So yes, you can raise the price and total revenue will go UP (we'll prove that in §6.2). Quick check with the class: at $25 × 100 = $2,500; at $30 × 90 = $2,700. Revenue went up by $200. Reading the PED Number

PED measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a change in price.

  • Always a negative number — that's why we use the absolute value.
  • A larger number (in absolute value) means greater elasticity.
  • It is unit free — just a ratio of percentages, comparable across goods.

Example (from A to B)

An elasticity of 0.5 means: for every 1% increase in price, quantity demanded falls by 0.5%.

Inelastic ( |PED| < 1 ) — quantity reacts less than price · Elastic ( |PED| > 1 ) — quantity reacts more.

PED = |−0.5| = 0.5
Three lessons on reading the PED number: the sign (always negative for normal goods, so we drop it with absolute value), the magnitude (bigger = more responsive), and the unit-free property (a 0.5 here is comparable to a 0.5 on coffee or housing). Walk the diagram: A is the starting point ($25, 100), B is where we'd land if we raise price ($30, 90). The vertical 20% bracket is the price hike; the −10% horizontal bracket is the customer loss. Reinforce the example reading: "for every 1% price increase, quantity falls by 0.5%." That's exactly what inelastic means — quantity moves less than price.

Price: $30

Same data,
reverse direction

Consumer reaction from B to A:

\(\text{PED} = \dfrac{\%\Delta Q_D}{\%\Delta P}\)

\(\%\Delta Q_D = \dfrac{100 - 90}{90} \times 100 = \mathbf{+11.11\%}\)

\(\%\Delta P = \dfrac{25 - 30}{30} \times 100 = \mathbf{-16.67\%}\)

\(\text{PED} = \dfrac{+11.11\%}{-16.67\%} = \mathbf{-0.67} \;\;\color{#dc2626}{\boldsymbol{\neq -0.5}}\)

  We get a different value of PED for the same set of numbers — just because we are moving in the opposite direction.

PED = |−0.67| = 0.67
Reverse the direction (price falls from $30 to $25 instead of rising). Same two points on the same demand curve — but the percentages flip: now −16.67% on price (because we divide by the larger old value $30), and +11.11% on quantity. PED = 11.11 / −16.67 = −0.67. Take absolute value: 0.67. But the A→B calculation on the previous slide gave 0.5. Same data, different answer — just because we changed direction. This is the base method's directional asymmetry. The midpoint method (next slide) fixes it by using the average of the two values as the denominator, so direction no longer matters.

Midpoint Method — One Answer Either Way

Base method, A→B

|PED| = 0.50

Base method, B→A

|PED| = 0.67

Goal

A single number for both directions

The fix

Use the average of the two values as the base — not the “old” one.

Midpoint Formula — memorize this

\(\displaystyle \color{white}{\%\Delta = \frac{\text{New} - \text{Old}}{\dfrac{\text{New} + \text{Old}}{2}} \times 100}\)

Avg price = \(\dfrac{25 + 30}{2} = \mathbf{\$27.50}\) · Avg quantity = \(\dfrac{100 + 90}{2} = \mathbf{95}\)

Apply to Husky Tumbler

\(\%\Delta Q = \dfrac{90 - 100}{95} \times 100 = \mathbf{-10.53\%}\)

\(\%\Delta P = \dfrac{30 - 25}{27.5} \times 100 = \mathbf{+18.18\%}\)

\(\text{PED} = \left|\dfrac{-10.53\%}{+18.18\%}\right| = \mathbf{0.58}\)

  Reverse the direction (B→A) and you get the same 0.58 — the asymmetry is gone.

Sequential reveal. Top recap (always visible) sets the problem: 0.50 vs 0.67. Step 1: introduce the fix — use the average. Step 2: the emphasized formula card — this is THE formula to memorize. Step 3: show the two averages ($27.50 and 95). Then on the right, step through the application: %ΔQ, %ΔP, the final PED = 0.58, and finally the reversibility check. Punchline: 0.58 sits between the base method's 0.50 and 0.67 — the symmetric compromise. Reverse direction and the answer is invariant. This formula is what students use on homework and exams.

What Determines Elasticity?

Four factors push demand toward elastic (responsive) or inelastic (sticky). Press ↓ to dive into each.

 Substitutability (+ market definition)

The more substitutes, the more elastic. Froot Loops → elastic (any cereal). Airfare → inelastic (no substitute for flying).

 Proportion of Income

The smaller the share of income, the more inelastic. Salt, toothpaste, gum → you don't shop around for cheaper.

 Luxuries vs. Necessities

Necessities (electricity, water, gas) → inelastic. Luxuries (Vision Pro, vacations) → elastic.

 Time Period

Short run → inelastic (can't switch cars overnight). Long run → elastic (buy EV, move closer to work).

Overview slide. Ask: "Which of these four factors is the most important?" (Answer: substitutes.) Then press ↓ to walk through each in detail. The next four vertical slides give a worked example for each factor before returning to the main flow with the → key.

Substitutability (& Market Definition)

The more substitutes a product has, the more elastic its demand.

Many substitutes → elastic

Froot Loops have many close substitutes (Cheerios, Chex, Cinnamon Toast Crunch...). If Kellogg's raises the price, buyers easily switch.

No close substitute → inelastic

Flying coast-to-coast has no close substitute (driving takes days, trains too slow). A price hike barely dents demand.

Market definition matters. “Froot Loops” (narrow) → elastic. “Cereal in general” (broader) → less elastic. “All breakfast food” (broadest) → very inelastic. Narrower categories have more substitutes outside them.

Price elasticity is higher when close substitutes are available.

Substitutability is THE most important determinant. Walk Froot Loops vs. airfare: the cereal aisle photo shows dozens of substitutes, while the plane has none. Then explain how market definition is the SAME idea: narrow market = more outside substitutes; broad market = fewer.

Proportion of Income

The smaller the share of income spent on a product, the lower its elasticity.

Example: salt, toothpaste, gum.

Consumers don't notice or care about minor price changes.

It's inconvenient to search for substitutes for low-cost items.

Perceived savings are trivial next to rent or groceries.

Even a 50% price hike on gum (\$1 → \$1.50) is only 50 cents — not worth comparing brands or walking to another store. Same for salt (a few dollars a year). Compare with cars (5-10% of annual income) — price-sensitive shoppers comparison-shop for months. The threshold is psychological, not economic.

Luxuries vs. Necessities

Luxuries have more elastic demand than necessities.

Example: Basic utilities vs. high-end electronics.

Necessity → inelastic

  

Electricity, water, and gas are indispensable. Consumers have limited ability to cut back even when prices rise.

Luxury → elastic

 

Premium smartphones, Apple Vision Pro, high-tech gadgets are desirable but not essential — demand drops sharply when prices rise.

The line between “necessity” and “luxury” depends on the buyer — not the product itself.

The classification is psychological, not objective. To a high-income household, the Vision Pro might feel like a necessity; to a low-income household, even cable TV might be a luxury. Ask the class: "Is a smartphone a necessity or luxury?" (For most Americans, increasingly a necessity for work and school.)

Time Period

When consumers have time to adjust their consumption, demand becomes more elastic.

Example: Gasoline, short run vs. long run.

Short Run (SR) → inelastic

 

Gas prices spike today — you still drive to work tomorrow. Not sensitive to gas price.

10 % rise → ~2.5 % drop after 1 year

Long Run (LR) → elastic

 

Over years, people buy EVs, move closer to work, switch to transit. Sensitive to gas price.

10 % rise → ~6 % drop after 5 years

Price elasticity is higher in the long run.

The classic U.S. gas-price example. When 2008 gas spiked to \$4/gal, short-run consumption barely moved (everyone still had to commute). But by 2012, hybrid sales had doubled and people had downsized cars — long-run adjustments made demand much more elastic. The lesson: any policy targeting demand (gas tax, carbon tax) underestimates its effect if it only looks at SR data.

Elasticity and Total Revenue

Back to the start-up: Custom Tumbler.

Your costs are rising (including the opportunity cost of your time) — you may want to raise prices.

Elasticity tells you how many fewer sales a price hike will cost you.

The real question: how much will your total revenue fall — or might it actually rise?

Re-anchor in the start-up story before we tackle the total-revenue test. The Husky Tumbler founder knows elasticity = 1.5 (elastic). The next question is: if I raise the price, does revenue go UP (smaller Q drop) or DOWN (bigger Q drop)? That depends on the elasticity number we just learned to compute. This slide is the bridge from "what is elasticity" (§6.1) to "why it matters" (§6.2 Total Revenue).

Total Revenue: Elastic Market

\(\text{TR} = P \times Q\). For Tumbler with PED = 2.75 (elastic), should we raise or lower the price?

  Price ↑ ( $25 → $30 )

  Price ↓ ( $30 → $25 )

\(P = \$30, Q = 60 \Rightarrow \text{TR} = \mathbf{\$1{,}800}\)
vs. \(P = \$25, Q = 100 \Rightarrow \text{TR} = \mathbf{\$2{,}500}\)

Price ↑ ⇒ revenue falls.
Price ↓ ⇒ revenue rises.

Walk the rectangles slowly. Old TR = \$25 × 100 = \$2,500 (full rectangle bottom-left to (100, $25)). New TR after price raise = \$30 × 60 = \$1,800. The GREEN rectangle is the “price effect”: \$5 extra on the 60 units you still sell = +\$300. The RED rectangle is the “quantity effect”: 40 lost units at \$25 each = −\$1,000. Since demand is elastic, the RED dominates — revenue falls by \$700. Same logic in reverse for the price decrease. Pedagogy rule: when D is elastic, MOVE PRICE THE OPPOSITE WAY of where you want revenue.

Total Revenue: Inelastic Market

Same Tumbler scenario, but during finals week the customer reaction is weak — PED = 0.14 (inelastic). Now what?

  Price ↑ ( $25 → $30 )

  Price ↓ ( $30 → $25 )

\(P = \$30, Q = 90 \Rightarrow \text{TR} = \mathbf{\$2{,}700}\)
vs. \(P = \$25, Q = 100 \Rightarrow \text{TR} = \mathbf{\$2{,}500}\)

Price ↑ ⇒ revenue rises.
Price ↓ ⇒ revenue falls.

Inelastic case — the OPPOSITE rule applies. Now the GREEN price-effect rectangle (\$5 × 90 = \$450) is bigger than the RED quantity-effect (\$25 × 10 = \$250). Raising price ADDS \$200 to revenue. Same logic flips for a price cut: revenue falls by \$200. Pedagogy rule: when D is inelastic, MOVE PRICE THE SAME WAY as where you want revenue. This is why monopolists, utilities, and insulin makers can squeeze price — their customers are stuck.

The Total Revenue Test

TR = P × Q

Two competing forces when price rises:

Price Effect

Higher price per unit → revenue up

Quantity Effect

Fewer units sold → revenue down

Demand Price ↑ Price ↓ Winner
Elastic (E d > 1) TR falls TR rises Quantity effect
Unit elastic TR unchanged TR unchanged Tie
Inelastic (E d < 1) TR rises TR falls Price effect
This is the core business insight. Draw the P×Q rectangle on the board. When price rises, the rectangle gets taller but narrower. Whether area grows or shrinks depends on elasticity.

Elasticity Along a Linear Demand Curve

The slope is constant along a straight demand line — but the elasticity is not.

Slope = \(\dfrac{0 - 30}{60 - 0} = \mathbf{-0.5}\) — same everywhere on the line.

Top segment — ($30, 0) → ($20, 20)

\(E_d = \dfrac{|20-0| / \tfrac{20+0}{2}}{|20-30| / \tfrac{20+30}{2}} = \dfrac{20/10}{10/25} = \mathbf{5}\) — elastic

Middle segment — ($20, 20) → ($10, 40)

\(E_d = \dfrac{|40-20| / \tfrac{40+20}{2}}{|10-20| / \tfrac{10+20}{2}} = \dfrac{20/30}{10/15} = \mathbf{1}\) — unit elastic

Bottom segment — ($10, 40) → ($0, 60)

\(E_d = \dfrac{|60-40| / \tfrac{60+40}{2}}{|0-10| / \tfrac{0+10}{2}} = \dfrac{20/50}{10/5} = \mathbf{0.20}\) — inelastic

The pattern: high price & low quantity (top) → elastic. Low price & high quantity (bottom) → inelastic. Same slope, very different responsiveness.

Sequential reveal. Initial: just the demand line + endpoints. Step 1: top segment ($30,0)→($20,20) highlighted purple + E=5 calc reveals on the right. Step 2: middle ($20,20)→($10,40) highlighted green + E=1 calc. Step 3: bottom ($10,40)→($0,60) highlighted brown + E=0.20 calc. Step 4: pattern insight. Counter-intuitive point: linear demand has CONSTANT slope but CHANGING elasticity. Near the top a 10-unit Q move is a huge percentage; near the bottom the same 10 units is tiny. Monopolists almost never operate on the inelastic portion (they could raise price AND grow revenue).

Common Misconception

 WRONG

“Elasticity and slope are the same thing.”

 RIGHT

Slope = ratio of absolute changes (fixed).
Elasticity = ratio of percentage changes (varies along the curve).

Why it matters: On a straight-line demand curve, demand is elastic at high prices (top) and inelastic at low prices (bottom). Firms need to know where they are on the curve.

This is one of the hardest distinctions for students. Draw a linear demand curve and mark the midpoint — above is elastic, below is inelastic. Same slope everywhere, but elasticity changes!

Total Revenue: Two Stories

Campus Coffee (Elastic, E d = 1.11)

Before: $4.50 × 200 = $900/day

After: $5.50 × 160 = $880/day

TR fell $20

Quantity effect won. Bad pricing decision.

Eggs (Inelastic, E d = 0.03)

Before: $2.50 × 100M = $250M

After: $5.00 × 98M = $490M

TR nearly doubled

Price effect won. Supply shock increased revenue.

This is the key contrast. Same logic, opposite outcomes. Campus Coffee lost revenue by raising prices (elastic). Egg farmers gained revenue from the supply shock (inelastic). This is why elasticity matters for business strategy.

Netflix: A Total Revenue Masterclass

Inelastic Segment

Standard plan: $15.49 → $18.99

Result: +50M subscribers

Revenue: $34 billion

Netflix became a household necessity. Price hikes raised TR.

Elastic Segment

Ad tier: $7.99/month

Captured: 94M users

Monetizes via: advertising

Price-sensitive consumers get a lower price. Netflix keeps them.

Strategy: Charge more to inelastic consumers (ad-free). Retain elastic consumers at a lower price (ads). Maximize total revenue from both segments.

This is tiered pricing based on elasticity segmentation. Students love this example because they all use Netflix. Ask: "Which tier are you on? What does that say about YOUR elasticity?"

The Farming Paradox

Why can good news for farmers be bad news for farmers?

 New wheat variety boosts yields 20% → supply shifts right

 Demand for wheat is inelastic — people don’t eat much more bread when it’s cheaper

 Price drop (%) exceeds quantity increase (%) → TR falls

 This is why farmers historically receive government subsidies

This counterintuitive result confuses students every year. The key: with inelastic demand, increasing supply makes prices fall MORE than quantity rises. Total revenue shrinks despite producing more.

Cross-Price & Income Elasticity

How demand responds to other prices and income

Salad + Dressing — What Goes Together?

Quick visual hook. Salad and dressing are textbook complements — you almost never buy one without considering the other. When salad gets cheaper, you buy more salad AND more dressing. Set up: how do we measure that link?

Cross Elasticity of Demand — Complements

Complements (salad & dressing) have a negative cross elasticity: when one's price falls, the other's quantity rises.

Salad (good a)

Salad price falls $4.99 → $3.29, Q rises 60 → 100

Dressing (good b)

Dressing demand shifts right at $3.99

Cross Elasticity

\(E_{ab} = \dfrac{\%\Delta Q_b}{\%\Delta P_a}\)

\(= \dfrac{(120-80)/100}{(3.29-4.99)/4.14} = \dfrac{+40\%}{-41\%}\)

\(= \mathbf{-0.98}\)

Negative → complements.

Cross elasticity formula: change in B's quantity over change in A's price. Negative sign signals complement. When salad gets cheaper, people buy more salad — and more dressing. Brand managers use this to plan promotions: discount the “anchor” product and complementary sales rise. Examples: razors + blades, gaming consoles + games, printers + ink.

Popcorn vs. Popcorn — Which Bag?

Two popcorn brands sitting next to each other. The classic substitute pair: nearly identical product, slightly different packaging. If one goes on sale, the other loses customers in real time.

Cross Elasticity of Demand — Substitutes

Substitutes (Skinny Pop & Boom Chicka Pop) have a positive cross elasticity: when one's price falls, buyers switch away from the other.

Skinny Pop (good a)

Skinny Pop price falls $4.79 → $3.50

Boom Chicka Pop (good b)

Boom Chicka demand shifts left at $3.49

Cross Elasticity

\(E_{ab} = \dfrac{\%\Delta Q_b}{\%\Delta P_a}\)

\(= \dfrac{(30-50)/40}{(3.50-4.79)/4.14} = \dfrac{-50\%}{-31\%}\)

\(= \mathbf{+1.61}\)

Positive → substitutes.

When Skinny Pop's price drops, customers switch away from Boom Chicka Pop. Positive cross elasticity. Most product categories with multiple brands — cereal, toothpaste, paper towels — have lots of high-positive cross elasticities. Anti-trust regulators use this to decide if two products are in the same “market.”

Question

Why do stores advertise certain discounted items — and not others?

A 7-Eleven shelf with the kind of promo signs you've all seen. Pause here and let the room try to answer. We'll explain on the next slide — it's all cross elasticity at work.

Stores Advertise the Complement — Not the Buddy

Drop the price on the visible item (energy drink). Demand for its complement (snack) shifts right at full price.

Energy drink (promoted)

$3 →  2 for $5

Snack (silent winner)

Stays at $3.99 — quantity sold jumps.

The promoted item is a loss leader. The store's profit comes from the complement at full price.

The visible discount creates traffic. The hidden margin on the complement pays the bills. Convenience stores have refined this into an art — \$5 monster + \$2 chips = a \$7 ticket that started as a "deal" the customer feels great about.

Loss Leaders — The Back-to-School Playbook

Walk in for Loss Leaders:

  99¢ notebooks  ·    $2.99 colored pencils  ·    40¢ glue

End up buying other things:

  laptop  ·    headphones  ·    backpack  ·    clothes

Loss-leader strategy: intentionally price one product below cost to attract foot traffic — then boost total revenue with full-price complements.

Staples does this every August. The notebooks are not the revenue source; the laptop and the bluetooth speaker are. The cross elasticity between “cheap school supplies” and “parent's wallet” is what pays for the discount.

Loss Leaders — The Numbers

One product priced below cost creates the traffic; a complement at full price creates the profit.

Notebook (below cost)

$1 → $0.10

Sales double. Store loses money on every notebook.

Laptop (the real profit)

Stays around $699 – $799. Quantity jumps.

Demand shifts right; store can even nudge the price up.

Concrete numbers tell the story. A 90¢ loss per notebook times 500 extra notebooks is −\$450. A laptop sale at \$799 with \$200 margin times 50 extra sales is +\$10,000. The complement (laptop) pays for the loss leader (notebook) many times over. This is cross elasticity as a pricing weapon — not just an academic concept.

$$E_{cross} = \frac{\%\;\Delta\; Q_A}{\%\;\Delta\; P_B}$$

Section 5

Supply Elasticity & Tax Incidence

Who really pays when the government taxes a good?

Supply Elasticity: Time Is Everything

Market period: Supply fixed. Flower shop on Valentine’s Day.

Short run: Extra shifts, spare capacity. Egg farms raise replacement pullets.

Long run: New firms enter. Austin adds 120K housing units.

Austin (elastic supply): rents fell 16%. SF (inelastic): prices soared.

Austin vs San Francisco is the best supply elasticity comparison. Austin reformed zoning → elastic supply → more homes, lower rents. SF restricted building → inelastic supply → all demand pressure became price increases.

Tax Incidence: The Inelastic Side Pays

The side that can’t escape the tax bears the burden.

Regardless of who legally writes the check.

Cigarettes (PED 0.3–0.4)

Smokers bear most of the tax. Can’t easily quit. Avg state tax: $2.01/pack.

Youth cigarettes (PED 0.7)

More elastic! Taxes prevent youth smoking better than reduce adult smoking.

Payroll taxes (50/50 legal split)

Labor supply inelastic → employees bear most of burden through lower wages.

The statutory 50/50 split of payroll taxes is economically irrelevant — what matters is relative elasticities. This principle applies to every tax: cigarettes, carbon, soda, tariffs.

Discussion: The Policymaker’s Paradox

You’re advising the governor on funding universal pre-K. Two options:

Option A: Cigarette Tax

+$1/pack • PED = 0.3

Option B: Soda Tax

1.5¢/oz • PED = 0.8

 Which raises more stable revenue ?

 Which changes behavior more effectively?

 Which is fairer — and how do you define fair?

5 min
3 min debate, 2 min debrief. Key insight: cigarette tax is better for revenue (inelastic base doesn't shrink), soda tax is better for behavior change (elastic response). But cigarette tax is regressive — burden falls on low-income smokers. No tax maximizes BOTH revenue AND behavior change.

Key Takeaways

1. Elasticity measures responsiveness — ratio of percentage changes, not slope.

2. Midpoint method eliminates directional asymmetry.

3. Total revenue test — elastic → price hike hurts. Inelastic → price hike helps.

4. Four determinants — substitutes, necessity, market definition, time horizon.

5. The inelastic side bears the tax burden — governs every debate about sin taxes, carbon taxes, and payroll taxes.
