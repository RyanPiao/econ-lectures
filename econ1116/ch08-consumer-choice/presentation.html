Section 2  ·  LO 8.2

Utility

What you want

How do we turn satisfaction into a number?

The old hope (1890s)

Utility was thought to be a real, measurable, cardinal scale. A cardinal ranking would let you say how much better one bundle is than another — not just which one wins.

The modern attempt — neuroeconomics

Grounds economic decisions in first principles — calories, water, warmth — and uses fMRI and PET scans to watch the brain while subjects make economic choices.

Measuring “satisfaction” directly — a century-old dream.

Obvious problems with a cardinal scale.

• Comparing two different consumers' preferences might make no sense at all.
• There is no real-world measuring stick for how much more a consumer likes bundle \(A\) than bundle \(B\).

Ordinal = comparing rankings.   Cardinal = comparing differences.   Consumer theory needs only the first.

1890s marginalists (Jevons, Menger, Walras) hoped utility was literally measurable. Neuroeconomics is the modern echo — fMRI/PET to ground choices in biology. The two "obvious problems" are why economics retreated to ordinal utility: no interpersonal comparison, no cardinal yardstick.

Individual Objectives and Preferences

Economists assume consumers are rational — able to “optimize” their consumption decisions given scarce resources.

“De gustibus non est disputandum” — there is no disputing about taste. Economics doesn't judge what you want; it takes preferences as given.

To predict choices, we need a consumer's preferences: between any two products \(a\) and \(b\), which is preferred — and how does that ranking behave?

(1) Rationality = the consumer optimizes. (2) "De gustibus non est disputandum" — economics is positive, not normative, about taste. Open question for next slide: how do we OBSERVE preferences?

Preference — revealed by what people do

We can't read minds — but we can watch actions. Far more plausible to infer preferences from what people actually choose.

“Actions speak louder than words.”

Principle of Revealed Preference. If a person chooses \(a\) over \(b\) when both are affordable, then they must prefer \[a \succeq b.\]

Flawless? Of course not — but an extremely useful approximation. People tend not to leave money on the table.

Preferences — comparing consumption outcomes

An ordinal ranking lets us order bundles from best to worst. For any two bundles \(a\) and \(b\), we allow exactly three answers:

\(a \succ b\)  —  strictly prefer \(a\) over \(b\)

\(a \prec b\)  —  strictly prefer \(b\) over \(a\)

\(a \sim b\)  —  indifferent between \(a\) and \(b\)

A consumer's preferences are just the list of all such comparisons. The "rules" of utility allow only an ordinal ranking — never a cardinal "how much."

a
?
b

Which do you prefer — a, b, or neither?

Three relations: strict-better, strict-worse, indifferent. A full preference relation is the complete list of these pairwise verdicts. "?" (indifferent) is a legitimate third answer, not a failure to decide.

Do Capuchin Monkeys Have Utility Functions?

Chen, Lakshminarayanan & Santos (2006)

Taught capuchins to use tokens as currency. Then watched them “shop” for jello, apples, grapes, cucumber.

What they found

  • Consistent ranking: jello > apple > grape > cucumber
  • Transitivity: if A > B and B > C, then A > C
  • Diminishing MU: 11th jello served < 1st
  • Loss aversion too — preview of §5

Implication: If a monkey behaves consistently with utility theory, the theory is describing something more fundamental than human sophistication. Rational and irrational behavior may both be evolutionary bedrock.

Surprising and memorable. The capuchin study is a great anchor — "even monkeys obey ordinal utility theory." But the SAME study found loss aversion. That foreshadows §5: deviations are deep too.

The Concept of Utility

🥒 10

UTILITY FUNCTION

In economics, utility is a measure of a good or service's usefulness, worth, or satisfaction it provides to a consumer — often quantified in subjective units called “utils.”

Utility measures how “satisfied” consumers are.

  • A measure of happiness or well-being
  • It is not a measure of consumer income

A utility function maps what consumers consume to their level of well-being.

  • Represents the consumer's preferences
  • Can take a variety of mathematical forms
  • Must conform to the four assumptions about preferences
The gauge metaphor: a utility function takes a consumption bundle (here, a cucumber) and returns a number on a satisfaction scale — 10 out of 100. Stress: utility is well-being, NOT income; the function just has to (a) represent the preferences and (b) obey the four assumptions. "Utils" are subjective units — only the ranking they induce is meaningful (ordinal).

Cobb-Douglas U(x,y) = x^0.8 \u00d7 y^0.2 is the most common functional form in applied micro \u2014 the exponents (0.8, 0.2) are weights that sum to 1 and encode how much the consumer values each good at the margin. Compute: U_a = 1^0.8 \u00d7 2^0.2 \u2248 1.15; U_b = 2^0.8 \u00d7 2^0.2 = 2.00; U_c = 2^0.8 \u00d7 1^0.2 \u2248 1.74. Students DON\'T need to redo the math; the point is just \"this is what utility functions look like as math objects that researchers and firms actually fit to demand data.\"

Utility Functions in the Wild — 2. Perfect Complements

Some goods only work together. One bicycle needs one frame AND two wheels — extras of either alone add zero utility.

1 Set of Wheels (y)

Bundles

Perfect complements (Leontief)

Utils

Ua = 1 + leftover
Ub = 2 no leftover
Uc = 3 + leftover

Other examples: left + right shoes; peanut butter + bread; GPU + monitor. Adding more of just one side adds nothing.

Perfect complements (Leontief) U(F,W) = min(F, W) \u2014 two goods consumed in fixed proportions. Bicycle math: U_a = min(1, 2) = 1 (one bike built; 1 leftover wheel); U_b = min(2, 2) = 2 (two bikes, no leftover); U_c = min(4, 3) = 3 (three bikes; 1 leftover frame). Indifference curves are L-shaped at the kink F = W. The leftover units add zero utility \u2014 that\'s the perfect-complements signature.

Perfect substitutes (linear) U(x,y) = x + y \u2014 two goods that swap 1-for-1. A tangerine and an orange are pretty close substitutes; the consumer just cares about TOTAL citrus. U_a = 1+2 = 3, U_b = 2+2 = 4, U_c = 4+3 = 7. Indifference curves are straight lines with slope \u22121. Other examples: $1 bill + 4 quarters; identical brands of bottled water. The big closing point \u2014 these specific numerical values are arbitrary; only the RANK matters. If we multiplied every U by 10 or took log, the consumer\'s preferences wouldn\'t change. This is the formal content of *ordinal utility* (callback to slide 6 satisfaction-to-number).

Utility — A Ranking System

Utility: an abstract measure of the satisfaction a consumer gets from goods & services.

You don’t need to measure utils

You just need to rank options consistently. “I prefer pizza to salad” is enough.

Two flavors

  • Total utility: total satisfaction from a bundle
  • Marginal utility (MU): ΔTU from one more unit

Misconception: “Utility is measured in exact units.” Hicks (1934): we only need ordinal rankings. Total utils are an analytical fiction; relative rankings are real.

Make the cardinal-vs-ordinal point quickly — modern theory only needs that consumers can rank consistently. The math we do uses numbers for convenience, but nothing in the chapter requires precise cardinal measurement.

Diminishing Marginal Utility — The Late-Night Pizza Test

Law of diminishing MU: as you consume more, each next unit adds less — until it adds nothing, then turns negative.

Universal student example. First slice: starving, MU huge. By slice 7: miserable, MU negative. Notice TU peaks (and can fall) before MU turns negative. The exact slice doesn't matter — the pattern does.

The Marginal Revolution — Why Diamonds Cost More Than Water

The 1870s puzzle

Water is essential, almost free. Diamonds are decoration, hugely expensive. Why?

Jevons (UK), Menger (AT), Walras (CH) — independently, same answer.

Their answer

Price reflects marginal utility, not total utility. Water: huge total, tiny marginal (it’s abundant). Diamonds: modest total, huge marginal (they’re scarce).

Update, 2025: Water = $2 at 7-Eleven; $5 inside TD Garden. Nothing about the water changed — venue scarcity raised its marginal utility. Apple Vision Pro at $3,499? Sales fell 95% — revealed preference said marginal utility didn’t justify the price.

Centerpiece historical story — the Marginal Revolution is *the* moment economics found its modern math. Marginal-not-total is the conceptual hinge for the next section. The TD Garden water example always lands.

Subscription Fatigue — Diminishing MU in Action

Households are cutting

  • 2024: 4.1 services. 2025: 2.8. (−32%)
  • 41% report “subscription fatigue”
  • Half would cancel over a $5 hike

Why?

By service 5 or 6, libraries overlap — you can’t watch enough hours for the marginal subscription to add value. MU per dollar of the next service → 0.

Consumers are doing the equimarginal math, whether they call it that or not.

Netflix tiers tell the same story. Standard-with-Ads ($9) vs. Premium ($27). 40% of accounts now on ad-tier (up from 26%). Price-sensitive consumers reveal MU(ad-free) < $11/mo.

Connect the abstract concept to data they can verify in their own lives. The Netflix tier shift is direct evidence of marginal utility per dollar in action: people facing tighter budgets reveal that the disutility of ads is less than $11/mo.

Section 3  ·  LO 8.3

The Equimarginal Principle

Combine the budget with the utility

The Rational Consumer’s Rule

Equimarginal principle: at the optimum, the marginal utility per dollar is equal across all goods.

$$ \dfrac{MU_A}{P_A} \;=\; \dfrac{MU_B}{P_B} \;=\; \dfrac{MU_C}{P_C} \;=\;\ldots $$

The logic: if your last $ on A gives more satisfaction than your last $ on B → shift to A. Keep going until no further reallocation helps.

The most powerful single equation in the chapter. Re-state it three ways: (1) marginal utility per dollar should be equal; (2) if it's not, you can do better by reallocating; (3) at the optimum you've extracted all gains-from-reallocation.

The Costco Puzzle

The trick

$65 membership fee + ~3% markups (vs. 30% at typical retailers). The fee is a sunk cost; the merch is priced for high MU/P.

Members average $100 × 30 visits/yr. Renewal: 92.9%.

Why MU/P is high

Kirkland private label: 20–40% below national brands. ~4,000 SKUs (vs. 30K-50K at supermarkets) → faster, more confident decisions.

Membership fees: 70% of operating profit ($4.8–5.3B/yr). The whole model is engineered to make MU/P at Costco beat MU/P everywhere else for loyal members.

Costco is a beautiful applied example. Mention that limiting SKUs to 4,000 is also a behavioral move (anti-choice-overload — preview §5). Bulk sizing only "works" for shoppers whose MU stays high deep into a unit count.

Cheapest-Per-Unit ≠ Highest MU per Dollar

Plan Price/mo Storage Per GB
Apple iCloud+ $0.99 50 GB $0.020
Google One $9.99 2,000 GB $0.005

By per-GB: Google wins in a blowout. By MU/$ for a typical student (using ~30 GB): the extra 1,950 GB on Google have MU ≈ 0 — you’re paying for empty space.

Lesson: the rational rule is pick the plan where the units you’ll actually use justify the marginal dollar — same logic for gym memberships, family-size groceries, and bulk in-game coins.

This is the subtle but important application of MU/P. Cheapest-per-unit is irrelevant if you don't consume those units. Tie back to bulk pricing: it's engineered for consumers with flat MU curves (the "whales").

Answer: B. MU/P matters, not MU alone. Tacos: 12/3 = 4. Smoothies: 18/6 = 3. Shift spending from smoothies to tacos until ratios equalize.

Always normalize by price — that’s what the rule is for.

The classic exam-question wording. The "MU is higher" trap is the most common error — it ignores price. Hammer the divide-by-P step.

Section 4  ·  LO 8.4

Deriving the Demand Curve

The payoff: where the law of demand comes from

From Equimarginal to the Law of Demand

When the price of a good rises, its MU/P falls (denominator up, numerator unchanged). The rule says: buy less of it — until MU/P is equal across goods again.

Price ↑  →   MU/P ↓  →   Qd ↓

That’s the law of demand — not an empirical regularity, a theorem from consumer theory.

The conceptual punchline of the chapter. The downward slope isn't from data — it's a mathematical consequence of the rule we just derived. This closes the loop with Ch 4 (where we assumed it).

1st1005.02.5
2nd804.02.0

4th201.00.5

At $20: buys 4 services. At $40: units 3 & 4 now lose to nights out (MU/P 2.0) — she drops to 2 services. Two price-quantity pairs: (20, 4) and (40, 2).

This is the explicit derivation. The MU table doesn't change — only the price changes. When you divide a fixed MU by a doubled price, MU/P halves, and the right answer to "how many units?" drops. This produces the (P,Q) pairs that we then plot.

Connect the Dots: the Demand Curve

Two prices, two equimarginal solutions, two points. Connect them — you have a downward-sloping demand curve. This is where the curve from Ch 4 comes from.

Pay-off moment. The demand curve we assumed in Ch 4 isn't a pattern in data — it's a derivation. Repeat the same exercise for every consumer in the market, sum horizontally, and you have market demand.

Shrinkflation — When the Price Hike Is Hidden

2025 data

  • Downsizing 5× more common than upsizing
  • Average shrink: 14.8% across major brands
  • Some products: >30% smaller

Asymmetric attention

Consumers are 2× as sensitive to a visible price hike as to an equivalent size cut. Sticker dominates per-ounce.

Bag of chips: 10 oz → 9.5 oz at $4.99. Per-ounce up 6%. Most consumers don’t notice.

Demand response is muted when the price signal is muddled. Same equimarginal math — but the inputs are obscured.

Bridge to behavioral. The rational model says "raise per-ounce price → quantity falls." Shrinkflation works because consumers anchor on sticker price, not per-ounce. Preview: this is the first crack in the rational model.

Concerts — When MU Is So High Demand Is Inelastic

Prices rising…

  • Average ticket: $128 (+34% / 6 yrs)
  • Bruno Mars: $254
  • Kendrick Lamar tour: $206

…but quantity rises too

  • To fund it: cut dining (35%), savings (30%), extra work (24%)
  • Coachella 2025: 60%+ used BNPL
  • 86% of Gen Z exceed concert budget

Two forces at once: MU of unique experiences is so high (few substitutes → Ch 6) that consumers tolerate steep prices and borrow from future budgets to pay.

Connects Ch 6 (elasticity) + Ch 8 (consumer choice). Demand still slopes down — but it takes a *big* price move because substitutes are sparse and MU is huge. Note the BNPL link: students rationalize concert spending by borrowing across periods.

Section 5  ·  LO 8.5

When Consumers Don’t Seem Rational

Five predictable deviations (preview of Chapter 18)

1. Choice Overload

Iyengar & Lepper (2000)

“Jam experiment”: shoppers seeing 24 jams were less likely to buy than those seeing 6.

More options → decision paralysis.

Netflix today

~18,000 titles. Average user spends 15–20 min browsing — often defaults to re-watches. “Play Something”, top-10 lists, recommendations = choice architecture.

Violates a rational assumption: a larger choice set should never reduce utility. In real consumers, it can.

The cleanest counter-example to "more options always better." Note the meta-analysis context: effect is real but strongest when preferences are weak. Where preferences are strong, more options DO help.

2. Default Bias — The Power of Inertia

Organ donation

Opt-out countries: registration >90%. Opt-in: <15%. Same populations, same organs.

2024 update: opt-out raises registration but not actual donation rates — institutional capacity matters too.

SECURE 2.0 (Jan 2025)

New 401(k) plans must auto-enroll at 3–10%, escalating 1%/yr. Employees can opt out. Most won’t.

Built directly on default-bias research.

The default isn’t neutral. Whoever sets it makes the choice for most people.

Most important policy lever in behavioral econ. Note the nuance: opt-out changes registration but not the underlying medical/legal capacity. Defaults move first-stage behavior; downstream outcomes still need institutional design.

3. Framing Effects — Same Info, Different Decisions

90% lean vs 10% fat

Identical ground beef. The “90% lean” label outsells “10% fat” — persistently, across education levels.

2025: temporal framing

Negative + immediate (“heart disease starts in your 20s”) outperforms positive or distant framing.

Rational benchmark: presentation shouldn’t change preferences. Real consumers respond to it — nearby threats more than distant gains.

Quick example. The persistence-across-education-levels point is important: framing isn't fixed by knowing more econ. It's a feature of human cognition.

4. The Decoy Effect — The Option Nobody Wants

Ariely’s Economist experiment. Add a “print-only $125” decoy and the share choosing print+digital jumps 32% → 84%.

Adding an option that's strictly worse than another shouldn't change anyone's choice in the rational model. But it dramatically does in practice. Disney+ at $19 standalone vs. $20 bundle is the same trick — the bundle looks like an "extraordinary deal" once the decoy frames the comparison.

The trick

The $19 standalone is the decoy. It doesn’t need to sell — just to make the bundle look obviously better.

You’re not being tricked by a price — you’re being tricked by a comparison. Choice architecture matters as much as the menu.

Make this directly applicable: every time they see a "weird middle option" that nobody picks, the firm is using it as a decoy. Movie theater popcorn (small/medium/large priced to push the large) is the same trick.

5. Mental Accounting — Not All Dollars Are Equal

Thaler (1985)

Consumers sort money into mental buckets (rent, food, entertainment, savings) and resist transferring between them.

Two clean examples

  • Tax refund → vacation. Credit-card debt at 25% APR → stays.
  • Uber surge: OK in “emergency” bucket, outrage in “commute”.

Violates a rational assumption: a dollar is a dollar. Real consumers price the label on the dollar.

Mental accounting is everywhere once students learn the name. Uber surge pricing is a great example — identical ride, different mental bucket, different willingness to pay. Note: 2025 Oxford study of 1.5M Uber trips found surge pricing extracted commissions exceeding 50% on some trips because passengers categorized as "emergency."

Is the Rational Model Still Useful?

“As if” defense

Individuals make mistakes, but competitive markets discipline error. Aggregate outcomes look as if everyone were rational.

The counter

Some deviations are systematic, not random. Defaults push everyone the same way. Firms design environments to exploit biases.

The resolution: the rational model is the benchmark, not the description. Behavioral insights (Ch 18) build on it by identifying the predictable deviations.

Important not to oversell behavioral OR rational. The right framing: rational gives us the bench, behavioral gives us a catalog of when reality drifts. Both matter. Thaler's "Save More Tomorrow" is the best success story of using both: rationally-modeled people would save more, behavioral defaults make them.

Key Takeaways

1. Two ingredients of choice. Budget = what you can afford. Utility = what you want.

2. Price rotates, income shifts. The slope is opportunity cost; the intercepts are scale.

3. MU diminishes; MU/P equalizes. The equimarginal rule is the bridge.

4. Demand slopes down by theorem. Price ↑ → MU/P ↓ → Qd ↓.

5. Real consumers deviate — predictably. Overload, default, framing, decoy, mental accounting. The rational model is the benchmark; behavioral econ catalogs the gap.
