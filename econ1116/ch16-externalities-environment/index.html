Section 1 · LO 16.1

Externalities: When Markets Get the Quantity Wrong

Negative externalities → too much. Positive externalities → too little.

Two Definitions to Lock In

Market failure

When a free market, left on its own, fails to allocate resources efficiently — producing a suboptimal outcome for society.

Externality

A cost or benefit of production or consumption that falls on a third party who is not part of the transaction.

Pigou spotted it in 1920: factory smoke was an “uncompensated damage” that never showed up on any invoice. A century later: carbon, PFAS, congestion.

Externalities are the most common source of market failure. The key is "uncompensated" — the cost or benefit never passes through a price, so neither buyer nor seller has any reason to account for it. Everything in this chapter follows from that one wedge.

The Wedge: Private vs Social Cost

Marginal private cost (MPC)

The cost the producer pays for one more unit. This is the supply curve.

Marginal social cost (MSC)

Private cost plus the external cost to bystanders.
MSC = MPC + external cost

When there's a negative externality, MSC > MPC. Producers ignore the gap → the market overproduces. The excess output is a deadweight loss.

This is the whole mechanism. Producers face only MPC, so they set MPC = demand. But society's cost is MSC. Every unit between the social optimum and the market quantity costs society more than buyers value it — that's the DWL. Same logic mirrors for positive externalities on the benefit side.

Negative Externality: Overproduction

Market sets MPC = demand → Q = 35. Too much.

Society wants MSC = demand → Q* = 25.

The wedge is real damage to people who never bought a drop.

Walk the diagram: the vertical gap between MSC and MPC is the $40 external cost per unit. The market ignores the red line entirely. The shaded triangle from Q*=25 to Qmkt=35 is the deadweight loss — ½ × 10 × $40 = $200k/week. Set up the numbers next.

Worked Example: Clearwater Chemical

MPC = $60 + $2Q  ·  Demand: P = $200 − $2Q

Unregulated: 60 + 2Q = 200 − 2Q
→ Q = 35, P = $130

Each unit dumps $40 of contamination downstream.
MSC = $100 + $2Q

Efficient: 100 + 2Q = 200 − 2Q
→ Q* = 25, P = $150

Overproduction = 10 thousand gallons
DWL = ½ × 10 × $40 = $200k / week

Real damage, borne by people who never bought a drop of solvent.

Have them do the algebra with you. The trick students miss: the external cost shifts the relevant cost curve UP by $40, so the intercept goes 60→100. The DWL triangle base is the 10-unit overproduction, height is the $40 wedge. This same example returns when we add a Pigouvian tax in Section 3.

Negative Externalities Are Everywhere (2024–26)

PFAS “forever chemicals” — hidden in a $30 jacket; EPA's 2024 water rule costs ~$1.5B/yr to clean up.

Fast fashion (Shein) — 11.2M tons CO₂e in 2024; the $5 t-shirt's price ignores dye pollution & landfill.

Wildfire smoke — PM2.5 drifts thousands of miles; exposure falls hardest on Black & Hispanic residents.

Livestock methane — ~1.5B cows, ~14% of global GHGs. No single owner bears the climate cost.

The unifying thread: the external cost is hidden inside a cheap private price. PFAS in a jacket, emissions in a t-shirt, smoke from someone else's forest, methane from a burger. Diffuse, uncompensated, and usually regressive. The fast-fashion case is a good one to linger on — air-freighting individual packages drove a 13.7% surge in distribution emissions.

Positive Externalities: Too Little

Marginal private benefit (MPB)

What the consumer gets from one more unit — the demand curve.

Marginal social benefit (MSB)

Private benefit plus the external benefit to others.
MSB = MPB + external benefit

Consumers weigh only their own benefit → they buy where MPB = supply, not MSB = supply. The market underproduces.

Mirror image of the negative case. Now the gap is on the BENEFIT side: society values the good more than any individual buyer does, so we get too little of it. The fix will be a subsidy (Section 3) rather than a tax.

Positive Externality: The Flu Shot

Your private benefit from a flu shot: $50. Cost: $20. You get it.

But you also protect roommates & classmates — ~$30 external benefit. MSB = $80.

Healthy students with $15 private benefit skip it — even though society gains $45. Under-vaccination.

This is why insurers cover flu shots at $0 out of pocket — it's a Pigouvian subsidy pushing toward herd immunity. The forgone-welfare triangle is the mirror of the negative-externality DWL: units society would value but that never get produced.

Where Society Gains More Than the Buyer

Education — you earn more; society also gets more taxes, less crime, knowledge spillovers. → public schools, Pell Grants.

R&D & the CHIPS Act — knowledge leaks to rivals & society. NIH $48B/yr; $52B for chip fabs.

Open-source AI — Llama, Mistral, Gemma. Firms eat the training cost; thousands of startups reap the benefit.

EV charging — each port cuts “range anxiety” for every driver. 70,000+ fast ports in 2025, heavily subsidized.

When the social return exceeds the private return, the market under-invests — so governments subsidize.

Connect back: the EV credits in the IRA (Ch 7) and patent monopolies (Ch 11) are both answers to positive externalities — patents temporarily overprice a drug precisely to reward the R&D spillover. The open-source AI case is the cleanest modern example: ~90% of proprietary performance, given away free.

Your Turn — Spot the Externality

Your neighbor plants a flower garden in their front yard. Everyone on the block enjoys the view, but no one pays them. What does the free market do?

A. Overproduces gardens (negative externality)
B. Underproduces gardens (positive externality)
C. Gets it exactly right — no failure
D. It depends on the price of flowers
Answer: B. The gardener captures only the private benefit (their own enjoyment), not the block-wide benefit. MSB > MPB, so they plant fewer/smaller gardens than is socially optimal — classic positive externality. This is why cities offer beautification grants and why HOAs sometimes subsidize landscaping.

Think Like an Economist: Should Pollution Be Zero?

Eliminating the last 1% of pollution might mean grounding planes, closing hospitals, halting food production.

The optimum is where the marginal cost of abating = the marginal damage avoided — not zero.

This is the economist's answer to the environmentalist's instinct, and it surprises students. The question is never "pollution or no pollution?" It's "how much pollution is efficient?" As abatement approaches 100%, marginal cost rockets toward infinity while the marginal damage of the last bit of pollution is small. Optimal cleanup is interior.

Section 2 · LO 16.2

The Coase Theorem: Can Markets Fix Themselves?

Ronald Coase, 1960 — sometimes private bargaining beats the government.

The Coase Theorem

If property rights are clear and transaction costs are low, private parties bargain to the efficient outcome — no matter who holds the right.

Your two dogs bark all day. Your neighbor wants quiet. How many hours of barking is efficient?

MB falls as you bark more; the neighbor's MC rises. Efficient where they cross.

Coase's radical claim: the EFFICIENT quantity is the same whether you have the right to make noise or the neighbor has the right to silence. Only WHO PAYS WHOM changes. We'll prove it with the numbers next, then show why it usually breaks.

The Efficient Amount of Barking = 2 Hours

Hours 1–2: your benefit > neighbor's cost → net value created.

Hour 3+: cost ($400) > benefit ($200) → value destroyed.

Efficient = 2 hours, regardless of who holds the right.

Read the bars: hour 1 (MB 400 vs MC 100), hour 2 (300 vs 200), hour 3 (200 vs 400). The crossover is between 2 and 3. Any hour where MB > MC should happen; any where MC > MB should not. That defines 2 hours as efficient. Now the bargaining.

Either Way, You Land on 2 Hours

You hold the right to bark

Neighbor pays you to silence hour 3 (between $200 and $400). Both win. Hours 1–2 keep barking.

Neighbor holds the right to quiet

You pay for hours 1–2 (MB > MC, deal possible). Hour 3: your $200 < their $400 — no deal.

The efficient quantity (2 hrs) doesn't depend on the assignment of rights. Only the distribution — who pays whom — changes.

This is the surprising part. Rights determine fairness/who-pays, but not efficiency. The market for the externality clears at the same Q either way. Coase's deeper point: externalities are reciprocal — stopping the dog harms you just as the barking harms the neighbor.

Why Coase Usually Fails

High transaction costs — air pollution hits millions. You can't get them all in one room.

Unclear property rights — who “owns” clean air or the ocean? Bargaining can't even start.

Information asymmetry — the polluter may not know (or admit) the true damage.

Strategic behavior — holdouts & “environmental mugging” (threaten more pollution for leverage).

Common misconception: “Coase proves government is never needed.” Backwards — Coase shows why intervention is usually needed: transaction costs are rarely low enough.

The externalities that matter most — climate, water contamination, congestion — involve exactly the conditions that kill Coasian bargaining: too many parties, missing rights, hidden information, incentives to lie. So Coase is best read as a benchmark that tells us WHEN we need public solutions.

Coase in Practice: Voluntary Carbon Markets

Ronald Coase (1910–2013) — Chicago economist, Nobel 1991. “The Problem of Social Cost” (1960) challenged Pigou.

Carbon-offset markets are the biggest real-world Coasian experiment — over $1B/yr in trades by 2025.

But you can't verify a forest was truly saved → high-quality credits cost 381% more. Information friction in action.

The voluntary carbon market proves Coase's own limitation: even when parties WANT to bargain, verification and monitoring costs are enormous. The 381% premium for verified removal over cheap avoidance credits is the price of information asymmetry. This sets up the public solutions in Section 3.

Section 3 · LO 16.3 & 16.4

Public Solutions: Taxes vs Mandates

When bargaining fails, put a price on the externality.

The Pigouvian Tax

A tax on a negative externality set equal to the marginal external cost at the optimal quantity. It forces producers to internalize the spillover.

The tax turns the firm's private cost into the social cost: MPC + tax = MSC.

It doesn't ban pollution — it makes firms pay for it, and they cut to the efficient level.

The elegance: a tax achieves efficiency while letting each firm decide HOW to respond. Firms with cheap abatement cut; firms with expensive abatement pay. The NYC toll is exactly this — a price on the congestion externality, not a ban on driving.

The Tax Hits the Social Optimum

Clearwater's external cost = $40. Set the tax = $40.

New cost: 100 + 2Q = MSC → Q = 25. Exactly efficient.

Revenue = $40 × 25 = $1,000k/wk — fund cleanup or cut other taxes.

Same Clearwater numbers as Section 1. The tax shifts the dashed MPC up onto the MSC line, so the firm now voluntarily produces 25 — the social optimum — and the DWL vanishes. Bonus: the tax raises revenue, unlike a pure regulation. Note the misconception that carbon taxes cause runaway inflation: Moessner (2025) finds a $10/ton rise adds just 0.08pp to inflation.

The Mirror Image & a Real Tax

Pigouvian subsidy

Pay for activities with external benefits — tuition aid, R&D credits, free vaccines — to push quantity up to the optimum.

Soda taxes (negative)

Philadelphia's 1¢/oz tax cut sugary-drink purchases 27.7%. A 16-country meta-analysis: ~15% average drop.

Crucially, buyers didn't just switch to other sweets — UK kids cut sugar 5g/day, adults 11g/day.

Sugary drinks impose health externalities (diabetes, obesity) on public healthcare. Soda taxes are textbook Pigouvian corrections, and the elasticity is high enough that they genuinely change behavior — not just relabel it. Subsidies are the exact mirror for positive externalities.

Command-and-Control vs. Market-Based

FeatureCommand-and-controlPigouvian tax / market-based
MechanismSets quantity limits or technology standardsSets a price on the externality
FlexibilityLow — one rule for all firmsHigh — firms choose how to respond
Cost efficiencyHigher costLower cost (cheap abaters cut most)
CertaintyCertain quantityCertain price, uncertain quantity
Best whenAny exposure is dangerous (PFAS, toxins)Goal is lowest-cost reduction (GHGs)

Neither wins everywhere. The SO₂ trading program cut acid rain at ¼ the cost regulators predicted.

The reason market-based usually wins for GHGs is heterogeneity: firms face wildly different abatement costs. A blanket "everyone cut 20%" ignores that some can cut cheaply and others can't. But for substances dangerous at any level (PFAS in drinking water), a hard limit beats a price. Most countries mix both.

Economist at Work: The Social Cost of Carbon

Kevin Rennert & team (RFF, 2022) rebuilt the dollar value of one extra ton of CO₂ — health, crops, flooding, energy.

Old federal estimate

$51

EPA estimate (2023)

$190 / ton

This single number sets how big a carbon tax should be — and shapes billions in regulatory cost-benefit analysis.

Nearly a 4× jump from the old $51. Every environmental cost-benefit analysis uses the SCC, so this one research finding moves billions of dollars. In March 2025 the new administration announced plans to "overhaul" it — a vivid case of a research number becoming a political battleground. Keep the $190 in mind for the carbon-price gap later.

Your Turn — Pick the Tool

A chemical is a known carcinogen dangerous at any dose in drinking water. Which policy fits best?

A. A per-unit Pigouvian tax
B. Cap-and-trade with permits
C. Command-and-control: a hard limit
D. Do nothing — let Coase work
Answer: C. When any exposure is unacceptable, you want a hard quantity limit — not a price that leaves some contamination in place. This is exactly the EPA's PFAS drinking-water standard. A tax/permit system is great for GHGs (where the goal is cheapest total reduction) but wrong when the safe level is essentially zero.

Section 4 · LO 16.4 & 16.5

Cap-and-Trade & the Climate Problem

Quantity certainty of a mandate, cost efficiency of a market.

How Cap-and-Trade Works

1. Set a cap — a legal limit on total emissions for the period.

2. Issue permits — one permit = one unit of pollution. Permits total exactly the cap.

3. Allow trading — cheap abaters sell spare permits to firms that find cutting expensive.

Trading routes every reduction to where it's cheapest. The cap fixes the total; the market finds the low-cost path.

The genius is in step 3. The government doesn't need to know which firm can cut cheaply — the permit price reveals it. Firms that can abate below the permit price do so and sell; firms above it buy. The market sorts it out.

Three Power Plants, One 90-Ton Cap

PlantEmissions nowPermitsAbatement cost / ton
A (modern gas)5030$10
B (older coal)5030$30
C (heavy industry)5030$50

No trading: each cuts 20 tons. Cost = (20×10)+(20×30)+(20×50) = $1,800.

With trading: A cuts 50 (sells permits), B cuts 10, C cuts 0. Cost = (50×10)+(10×30) = $800.

Walk it: total cut needed is 60 tons (150→90). Cheapest first: Plant A cuts all 50 at $10. Remaining 10 from Plant B at $30. Plant C ($50) cuts nothing. Permits: A holds 30 but emits 0, sells all 30; B buys 10, C buys 20. Used = 0+40+50 = 90 = the cap. Same reduction, < half the cost.

Same Cut, Less Than Half the Cost

Trading equalizes marginal abatement cost across firms — the condition for minimum total cost.

The cap guarantees the environmental result; the market guarantees it's done cheaply.

$1,800 → $800 for the identical 60-ton reduction. This is the single most important result in environmental policy: let the market find where cutting is cheap. The permit price ($30, Plant B's marginal cost) is the market signal.

The EU ETS & the Border Tax (CBAM)

EU ETS — world's largest cap-and-trade, since 2005. Permits ~€75/ton; the cap tightens ~8%/yr.

Carbon leakage — strict rules push dirty production abroad. No net emissions cut.

CBAM (Jan 2026): importers pay the EU carbon price they dodged. Chinese steel faces $72–83/ton.

CBAM's strategic brilliance: to avoid paying Brussels, exporting nations are incentivized to adopt their OWN carbon price and keep the revenue. China expanded its national ETS to steel & aluminum in 2025 partly under CBAM pressure. It forces global convergence on carbon pricing.

Carbon Tax vs. Cap-and-Trade

Carbon taxCap-and-trade
Price certaintyYes — firms know the costNo — permit price swings
Quantity certaintyNo — emissions uncertainYes — cap fixes the total
Best whenFirms need to plan investmentThere's an ecological tipping point

Modern systems go hybrid — cap-and-trade with price floors/ceilings (California's $27.94 floor; the EU's Market Stability Reserve).

Both can reach efficiency; they differ in what they make CERTAIN. Canada's tax (C$80→C$170 by 2030) gives firms a known price to plan around. A hard cap is more reliable if scientists identify a temperature threshold that must not be crossed. Hybrids capture both.

Climate: The Ultimate Externality

Coal burned in Ohio warms the whole planet — global, long-term, deeply uncertain.

Free-rider problem — every country wants others to cut. It's the prisoner's dilemma from Ch 13, played by nations.

Kyoto → Paris → COP28: every climate treaty struggles with enforcement because no global authority can punish defectors.

Recall the PD from Ch 13: "defect" (pollute) dominates for each player, so the Nash equilibrium is too little cooperation — even though all are better off if all cut. That's why treaties need enforceable side-payments or border adjustments (CBAM) to change the payoffs. Also depends on the discount rate: how much we value future welfare.

The World Still Under-Prices Carbon

73 carbon-pricing systems cover ~23% of global emissions in 2026 — but prices vary wildly.

Nearly all sit far below the $190 social cost of carbon. The US has no national price.

The gap between actual prices and the $190 SCC is the headline: even the EU's ~$82 is less than half. This is the quantitative case that the world is dramatically under-pricing carbon. Connect to the SCC slide — the "right" Pigouvian tax is far higher than almost any existing carbon price.

Information as a Third Instrument

MethaneSAT (EDF, 2024) maps oil & gas methane from space — revealing US emissions 4× higher than EPA estimates.

In the Permian Basin, the Texas side leaked 2× the New Mexico side — same geology, different rules.

Making pollution visible lowers the information asymmetry Coase identified — transparency moves us toward efficiency.

This is the chapter's most modern idea: alongside taxes and permits, INFORMATION is a third environmental instrument. When everyone can see who's polluting, accountability follows — and the wasted gas (worth millions/day) is itself an incentive to stop leaking. Directly lowers the transaction costs that block Coasian bargaining.

Environmental Justice: Who Bears the Cost?

PFAS & wildfire smoke fall hardest on low-income communities and communities of color.

Carbon taxes can be regressive — the poor spend more of their income on energy.

Revenue recycling fixes it: Canada's rebate checks give low-income households back more than they pay.

The tension from Ch 1: efficiency vs equity. A Pigouvian tax can be efficient AND unfair. Revenue recycling (Canada's Climate Action Incentive) is the standard fix — the carbon price still steers behavior, but the revenue is returned progressively. Efficiency is necessary but not sufficient.

Behavioral Lens: Why We Underinvest

Present bias — we overweight costs now and underweight benefits decades away.

Cutting emissions costs money today; the avoided floods and heatwaves arrive in 30–50 years. Hyperbolic discounting makes us shrug at distant—even catastrophic—damage.

Climate policy is a fight between what our models recommend and what our psychology will accept.

This explains the puzzle: the economics of carbon pricing is overwhelming, yet political support stays weak. Present bias and steep discounting of far-future damages are a big part of why. Bridges to Ch 18 (behavioral economics).

Key Takeaways

1. Externalities break efficiency. Negative → MSC > MPC → overproduce. Positive → MSB > MPB → underproduce.

2. Coase: clear rights + low transaction costs → private bargaining is efficient. But costs are rarely low enough.

3. Pigouvian tax = marginal external cost. It internalizes the spillover and raises revenue.

4. Cap-and-trade fixes the quantity; trading delivers it at the lowest cost by exploiting heterogeneity.

5. Information (MethaneSAT) is a third instrument; equity (env. justice) is a separate question from efficiency.

6. Climate is the ultimate externality — global, long-term, and a free-rider problem.

Next time: Chapter 17 — Public Goods & Common Resources. Goods the market can't price at all.

The unifying idea: an externality is a missing price. Every tool — bargaining, taxes, permits, even satellites — is a way to supply that missing price so private incentives line up with social value. The optimal level of pollution is never zero.
