Recall Debrief — and the Question for Today

Q1 — At equilibrium, Qs = Qd. The market clears: no shortage, no surplus.

Q2 — A shortage pushes price up — buyers bid against each other until Qd falls back to Qs.

Q3 — Chapter 4 told us markets clear. It never told us that clearing is good. "The price settled" is a positive statement. "The outcome is good" is a normative one — and it needs a yardstick.

Today's job

Build the yardstick — and then ask whether it measures the right thing.

Drive the positive/normative distinction home — it's a Chapter 2 callback. The market "working" (clearing) is not the same as the market being "good." Welfare economics is the attempt to make "good" measurable. The honest caveat (5.4) is that the yardstick measures the size of the pie, not the fairness of the slices.

The Eras Tour Ticket Puzzle

In 2023, Taylor Swift announced the biggest concert tour in history. Face value: $49–$499.

Demand was so overwhelming that Ticketmaster crashed. Resale tickets averaged $1,088 — premium seats topped $8,000.

A fan who paid $200 face value would gladly have paid $1,500. Another fan, priced out entirely, got nothing.

Who won? Who lost? And how would you even measure it?

$1,300

Value the $200 fan got beyond what they paid

$0

Value the priced-out fan got

The hook is a puzzle, not a story. The same concert produced wildly different outcomes for different people, and the artist earned far less per seat than fans were willing to pay. Students intuitively feel "something is being gained and lost here" — but they don't have words for it yet. By the end of today they will: that $1,300 is consumer surplus, and the priced-out fan is the start of the deadweight-loss story.

The Toolkit: Welfare Economics

Welfare economics — the study of how the allocation of resources affects economic well-being. It gives us a way to measure the gains a market creates and to see who captures them.

Four tools we build today

Consumer surplus

The buyer's "good deal" — value above price.

Producer surplus

The seller's reward — price above cost.

Total surplus

The whole pie — and why markets maximize it.

Deadweight loss

Pie that disappears when markets are distorted.

And one honest limit (5.4): every tool here measures the size of the pie. None of them measures whether the slices are fair.

This slide is the roadmap. Put the four tools on the board as a visual the whole lecture hangs on. The misconception box at the bottom is planted early on purpose — it pays off in Section 5. Students who only learn 5.1–5.3 will walk away thinking "efficient = good." The whole point of ending with 5.4 is to break that.

Section 1

Consumer Surplus

LO 5.1 — The value of a good deal

Every Purchase Hides a Calculation

Every time you buy something, you ask: "Is this worth it to me?" If the price is below what you'd have paid, you walk away with a bargain. Economists name both halves of that thought.

Willingness to pay (WTP) — the maximum amount a buyer would pay for a good. It measures the value the buyer places on it.

Consumer surplus (CS) — the benefit a buyer gets from a transaction:
CS = WTP − Price.

The Eras Tour fan from the hook: WTP = $1,500, Price = $200, so CS = $1,300. That's the number we couldn't name five minutes ago.

WTP is subjective — it's not the "real" value of the good, it's the value to that buyer. Consumer surplus is just the gap. Anchor it immediately back to the hook so the abstraction lands. The key move all of 5.1 makes: WTP varies across buyers, and the demand curve is the map of that variation.

The Strawberry Market — Four Buyers

A running example we'll carry through the whole chapter. Market price: $4 per pint.

BuyerWTPBuys at $4?
Amara$8Yes
Ben$6Yes
Carlos$4Indifferent
Diana$2No

The rule: a buyer purchases only when WTP ≥ Price. Amara and Ben gain; Carlos breaks even; Diana stays home.

Notice: the buyers are ranked by WTP, highest to lowest. Hold that thought — it's the demand curve in disguise.

Walk the table row by row. Emphasize Carlos: he buys but gains zero surplus — the marginal buyer always does. Diana doesn't buy, not because she's irrational, but because $4 exceeds what a pint is worth to her. The ranking observation is the bridge to the demand curve on the next slides.

Individual Consumer Surplus — Adding It Up

Each buyer's surplus is the gap between their bar and the price line:

  • Amara: $8 − $4 = $4
  • Ben: $6 − $4 = $2
  • Carlos: $4 − $4 = $0

Total consumer surplus = $4 + $2 + $0 = $6. Amara gains most — her valuation sits farthest above the price.

The staircase is the discrete version of the CS triangle. Each bar is a buyer; the shaded top of each bar is their surplus. Diana's bar is dashed and faded — she's not in the market. The visual point: CS is the area between the "staircase of WTP" and the price line. Next slide smooths the staircase into a curve.

Figure 5.1 — Consumer Surplus Is a Triangle

Scale up from 4 buyers to thousands and the staircase smooths into the demand curve.

Consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price. Every buyer to the left of Q was willing to pay more than P — that gap, summed up, is the triangle.

For a straight-line demand curve:

CS = ½ × Q × (WTPmax − P)

The demand curve is a ranking of WTP from highest to lowest — this is the single most important sentence in Section 1. The triangle is just the staircase from the last slide with infinitely many tiny steps. Half-base-times-height: base is Q, height is the vertical distance from the price up to the demand intercept. Drill the formula; they'll use it in the next slide and on the exam.

Working the Numbers — The Strawberry Market

Suppose the strawberry market has a linear demand curve with a maximum WTP of $10, and at the equilibrium price of $4 the quantity is 600 pints.

CS = ½ × 600 × ($10 − $4)
CS = ½ × 600 × $6 = $1,800

That $1,800 is the total value buyers receive beyond what they pay. It's a dollar measure of how much the market improves buyers' well-being — the same idea as the $1,300 in the Eras Tour hook, scaled to a whole market.

Keep the strawberry numbers visible — CS = $1,800 reappears in Section 2 (to add PS) and Section 3 (to show equilibrium maximizes total surplus). This is the chapter's spine. Make sure every student can reproduce the calculation cold; it's the most testable skill in 5.1.

What Happens When the Price Changes?

Price falls → CS rises

Two gains: existing buyers get a wider margin, and new buyers enter who were priced out.

Strawberries, $4 → $3: Q rises 600 → 700. CS = ½ × 700 × $7 = $2,450 — up $650.

Price rises → CS shrinks

The 2024–25 egg crisis: avian flu drove a dozen from $2.50 to $6.23.

Consumers lost an estimated $1B+ in surplus. The price line slid up, slicing into the triangle.

Who gets hurt most by a price increase? The low-WTP buyers — who tend to be lower-income. When a necessity like eggs or insulin spikes, lost consumer surplus falls hardest on those least able to absorb it. (We return to this in Section 5.)

Two-sided slide: symmetric mechanics, very different human stakes. The price-fall case is a clean two-source decomposition (wider margins + new entrants). The price-rise case — eggs — is a Chapter 4 callback and also seeds the equity discussion in Section 5. The distributional point at the bottom is deliberate foreshadowing.

Discussion: Two Fans, One Concert

5 min — Think-Pair-Share

Bloom's L4: Analyze

You scored a face-value ticket for $150. Your friend paid $900 on a resale site for the seat right next to yours. You both enjoy the exact same concert.

Who received more consumer surplus — and what does that tell you about how markets distribute value?

Hint: CS = WTP − Price. You can't answer it from the prices alone — what else do you need to know?

The trap: students will say "you got more — you paid less." But CS depends on WTP, which we don't know. If your WTP was exactly $150 and your friend's was $2,000, your friend got far more surplus despite paying more. The lesson: price paid is observable; surplus is not. Markets allocate goods by WTP, and WTP is shaped by income — a thread we pick up in Section 5.

Debrief: Surplus Is Hidden

The tempting answer

"I got more — I paid $750 less." Intuitive, but it confuses price paid with surplus.

The economist's answer

It depends on WTP. If your friend's WTP was $2,000, their CS ($1,100) beats yours — even though they paid far more.

Takeaway: price paid is visible; surplus is not. Markets route goods to whoever has the highest WTP — and WTP is shaped by how much money you have. Efficient allocation and fair allocation are not the same thing.

This debrief plants the Section 5 flag firmly. The resale market is "efficient" in the narrow sense — the ticket flowed to the higher-WTP buyer. But whether that's fair depends on why WTP differs, and income is a big part of the answer. Don't resolve the tension — name it and move on.

Section 2

Producer Surplus

LO 5.1 — The reward for low costs

The Seller's Side of a Good Deal

Buyers benefit when the price is below their WTP. Sellers benefit when the price is above their cost. Same idea, mirror image.

Producer surplus (PS) — the benefit a seller gets from a transaction:
PS = Price − Cost.

"Cost" here means the minimum a seller would accept — their opportunity cost of production, including everything else they could do with their time and resources.

Consumer surplus sits below demand, above price. Producer surplus sits above supply, below price. The two triangles meet at the equilibrium.

Stress that "cost" is opportunity cost, not just accounting cost — a Chapter 1 callback. The mirror-image framing is the whole slide: anything they learned about CS flips cleanly onto PS. The last callout previews Figure 5.3, where both triangles appear together.

Mirror the buyers slide exactly. Elena is the low-cost star; Hassan is priced out of producing. The ranking point: just as demand is a WTP ranking, supply is a cost ranking. That symmetry is what makes the next figure click.

Figure 5.2 — Producer Surplus Is a Triangle Too

Above the supply curve, below the price — the mirror of consumer surplus.

Producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the price. Every seller to the left of Q had a cost below P — that gap, summed, is the triangle.

For a straight-line supply curve:

PS = ½ × Q × (P − Costmin)

Strawberries: supply starts at $1, Q = 600, P = $4 → PS = ½ × 600 × $3 = $900.

Same half-base-times-height logic as CS, flipped vertically. Height is the distance from the supply intercept up to the price. The strawberry PS = $900 joins CS = $1,800 from Section 1 — both numbers go on the board and stay there. Source these as the two halves of total surplus, computed next.

When the Price Rises — Windfall, or Not

The egg windfall

Avian flu shifted supply left; prices tripled. Surviving producers' costs barely moved — so the price–cost gap exploded.

Cal-Maine Foods, the largest US producer, saw gross profit more than triple. That's pure producer surplus.

The cocoa paradox

Cocoa prices went $3,000 → $12,900/ton in 2024. You'd expect farmers to capture the surplus.

They didn't — Ivory Coast smallholders earned ~$1,050/year. Marketing boards fixed farm-gate prices; traders captured the gain.

The geometry tells us how much surplus a market creates. It does not tell us who captures it — that depends on market structure, institutions, and power. (Chapters 7, 11, 14.)

Two cases, same supply shock, opposite outcomes for producers. Eggs: surplus flows to producers because they sell at the market price. Cocoa: institutional frictions — national marketing boards, fixed farm-gate prices, traders in between — intercept the surplus before it reaches the grower. This is the single most important caveat in Section 2, and it's a direct setup for the equity discussion.

Economist at Work: Producer Surplus as a Signal

73%

Nvidia GAAP gross margin, Q4 FY2025 — ~73¢ of every revenue dollar is surplus above marginal cost

Textbooks call producer surplus "just firm profit." That framing misses its function.

A 73¢ margin is a giant flashing signal: resources should move here. And capital did — TSMC expanded fabs, rivals retooled for AI chips, venture money poured into data centers.

PS is not just a number. It's the message that tells an economy where to build next. When institutions block that signal — as with cocoa — the capital response stalls.

The pairing with the previous slide is intentional: Nvidia is the signal working, cocoa is the signal blocked. Adam Smith's 1776 insight, restated — producer surplus directs scarce inputs toward their highest-valued uses. Whether you think Nvidia's margin is "fair" is a separate question (Section 5); here the point is purely about coordination.

Section 3

Total Surplus & Market Efficiency

LO 5.2 — The invisible hand, formalized

Figure 5.3 — Total Surplus at the Competitive Equilibrium

Both triangles, meeting at E. Total surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus.

Total surplus = CS + PS = (value to buyers) − (cost to sellers). It's the net benefit everyone in the market receives — the total gains from trade.

Strawberry market: $1,800 + $900 = $2,700. The whole pie, in one number.

Next: why no other quantity can make this pie bigger.

The two triangles share an edge at the price line and a vertex at E. Total surplus is the whole quadrilateral — the area between demand and supply, from 0 to Q*. The setup question for the rest of the section: is this the biggest that area can be? (Yes — that's the First Welfare Theorem.)

Comparative Statics — When Demand Grows, So Does the Pie

Total surplus isn't fixed — it responds to every shift. A Chapter 4 callback with a welfare lens.

When demand shifts out (D → D′), equilibrium moves from E to E′. More buyers, more trades — and the whole surplus region between the curves expands.

This is the welfare lens on Chapter 4's shifts. Every comparative-statics question now has a second answer: not just "what happens to P and Q?" but "what happens to total surplus?"

The Invisible Hand at Work

A competitive market, left to reach its own equilibrium, maximizes total surplus. No planner, no algorithm, no committee could do better. Why? Three things happen at once:

1. The right buyers

Only buyers with WTP ≥ P purchase — the good goes to those who value it most.

2. The right sellers

Only sellers with cost ≤ P produce — the good is made by the lowest-cost producers.

3. The right quantity

At Q*, the marginal buyer's WTP exactly equals the marginal seller's cost. One more unit would cost more than it's worth.

A central planner would need every buyer's WTP and every seller's cost to match this. The market does it for free — just by letting prices clear.

Three conditions, revealed one at a time. This is the mechanism behind the First Welfare Theorem, stated in plain language before the formal name. The "asymmetry of effort" line is the punch: the market reproduces, with zero information, what a planner could only do with perfect information.

Three Names for What Just Happened

Allocative efficiency — goods go to the buyers who value them most, supplied by the lowest-cost producers. No mutually beneficial trade is left unmade. Every unit has WTP ≥ MC, with the marginal unit at WTP = MC.

Pareto efficient — no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. At equilibrium, every surplus-creating trade has already happened.

First Welfare Theorem — under perfect competition, no externalities, and complete information, a competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient. Proven by Arrow & Debreu in the 1950s.

What the theorem does NOT say: it does not say the outcome is fair, or that everyone ends up equally well off. It says only that the pie is as large as possible. Who gets which slice is Section 5.

Three vocabulary items that all describe the same equilibrium from different angles. Allocative efficiency is the §3.1.3 callback — producing the right mix — now made precise. Distinguish it from production efficiency (lowest-cost per unit, which returns in Chapter 10). The misconception box is non-negotiable: say it out loud, twice.

Numerical Proof — Why Q* = 600 Is Special

Too little (Q = 400): the 400th buyer values a pint at $6; the 400th seller's cost is only $3. That's $3 of surplus going uncaptured — on 200 missing units. Lost surplus = ½ × 200 × $3 = $300.

Too much (Q = 800): the 800th buyer values it at $2; the 800th seller's cost is $5. Each of those units destroys $3 of surplus.

Only at Q* = 600 — where the marginal WTP equals the marginal cost — is no surplus left uncaptured and none destroyed. Total surplus = the full $2,700.

The proof by contradiction: move off equilibrium in either direction and total surplus falls. At Q = 400 the orange triangle is surplus the market could have captured but didn't — $300 worth. At Q = 800 you'd be producing units that cost more than they're worth. Q* is the unique quantity where neither mistake happens. This is the payoff to "hold onto $2,700."

The Market's Kill Switch — Efficiency in Reverse

Allocative efficiency also pulls resources away from things the market doesn't value. Two 2024 AI gadgets show it vividly:

Humane AI Pin

$699 wearable, ~$240M in venture funding. By summer 2024, returns outpaced sales. HP bought the assets for $116M; the Pin was switched off — under a year after launch.

Rabbit R1

$199 pocket AI companion, ~100,000 sold. Five months in, only ~5,000 buyers — a 5% retention rate — still used it daily.

These aren't failures of the market — they're allocative efficiency doing its job. WTP fell below price and cost, so capital and engineering talent were freed to flow elsewhere. The missing producer surplus is itself the signal: it says stop.

Students often think "the market" means "things succeed." Flip it: the market's most valuable move is sometimes shutting something down. The Pin and the R1 tied up capital and talent producing things consumers didn't want at the price; killing them releases those resources. This reframes "failure" as the price mechanism working — and connects back to the Nvidia signal slide.

When the First Welfare Theorem Breaks Down

The theorem's conditions are demanding. Violate any one of them and the market stops maximizing total surplus — a situation we call market failure.

Market power

A firm that isn't a price taker restricts output and raises price. → Ch 11

Externalities

When a trade affects third parties, the price misses the true social cost. → Ch 16

Incomplete information

When buyers or sellers lack key facts, markets can unravel. → Ch 18

Public goods

Non-excludable, non-rival goods get underprovided — no one wants to pay. → Ch 17

The welfare theorems don't say markets are broken — they tell us exactly where to look. The rest of this course is a guided tour of these four failures.

This is the course roadmap slide. Each failure gets its own chapter later; here students just need the pattern: whenever a First Welfare Theorem condition is violated, total surplus falls short, and well-designed policy might help. Emphasize "might" — Chapter 7 will show policy can also create deadweight loss of its own.

Discussion: Could a Planner Beat the Market?

5 min — Think-Pair-Share

Bloom's L5: Evaluate

A benevolent social planner has perfect information — every buyer's WTP, every seller's cost in the strawberry market.

Could this planner achieve a higher total surplus than the competitive equilibrium? Why or why not?

Hint: what would the planner actually do differently? And how realistic is "perfect information" in the first place?

The intended answer: no, the planner can't beat $2,700 — the market already captures every surplus-creating trade. The best the planner can do is tie. The deeper point students should reach: the market achieves the efficient outcome with none of the information the planner would need. Push the realism question — "perfect information" is the assumption doing all the work, and it's exactly what real planners lack.

Debrief: The Planner Can Only Tie

The planner can't win

The competitive equilibrium already completes every trade with WTP ≥ cost. There's no surplus left to capture — the best the planner can do is reproduce $2,700.

The real lesson is the information

The market hits the efficient outcome knowing none of what the planner would need. Prices do the work that perfect information would otherwise require.

But notice the smuggled assumptions. "Could the planner beat it on efficiency?" — no. "Could the planner produce a fairer distribution?" — that's a different question entirely, and it's where Section 5 begins.

Close the loop: efficiency is settled — the market wins, or at best ties. But the discussion quietly assumed "better" means "more total surplus." The last callout reopens the question: a planner could redistribute the surplus differently, even if they can't enlarge it. That pivot is the bridge into deadweight loss and then equity.

Section 4

Deadweight Loss

LO 5.3 — When surplus is destroyed

When a Market Is Distorted, the Pie Shrinks

A tax, a price control, monopoly power — any distortion pushes a market away from the competitive equilibrium. When it does, total surplus shrinks.

Deadweight loss (DWL) — the reduction in total surplus caused by a market distortion. It is the value of mutually beneficial trades that no longer occur.

DWL is not a transfer from one group to another. It is value that disappears entirely. The buyers had WTP above cost; both sides would have gained — but the trade never happens, and that surplus is lost to everyone.

The single hardest idea in the chapter for students. Hammer the distinction: a transfer moves surplus (consumers → monopolist, or consumers → government). Deadweight loss destroys it — it's the trades that simply don't happen. Nobody's pocket gets that money. The next slide gives it a shape; the slide after that nails the transfer-vs-loss distinction.

Figure 5.4 — Deadweight Loss Is a Triangle

When output is held below Q*, the gap between demand and supply over the missing units is lost surplus.

Over the missing units (Qr to Q*), buyers' WTP sits above sellers' cost. Every one of those trades would have created surplus. The gray triangle is the surplus that simply vanishes.

Sometimes called a "Harberger triangle" — Arnold Harberger first estimated monopoly DWL for the US economy in 1954. Its size depends on the distortion and on elasticity.

Geometry: the DWL triangle has its base on the Qr line (the vertical gap between the demand point and the supply point) and its tip at E. Every unit between Qr and Q* had WTP > MC — a trade both sides wanted — and the distortion blocked it. Harberger's 1954 estimate was famously small (~0.1% of GDP), which kicked off decades of debate about the true cost of market power.

Common Misconception: "Someone Stole the Surplus"

The mistake: "Deadweight loss means the monopolist (or the government) took the surplus." That confuses a transfer with a loss.

Transfer = a rectangle

When a monopolist raises price, some surplus shifts from consumers to the monopolist. It changed hands — total surplus is unchanged.

Loss = a triangle

The trades that don't happen at all — priced-out buyers who valued the good above cost. Nobody pockets this. It's gone.

When you analyze any distortion, always split it: the rectangle of transferred surplus (who gained, who lost) and the triangle of destroyed surplus (gone for good).

The rectangle-vs-triangle picture is the most exam-relevant takeaway in Section 4. Draw it on the board: the rectangle is "redistribution" — you can argue about whether it's fair. The triangle is "waste" — nobody can argue it's good. Policies that only transfer are cheaper to society than policies that transfer and destroy.

DWL in Action: The Ticketmaster Monopoly

Live Nation/Ticketmaster controlled 80%+ of primary ticketing — enough to impose opaque, supra-competitive service fees on every ticket.

$16.4B

US ticketing fees, 2019–2024

15%

Fee cap in the 2026 DOJ settlement

Inflated prices priced out fans whose WTP exceeded the true cost of a seat. Those foregone trades — willing fans, empty seats — are the deadweight loss. The settlement aims to shrink the triangle by moving prices toward competitive levels.

Bookend with the opening hook — same Eras Tour world, now diagnosed. Internal company documents showed executives knew buyers balked at the true fee-loaded price, so they dripped fees into checkout. The fees themselves are largely a transfer (fans → Ticketmaster); the DWL is the concerts that didn't sell to fans who would have happily gone at a competitive price.

How Elasticity Sets the Size of the Triangle

Same tax wedge, two markets. The flatter the curves, the bigger the loss.

Figure 5.4 — identical tax wedge; the elastic market loses far more surplus.

Elastic curves → a small price distortion causes a large quantity change → a wide, tall DWL triangle.

Inelastic curves → the same distortion barely moves quantity → a narrow DWL triangle.

A tax on yachts (elastic) destroys lots of surplus; a tax on gasoline (inelastic) destroys less — but lands hard on consumers. Full story in Chapters 6–7.

Elasticity is Chapter 6, but the connection to DWL is worth previewing here. Two panels, same tax wedge: the elastic market (flat curves, left) loses a big triangle of surplus because buyers and sellers walk away; the inelastic market (steep curves, right) barely changes quantity, so the triangle is tiny. The teaching point: flatter curves → bigger DWL. The textbook's draggable elasticity-preset version is in the Chapter 5 ebook page.

DWL From Well-Intentioned Policy

Deadweight loss isn't only a monopoly story. Even a policy designed to help can create it.

The equity goal

After the January 2025 Palisades wildfires, LA County capped rent increases at 10% above pre-fire levels — to protect displaced families from price gouging.

The efficiency cost

By holding rents below market-clearing levels, the cap discouraged owners from converting units to rentals and slowed supplies rushing to the disaster zone — an artificial shortage. That's DWL.

A real policy trade-off: the cap pursued equity and paid for it in efficiency. Chapter 7 analyzes price ceilings, floors, taxes, and subsidies in full.

The point isn't "price controls are bad." It's that they have a measurable efficiency cost, and a policymaker should weigh it against the equity benefit explicitly — not pretend the trade-off doesn't exist. This is the cleanest possible setup for the insulin slide next, which argues the trade-off can be worth it.

The Insulin Paradox: When DWL Is Worth It

Insulin demand is nearly perfectly inelastic — diabetic patients will pay almost anything to survive. Manufacturers exploited that; 1 in 4 patients rationed doses.

The IRA's $35 monthly cap transferred huge surplus from manufacturers to patients — copays fell 55.8%; Medicare beneficiaries saved ~$734M/year.

Yes, the cap may create some DWL — CBO projected the IRA's drug-pricing rules could mean ~13 fewer new drugs over 30 years. But: is that future DWL worth the lives saved today?

Efficiency analysis alone cannot answer this. It can measure the triangle. It cannot tell you whether shrinking it is worth more or less than a rationed patient getting their insulin. That requires weighing efficiency against equity — our final section.

The honest slide. Because insulin demand is so inelastic, the DWL triangle from the cap is narrow — the static efficiency cost is small. The real efficiency worry is dynamic: weaker innovation incentives. But the chapter's framework deliberately stops short of answering "is it worth it" — that's a values question, and pretending economics settles it is exactly the overreach Section 5 warns against.

Answer: B — The Yacht Tax (Elastic Demand)

Elastic (yachts): buyers respond strongly — many stop buying — so lots of mutually beneficial trades vanish. Big DWL triangle.

Inelastic (insulin): buyers keep buying, so few trades are lost — small DWL — but they shoulder a heavy share of the tax.

The policy punchline: taxing inelastic goods minimizes deadweight loss but is often regressive; taxing elastic goods spreads the burden but destroys more surplus. There's no free lunch — just a choice about which cost to bear. This is the bridge into Chapters 6 and 7.

Deadweight Loss on a Global Scale: Tariffs

A tariff drives a wedge between the world price and the domestic price — exactly like a tax.

  • Domestic consumers pay more, buy less
  • Some domestic producers expand despite higher costs than foreign rivals
  • Both effects destroy surplus — a net DWL triangle remains after tariff revenue

$600–$1,300

Estimated annual purchasing-power loss per US household from 2024–26 tariffs (Yale Budget Lab; average effective rate rose to 13.7%)

Tariff revenue partially offsets the loss — but only partially. The triangle of foregone trades is real, and it scales to the whole economy. Full treatment in Chapter 7.

Tariffs make the DWL idea concrete at national scale. Same mechanism as a tax: a wedge between two prices, reduced consumption, inefficient domestic production. The government collects revenue (a transfer), but a net triangle of destroyed surplus remains. Keep it brief — Chapter 7 does the full diagram.

Section 5

Equity vs. Efficiency

LO 5.4 — Can a market be efficient but unfair?

The Uncomfortable Truth

We've shown competitive markets maximize total surplus — the pie is as large as possible. But nothing in that result says the pie is fairly divided.

Equity — the fairness of the distribution of economic well-being among members of society.

Imagine an economy where one person owns everything. If all trades happen at competitive prices, total surplus is maximized — it is efficient. Most people would also call it profoundly unfair.

The "one person owns everything" example is the sharpest tool here — it's a fully efficient outcome that nobody would defend as good. Efficiency is about the size of the pie; equity is about who gets which slice. These are distinct questions requiring distinct tools, and the rest of the section is about not confusing them.

Common Misconception: "Efficient Means Fair"

The mistake: "If the market outcome is efficient, it must be fair." One of the most persistent errors in introductory economics — and it leads to bad policy arguments on both sides.

Efficiency

Is the pie as large as possible? Are all mutually beneficial trades happening?

Equity

Is the pie fairly sliced? Who ends up with how much?

An economy where one person captures 99% of the surplus is just as efficient as one where it's shared equally — as long as all beneficial trades occur. Same-sized pie, very different slices.

Say it plainly: efficiency and fairness are orthogonal. A maximally unequal outcome can be perfectly efficient. Students on both ends of the political spectrum make this error — one side uses "efficient" to mean "good," the other uses "unequal" to mean "inefficient." Both are conflating two separate measurements.

Okun's Leaky Bucket

In 1975, Arthur Okun posed a thought experiment. You want to move money from a rich household to a poor one — but the bucket you carry it in leaks.

Some drips out: administrative costs, and behavioral distortions — the rich person works less because income is taxed; the recipient may work less because of the transfer.

The question is never "should the bucket leak?" — it always does. The question is: how leaky is too leaky? $900 of $1,000 arriving? $500? $100?

Okun's metaphor is 50 years old and still the standard frame for the equity–efficiency trade-off. Every redistributive policy — progressive taxes, welfare, minimum wage, price controls — is a version of the leaky bucket. The "leak" is the deadweight loss from the distortion. The debate is always quantitative: how big is the leak, and is the equity gain worth it?

What the Evidence Says — How Leaky Is the Bucket?

Universal Basic Income: two experiments, two answers

United States (OpenResearch, 2024)

$1,000/month for 3 years. Recipients cut work ~1.3–1.4 hrs/week; earned income fell ~$1,500/year.

Leak: ~13 cents per dollar transferred.

Germany (2024)

€1,200/month for 3 years. Recipients worked the same 40 hrs/week as the control group — just reported better well-being.

Leak: close to zero.

The size of the leak depends on policy design, context, and population. The trade-off is real — but it is not fixed. Zucman estimates a 2% billionaire tax could raise $200–250B globally; Norway's wealth tax, by contrast, drove capital flight to Switzerland. Design is everything.

The two UBI studies are the heart of the evidence slide: same idea, opposite leak rates. That's the lesson — "redistribution kills incentives" and "redistribution is free" are both lazy. The honest answer is "it depends on how you build it." Zucman's wealth-tax work makes the same point: a well-designed tax raises real money; a poorly designed one chases the tax base out of the country.

The Second Welfare Theorem: A Reason for Hope

Second Welfare Theorem — any efficient outcome, including highly equitable ones, can be achieved through a competitive market — if the government first adjusts the initial distribution of resources through lump-sum transfers.

The move: redistribute the starting points — endowments of wealth and opportunity — then let markets run untouched. A lump-sum transfer doesn't depend on what you do next, so it changes no one's marginal incentive. No distortion, no leak.

The catch: true lump-sum transfers are nearly impossible in practice. Almost every real tax changes behavior at the margin — that's why Okun's bucket leaks. But the theorem still tells us something profound: efficiency and equity are not inherently in conflict. The conflict comes from our imperfect tools.

This is the most optimistic result in welfare economics, and students should leave with it. The First Welfare Theorem says markets are efficient; the Second says efficiency doesn't require any particular distribution — you can have efficiency AND equity if you fix the starting line rather than meddling with prices afterward. The catch is practical, not theoretical: we lack clean lump-sum tools. Better tools → less leaky bucket.

Student Loan Forgiveness: Efficiency Meets Equity

The equity case

Forgiveness reduces financial stress, lets graduates buy homes and start businesses, and corrects a system that saddles young people with debt for getting an education.

The efficiency concern

Each $1 of forgiveness generates only ~$0.02–$0.27 in stimulus. ~70% of relief flows to the top 60% of earners — higher earners carry more debt.

This doesn't make forgiveness "wrong" — it makes it an expensive way to achieve its equity goal. Targeting low-income borrowers, or public-service careers, would transfer the same equity with a smaller leak. The framework doesn't tell you whether to forgive — it tells you how to design it so the bucket leaks less.

A live, polarizing policy — handle it as an analysis, not an opinion. The surplus framework is genuinely neutral here: it doesn't say "yes" or "no," it says "if equity is your goal, here's how to hit it more cheaply." That's the most useful thing economics can offer a values-laden debate — not the answer, but the cost of each option.

Where Economics Stops: Amartya Sen

A market can be perfectly Pareto efficient while people lack access to healthcare, education, or political participation. Welfare should be measured not just by income or utility, but by people's capabilities — what they can actually do and be. — after Amartya Sen, Nobel laureate

Efficiency, in Sen's view, is necessary but not sufficient for a good society. The tools of this chapter — CS, PS, total surplus, DWL — tell you how to measure the pie and spot when slices are lost. How to divide it is a question of values, and economics alone cannot answer it.

That's not a weakness of the framework — it's the framework being honest about its own edges.

End the section on intellectual humility. Sen's critique isn't anti-market or anti-economics — it's a reminder that the surplus framework measures one thing well and is silent on others. Tell students: the mark of understanding a tool is knowing what it can't do. They now have the welfare toolkit AND the honest caveat — that's the whole chapter.

Discussion: Where Do You Draw the Line?

6 min — Think-Pair-Share

Bloom's L5: Evaluate — values + analysis

You have $10,000 to redistribute from a wealthy household to a low-income one. But the bucket leaks — 30% is lost in transit (administration, work disincentives, compliance).

Would you still make the transfer? What if 50% leaked? What if 80%? Where do you draw the line — and what does your answer reveal about your values?

There is no "right" answer here. Economics tells you the size of the leak. It cannot tell you how much equity is worth to you. That part is yours.

The closing discussion — deliberately no debrief slide, because there's no answer key. Some students will accept an 80% leak (they weight equity heavily); others bail at 30% (they weight efficiency, or doubt the transfer helps). Both can be coherent. The goal is for students to see that their "line" is a values statement, and that economics sharpened the question without answering it. That is LO 5.4.

This Week's Micro Mission: The Surplus Auction

We'll run a live double auction in class. Half of you are buyers with assigned WTP values; half are sellers with assigned costs. Trade on your phones — when a bid meets an ask, a deal happens.

Round 1 — Free market

Trade freely, then compute your own surplus and the class totals. How close did we get to the theoretical maximum?

Round 2 — Price ceiling

Now trade under a binding ceiling. Recompute. Who gained, who lost — and find the deadweight loss.

Submission: a 300-word reflection comparing the two rounds — your surplus each round, and whether the ceiling made the market better or worse, and for whom.

Preview only — the full activity runs in a later session via a free phone-based double-auction platform. The pedagogical arc: Round 1 shows students the market converging to the efficient equilibrium on its own; Round 2 shows them creating a deadweight loss with their own trades. Living the model beats watching it.

Key Takeaways

1. Surplus measures the gains from trade. CS is below demand, above price. PS is above supply, below price.

2. The competitive equilibrium maximizes total surplus. The First Welfare Theorem — the invisible hand, formalized.

3. Deadweight loss is destroyed surplus. A triangle of trades that should have happened but didn't — not a transfer.

4. Efficiency ≠ equity. The same-sized pie can be sliced fairly or terribly. Efficient says nothing about fair.

5. The Second Welfare Theorem is the reason for hope. Efficiency and equity aren't inherently in conflict — the conflict comes from imperfect redistribution tools. Better tools, less leaky bucket.

Five sentences for the whole chapter. If students remember only one, make it #3 — the transfer-vs-loss distinction is the most-tested and most-misunderstood. If they remember two, add #4 — it's the one that keeps them honest in every policy argument for the rest of their lives.
