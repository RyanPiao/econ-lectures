Section 1 · LO 18.1

Asymmetric Information: When One Side Knows More

A trouble before the deal — and a trouble after.

Two Problems From One Gap

Asymmetric information — one party to a transaction has more or better information than the other. The seller knows the transmission grinds; you don’t.

Adverse selection — before

Hidden type. The wrong people show up: the sickest buy insurance, the worst cars get sold.

Moral hazard — after

Hidden action. Behavior changes once you’re covered: you drive the rental harder.

Before the contract = who shows up.   After the contract = what they do.

The whole section hangs on this before/after split. Adverse selection is hidden information about a fixed TYPE you can't observe; moral hazard is a hidden ACTION that changes because of the contract. Both come from the same root — one side can't see something. Keep the timeline mnemonic: selection before signing, hazard after.

Akerlof’s Lemons (1970)

Two used cars: a reliable plum worth $15,000 and a defective lemon worth $5,000. The seller knows which; you can’t tell them apart.

Half each → you offer the average, $10,000. But no plum owner sells for $10k — only lemon owners accept. You lower your offer… and good cars vanish.

Rejected by 3 top journals as “too trivial.” Won the 2001 Nobel.

Akerlof's "The Market for Lemons" — rejected by the AER, JPE, and Review of Economic Studies before QJE took it. The referees' objection: if true, markets couldn't exist. His answer is the rest of the chapter (signaling/screening). Common misconception to flag: adverse selection isn't about "bad products," it's that the SELECTION of who participates is adverse.

The Market Unravels — and the ACA Spiral

Each healthy person who drops coverage makes the pool sicker — so premiums rise — so more healthy people drop…

When enhanced ACA subsidies expired in 2026, premiums jumped ~114%; insurers proposed 18–20% median hikes.

A textbook death spiral — the lemons logic, played out in health insurance.

Same engine as the used-car market: the less-informed side (insurer, forced to community-rate) can't screen on health, so price rises drive out the healthy first. In California, Silver-plan enrollment fell 69%→51% as healthier consumers "bought down" to Bronze. The individual mandate was an explicit anti-adverse-selection device — pull the healthy back in.

Fighting the Lemons

If Akerlof were the whole story, no one would buy a used car — yet Americans spend $72B/yr on them. Markets fight back:

Third-party verification

Carfax, 150-point inspections, Certified Pre-Owned. Cuts the info gap ≈ 12%.

Regulation

“Lemon laws” mandate disclosure; the ACA mandate pulled healthy buyers in.

Reputation

Yelp, eBay feedback, seller ratings. A low score today = fewer sales tomorrow.

None erase the gap — they raise the cost of selling a lemon enough to keep the plums in the market.

These are the institutional patches Akerlof pointed to. Note that signaling/screening (next section) are the deeper market-driven fixes; this slide is the "verification, rules, reputation" trio. The 12% pricing-accuracy improvement from Carfax-type platforms is the empirical payoff — transparency keeps plums in.

Moral Hazard: Behavior Changes After the Deal

Moral hazard — you take on more risk because someone else bears the cost.

Do you drive a rental as carefully as your own car? Comparison-shop for a prescription when insurance pays?

SVB, 2023: guaranteeing all deposits stopped the run — but signaled “too big to fail.” The FDIC clawed back $16.7B to blunt it.

Moral hazard = hidden action after contracting. The SVB bailout is the cleanest 2023 case: insuring uninsured deposits prevented contagion but rewarded risk-taking, so the special assessment deliberately targeted the banks that benefited most — an attempt to re-internalize the cost. Everyday hook: rental cars, parents' phone plans, all-you-can-eat buffets.

Beating Moral Hazard by Contract Design

Cyber insurance — the fix

$15B market. Insurers require MFA, backups, endpoint detection to get coverage. Result: 64% of targets now refuse to pay ransoms.

Buy Now, Pay Later — the risk

$560B market. Painless 4-payment splits → 41% report a late payment; deep-subprime borrowers ≈ half of originations.

Insurers didn’t beat moral hazard with trust — they beat it with contract design (deductibles, required controls).

The cyber-insurance story is the teaching gem: by requiring security controls, insurers turned a moral-hazard problem into a SCREEN (only firms willing to invest get covered) — bridges to Section 2. BNPL is the cautionary tale, and note the honest caveat: BNPL late payments may be moral hazard OR adverse selection (riskier borrowers self-select). Disentangling the two is one of the hardest empirical problems in economics.

Your Turn — The Smoker’s Premium

Insurers charge smokers higher premiums. The concern they’re guarding against — that the people who most want cheap coverage are the riskiest — is an example of…

A. Adverse selection
B. Moral hazard
C. Signaling
D. A framing effect
Answer: A. Pricing on smoking status is the insurer SCREENING on a hidden type (health risk) before the contract — defending against adverse selection. The follow-up to discuss: if regulators banned smoking-status pricing, smokers would pool with non-smokers, raising everyone's premium and pushing healthy non-smokers out — an adverse-selection spiral. (Note: smoking also has a moral-hazard angle once insured — good place to flag "both" arguments.)

Section 2 · LO 18.2

Signaling & Screening: Restoring Information

The informed side signals. The uninformed side screens.

Signaling: Proving Your Quality (Spence, 1973)

A signal is a costly action the informed party takes to reveal their type — one that’s too expensive for a low type to fake.

A degree may signal more than it teaches: finishing a hard four-year program proves you’re the kind of person who can. The employer needn’t know your micro final grade.

Spence's radical 1973 claim: the value of a diploma may be the SIGNAL, not the skills. A signal works only if it's differentially costly — low-ability types find it prohibitively hard to mimic, so the signal separates types. This is the "sheepskin effect." Don't overclaim: signaling and human-capital (real skills, Ch 14) both operate; the question is the mix.

Is the Degree Signal Fading? Barely.

Google, Apple, IBM, Tesla dropped degree requirements. By 2024, 81% of firms claimed skills-based hiring (up from 57%).

But a Harvard study of 11,000+ postings: non-degree hiring rose just 3.5pp. Fewer than 1 in 700 hires benefited.

Median weekly pay — bachelor’s

$1,754

Median weekly pay — HS only

$953

A lifetime earnings gap of roughly $1.2 million. The signal is remarkably resilient.

The gap between policy (81% claim skills-based hiring) and practice (3.5pp actual change) is the punchline — firms still lean on the degree because it's a cheap, credible screen. The wage premium is the market's revealed valuation of the signal. Connect to Ch 14 human capital and to "Your Career" later.

Screening: Build a Menu That Sorts

Screening — the uninformed party designs choices so the informed party reveals their type by choosing.

Plan A — Bronze

$200/mo, $5,000 deductible. The healthy 22-year-old picks it.

Plan B — Gold

$500/mo, $500 deductible. The 55-year-old with a chronic condition picks it.

By choosing, each customer reveals their health. Same machinery as 2nd-degree price discrimination (Ch 11) — but now the goal is extracting information, not just surplus.

Signaling is initiated by the informed party (the worker gets a degree); screening is designed by the uninformed party (the insurer builds the menu). The menu is incentive-compatible: each type prefers the plan meant for it. Explicit callback to Ch 11 versioning — same self-selection mechanism, different purpose. The 2001 Nobel went to Akerlof (selection), Spence (signaling), Stiglitz (screening) — all three pieces of this section.

Section 3 · LO 18.3

Risk & Uncertainty: Gambling With the Unknown

Now the future is uncertain for everyone — not just one side.

Expected Value: What You Get on Average

Expected value = the probability-weighted average of every outcome.

EV = (0.20 × $100) + (0.80 × $0) = $20

Ticket costs $15? Buy — you pay $15 for $20 on average. Costs $25? Pass.

Sports betting: Americans wagered $157B in FY2025. House edge 5–10%; a $100 parlay returns ≈$85–90.

So why do people take the bet? Prospect theory (§18.4): we overweight small chances of big wins.

EV is the rational benchmark. The house edge = the gap between EV and price, guaranteed over many bets by the law of large numbers. Parlays are worse-than-fair and now 30% of bets — teed up for the prospect-theory probability-weighting point later. The decision rule: take a gamble only if EV > price (for a risk-neutral person).

Risk Aversion: Why People Pay for Certainty

Risk-averse: prefer a sure thing to a gamble with the same EV. Utility is concave.

Gamble: 50/50 $36 or $4. EV = $20, but utility of the gamble (EU=4) sits below U($20)=4.47.

Certainty equivalent = $16. You’d give up $4 to dodge the risk — the risk premium.

Concavity IS risk aversion: because the chord (expected utility) lies below the curve (utility of the mean), a sure $20 beats the 50/50 gamble averaging $20. The certainty equivalent ($16) is the sure amount giving the same utility as the gamble; the gap to EV ($4) is the risk premium — the most you'd pay to shed the risk. This is exactly what funds insurance. Full derivation in Appendix 18A (U=√x).

Risk Premium → Why Insurance Exists (and Fails)

2% chance of a $200k fire → expected loss $4,000. You pay $5,500 — the $1,500 gap is your risk premium. Insurers profit by pooling thousands of independent risks.

When risk is correlated, pooling breaks. CA FAIR Plan policies +137%; Jan-2025 wildfires → $28–45B losses, a $6B shortfall, $1B emergency assessment.

Insurance works because risk-averse people will pay above expected loss (the risk premium) AND because the law of large numbers makes the average predictable when risks are INDEPENDENT. The climate crisis breaks the second condition: when all homes in a fire zone burn together, risks are correlated, the average is no longer stable, and the market collapses — eight carriers left California. Ties to Ch 16 climate externalities.

Section 4 · LO 18.4

Behavioral Economics: Predictably Irrational

Real humans aren’t the calculator from Chapter 8.

Psychology Meets Economics

Behavioral economics — combines psychology with economics to model how people actually decide, especially when they break the rational-choice predictions.

Chapter 8 previewed choice overload, default bias, framing, the decoy effect, mental accounting, endowment. Now we build the systematic framework — and show why it matters for markets and policy.

The key: deviations are predictable — which makes them useful for both helping people and exploiting them.

"Predictably irrational" (Ariely) is the framing — the biases aren't random noise, they're systematic and directional, so you can model and anticipate them. That predictability is double-edged: it's what lets a nudge work AND what lets a dark pattern work. We'll hit six biases: loss aversion, anchoring, present bias, status-quo, framing, sunk cost.

Loss Aversion & Prospect Theory (1979)

Kahneman & Tversky: we judge outcomes against a reference point, and a loss hurts ~2× as much as the same gain feels good.

2024 meta-analysis, 607 estimates: coefficient = 1.955. Coin flip −$10 / +$12? 70–80% refuse.

Explains the Iliad Flow: cancelling Prime feels like losing benefits you already “have.”

Prospect theory replaced expected-utility's wealth-based curve with a reference-dependent, kinked value function: concave in gains, convex in losses, and STEEPER in losses (the kink at the origin is loss aversion). The λ≈2 ratio is one of the most replicated findings in social science. Kahneman (d. March 2024) won the 2002 Nobel — a psychologist, like Herbert Simon (1978). Loss aversion drives the disposition effect (hold losers, sell winners) and seller stickiness in housing.

Anchoring: The Power of a First Number

Anchoring — an initial number, even a random one, drags later judgments toward it.

Write your SSN’s last 2 digits, then bid: top-20% bid $56, bottom-20% bid $16 — same keyboard.

Zillow’s Zestimate anchors buyers — +5.94% buyer surplus, biggest help where info gaps are worst.

The SSN-digit experiment (Ariely, Loewenstein, Prelec) is the canonical demonstration — a provably irrelevant number moves WTP by 3.5×. Anchoring is why "originally $200, now $99" works, why list prices matter, why the first offer in a negotiation has outsized pull. Zillow shows anchors can HELP: an algorithmic estimate reduces uncertainty most in info-poor markets (Fu et al. 2025).

Present Bias: We Can’t Stick to Plans

Present bias (hyperbolic discounting) — we overweight immediate rewards and underweight future consequences, even against our own long-term plans.

BNPL splits $200 into four painless $50s. Users took 6.3 loans/yr; the new product feels real, the future payments abstract.

Under-saving for retirement, procrastinating on a 30%-of-grade paper, gym memberships busy in January, empty by March.

It’s not that we don’t know better — the cost of starting always feels heavier than the distant benefit of finishing.

Present bias = the discount rate between "now and next week" is far steeper than between "week 30 and week 31" — that's the hyperbolic shape, and it generates preference reversals (you plan to save, then don't). It's the engine behind commitment devices and the auto-enrollment nudge coming up. BNPL exploits it; SECURE 2.0 harnesses it for good.

Status-Quo Bias & Framing

Status-quo bias

We stick with the current state — the default — because changing takes effort. The Iliad Flow lives here.

Framing effect

“90% fat-free” beats “10% fat.” “BOGO half-off” beats “25% off” — identical price.

Shrinkflation exploits framing: shrink the box, hold the price. 2025 GAO — it added 1.6pp to cereal and 3.0pp to paper-goods inflation.

Status-quo bias = a special, powerful case of default + inertia + loss aversion (leaving the default feels like a loss). Framing = identical options, different presentation, different choice — violates the rational-choice axiom of description invariance. Shrinkflation is the timely example: consumers track nominal price closely but volume poorly, so firms cut size instead of raising price. Both set up the nudge-vs-dark-pattern distinction.

The Sunk Cost Fallacy

A sunk cost is already paid and unrecoverable. Rational rule (Ch 1): ignore it. Yet we don’t.

You go to the concert because you paid — not because you still want to.

Sony’s Concord: $400M over 8 years, shut down 2 weeks after launch. MR fell below MC long before — sunk-cost psychology kept it alive.

Economist at Work — Saurabh Bhargava (CMU): of 20,000+ employees, 61% chose a dominated health plan — strictly worse on every dimension — overspending 24% of premium. Not stupidity: complexity.

Sunk cost is the bias most directly contradicting Ch 1 marginal thinking — only forward-looking marginal benefit vs marginal cost should matter. Concord is the corporate version; the concert ticket is the personal one. Bhargava's dominated-plan finding is sobering for policy: if smart people can't pick among 4 plans, markets with hundreds of options (Medicare Part D, ACA) need simplification, not just "more choice."

Your Turn — Name That Bias

You keep grinding a video game you stopped enjoying — only because you already bought the $40 battle pass. Which bias is driving you?

A. Anchoring
B. Present bias
C. Sunk cost fallacy
D. Status-quo bias
Answer: C. The $40 is spent and unrecoverable — it should not affect whether the next 50 hours are worth your time. Letting it drive you is the sunk-cost fallacy. Tempting distractor D (status quo): plausible, but the defining feature here is the past payment, not inertia. Good place to ask students for their own sunk-cost stories.

Section 5 · LO 18.5

Nudges & Choice Architecture

If bias is predictable, should policy use it to help people?

Libertarian Paternalism (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008)

Choice architecture

The design of the decision environment — option order, defaults, the framing of information.

Nudge

A small change to that environment that predictably shifts behavior without banning options or changing economic incentives.

Steer toward better outcomes — but keep people free to choose otherwise.

"Libertarian paternalism" sounds like a contradiction and that's the point: paternalist because it steers toward welfare, libertarian because it preserves the freedom to opt out. The freedom-to-opt-out is the dividing line from a mandate AND (we'll see) from a dark pattern. Thaler won the 2017 Nobel. A nudge changes the architecture, not the incentives or the option set.

The Default That Reshaped Retirement

SECURE 2.0 (2025): new plans auto-enroll at 3–10%, auto-escalate yearly. You can still opt out.

Vanguard (5M accounts): 94% participate vs 64% opt-in; auto-savers put away 65% more.

Same options, same match — only the default flipped from opt-in to opt-out.

Possibly the most consequential nudge in history — Madrian & Shea (2001) showed flipping the 401(k) default doubled participation, and that evidence rode all the way to SECURE 2.0 federal law. The power is pure inertia + status-quo bias harnessed FOR the saver. The UK's NEST and India's Jan Dhan (530M+ accounts) are global versions. Caveat from Madrian: defaults also anchor people at the (often too-low) default rate — hence auto-escalation.

When Nudges Fail — or Get Weaponized

Nudges aren’t magic. A 2025 meta of 13 meta-analyses (~30M people): effect d=0.27 — but ~0.004 after correcting for publication bias.

Nudge serves the chooser. Sludge = friction designed to serve the firm — the Iliad Flow, Fortnite dark patterns.

FTC’s “click-to-cancel” rule (2024) was vacated in July 2025 — state laws now a patchwork.

Two cautions. (1) Effect sizes: the Hu et al. (2025) correction is a humbling result — publication bias inflates the literature, so be skeptical of "universal" nudge claims and demand pre-registered trials. Organ-donation opt-out also failed to raise viable donations (logistics dominate). (2) Same science, opposite intent: nudge vs sludge. A dark pattern (Key Concept) is sludge that exploits loss aversion / status-quo / framing against the user. The regulatory fight (click-to-cancel) is ongoing.

The AI Thread & Your Career

Manipulation at scale

AI personalizes anchors, times nudges to your weak moments, A/B-tests thousands of frames. FTC found 250+ firms doing surveillance pricing (2024–25).

Your career

Every job hunt is an info-asymmetry game: you signal (degree, GPA, internships), you screen (Glassdoor, interview questions). AI resume filters add a new layer.

The dark pattern is no longer a static button — it’s a living algorithm that learns which of your biases to exploit. (More in Ch 19.)

AI supercharges everything in this chapter: it can find and exploit each person's specific biases in real time, the static dark pattern becomes adaptive. Surveillance pricing (250+ firms) personalizes prices to your behavior. "Your Career": signaling and screening aren't exam topics — they're the framework for every job search; AI resume screeners may disadvantage non-standard resumes. Bridge to Ch 19 platforms.

Key Takeaways

1. Asymmetric information breeds adverse selection (wrong types show up) and moral hazard (behavior changes after).

2. Signals (degrees) & screens (menus) restore information and keep markets alive.

3. Expected value ranks gambles, but risk aversion means people pay a risk premium — that’s insurance.

4. Behavioral biases — loss aversion, anchoring, present bias, status-quo, framing, sunk cost — are predictable.

5. Nudges reshape choice architecture to help; dark patterns weaponize the same science to harm.

6. The line between a helpful default and a manipulative trap is whom the design serves — the chooser, or the firm.

Exit Ticket

A 50/50 gamble: win $200 or lose $100. A prospect-theory agent values gains at face value but losses at 2.25× (λ = 2.25).

Compute (a) the expected value and (b) the prospect-theory value V. Does she take the bet? One line of work.

Submit on Canvas. 3 minutes.

3 min
(a) EV = 0.5(200) + 0.5(−100) = +$50 — positive, so a risk-neutral EV-maximizer accepts. (b) V = 0.5(200) + 0.5(−2.25 × 100) = 100 − 112.50 = −$12.50 — negative, so the prospect-theory agent REJECTS the same bet. Loss aversion flips a positive-EV gamble into a felt loss.
