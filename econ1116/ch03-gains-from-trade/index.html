Q1 — Opportunity Cost: What You Give Up

Answer to Q1

Opportunity cost = the value of the next-best alternative you give up when you choose one option over another.

It is NOT just the price you paid — it’s the price PLUS what else you could have done with that time / money / attention. Two students at the same lecture can have different OCs.

Today: we draw OC visually as the slope of a curve.

"Quick gut check on Q1. Opportunity cost is the value of the NEXT-best alternative — not all alternatives, not just the dollar price. Cold-call: 'You're sitting in this lecture for 100 minutes. What is YOUR opportunity cost?' Listen for: 'sleeping,' 'work shift,' 'gym,' 'studying for another exam.' That's the OC. Today we put this on a graph."

Q3 — "The U.S. should ban Chinese electronics"

Answer: Normative — "should" is a value judgment. Economists can’t prove it true or false; we can only map the trade-offs.

Pros (claimed)

  • Protects U.S. manufacturing jobs in electronics
  • Shrinks dependence on a strategic rival (chips, telecoms)
  • Domestic firms can scale up without competitive pressure

Cons (claimed)

  • Higher prices on phones, TVs, laptops — everyone pays
  • Lost variety, slower innovation
  • Retaliatory tariffs on U.S. exports (soybeans, aircraft)

Turn it positive with the PPF

Instead of "should we ban?", ask: "If we ban, do we end up at a point inside our PPF (we lose efficiency) or on a different PPF (resources reshuffle to less-productive uses)?" That’s a question economics can answer.

"This is the move I want you to make every time you hear a 'should' claim — translate it into a positive question about WHERE we land on or relative to the PPF. We'll come back to tariffs in Section 4 with hard numbers from the China Shock literature. For now: keep the pros and cons in your head. They're the wage- and price-side of every trade decision."

Example — 2FA Trade-off: Efficiency vs. Security

Two-Factor Authentication (2FA) July 2019

NEU IT rolled out 2FA via Duo — required for VPN, email, Canvas, and most campus services.

By enrolling in 2FA, you take an extra step every sign-in — protecting your identity, your data, and Northeastern’s research / IT infrastructure.

Three choices — you can’t maximize both:

EFFICIENCY

Time / Convenience

Choice 1: Balanced — 2FA on sensitive systems only

Choice 2: Too much? — 2FA every login (high friction)

Choice 3: Too little? — password only (2003-style)

SECURITY

Account / Data safety

Today’s chapter is about every choice that looks like this. Source: security.its.northeastern.edu/mfa.

"Quick show of hands: who finds Duo annoying? Who has been locked out at 11pm before an exam? That friction is the cost of security. NEU made an explicit trade-off in July 2019 — accept the daily friction in exchange for fewer compromised accounts. This is exactly the structure of every PPF question we'll do today: you can't have all of X and all of Y; pick a point on the curve."

The Senior Engineer’s Tuesday

A senior software engineer at a fast-growing startup could answer customer-support tickets faster than the three people on the support team.

Yet she never touches the support queue. She writes code that ships to two million users next quarter.

Why?

The company gives up a little ground on ticket response times. It gains far more from her finishing the new checkout flow.

Same logic, bigger scale

Apple’s engineers in Cupertino could in principle assemble iPhones too. Instead, the iPhone 16 Pro is fabricated by TSMC in Taiwan, packed with Samsung displays from South Korea, and assembled in India and Vietnam — across ~12 countries .

The senior engineer, Mario’s pizza shop, the iPhone supply chain — all explained by one idea: comparative advantage .

One of the most counter-intuitive and powerful insights in all of economics.

"A senior engineer who's better at everything still delegates support tickets — because her opportunity cost of fixing one bug is enormous. Same answer for Apple: scattering production across 12 countries isn't because Apple CAN'T make a phone in Cupertino. It's because doing so would mean giving up something more valuable. This is the LeBron-and-the-landscaper logic, generalized to every team and every country. It's also one of the most counter-intuitive ideas in economics — Ricardo formalized it in 1817 and people still get it wrong today."

The Four Ingredients Required to Build the Modern World

Land

Natural resources — not just soil, but critical minerals like lithium for EV batteries and silicon for chips.

Labor

Human effort — plus "human capital" : education and training (like learning Python) that makes labor productive.

Capital

Manufactured goods used to make other goods (machines, trucks, fabs). Distinct from financial money.

Entrepreneurship

The risk-takers who combine the other three inputs to innovate — and bear the loss if it fails.

🍕 Concrete example: Mario’s Pizza (a few blocks from campus)

Land = the storefront + utilities.   Labor = Mario’s 2 employees + his daughter’s part-time hours.   Capital = deck oven, dough mixer, fridge, POS, pizza paddles, delivery scooter.   Entrepreneurship = Mario himself — signs the lease, takes the loan, decides Sunday how much flour to order. None of the four alone makes pizza.

"These four are the canonical 'factors of production.' Land = stuff that comes out of the ground. Labor = people, including their skills. Capital = tools (NOT money — money is a claim on capital). Entrepreneurship = the person willing to risk their savings to try a new combination. We'll spend whole chapters on each — labor in Ch 14, capital in Ch 15. Today: just notice that EVERY product blends these four. Mario's pizza shop is the recurring example we'll come back to in Ch 9 (firm theory) and Ch 10 (perfect competition)."

Production Methods (a.k.a. Technology)

Same four ingredients, different recipes. The recipe — how much of each input you use — is what economists call technology . There is rarely one right answer.

Capital-Intensive

🚜 Industrial Farming

$$ machines + very few workers per acre. Iowa corn: 2 people farm 2,000 acres — tractors, GPS, drones replace labor.

Labor-Intensive

💻 SaaS Companies

Highly trained workers + cheap capital (laptops, cloud). A 50-person team at a software firm builds a $1B product.

The right method depends on what’s scarce in your context. That’s exactly the trade-off the PPF will draw.

"There's no universally 'correct' way to make a thing. SaaS firms throw expensive humans at the problem because their bottleneck is creativity, not labor cost. Iowa farms throw expensive machines because their bottleneck is land + scale, not labor. Two videos make the contrast concrete — same INPUT logic (labor + capital + land), wildly different RECIPES."

Same Product, Different Recipes: Ford

The same Ford Focus rolls out of three Ford plants on three continents — built with three different recipes. Each plant matches its mix of labor and capital to local input prices .

🇺🇸 Detroit, USA

Capital >> Labor

Heavy automation. ~700 industrial robots welding, painting, lifting frames. ~$30/hr labor cost makes machines cheap by comparison.

🇲🇽 Cuautitlán, Mexico

Capital ≈ Labor

Skilled-labor assembly with selective automation. Lower wages tilt the recipe back toward humans for tasks that would be robotic in Detroit.

🇮🇳 Chennai, India

Labor >> Capital

Labor-intensive. Imported capital is expensive; local labor is abundant and cheap. Same blueprints, different ratios on the line.

The takeaway: same product, three recipes — each chosen because it’s the cheapest recipe given that location’s prices for labor vs. capital. Production methods are responses to prices.

"Ford makes the SAME car — Focus, Fiesta, EcoSport — at all three of these plants. The blueprints are identical. What changes is how many robots vs. how many humans you put on the line. In Detroit, $30/hr labor + cheap electricity = robotize everything. In Chennai, $4/hr labor + expensive imported capital = staff the line. There's no 'best' answer — only the cheapest answer for THAT location's input prices. We'll formalize this cost-minimization logic in Chapter 9. For today: a single firm uses three different recipes because input prices differ. That's the same logic that governs the PPF we're about to draw."

It’s Not Enough to Do Things Right; We Must Do the Right Things

— Peter Drucker. Two distinct kinds of efficiency, easy to confuse.

Production Efficiency

Doing things right

Same output, fewer inputs. No waste — you couldn’t remove an input without losing output.

Example: A bridge engineering team that delivers the same load capacity using 10% less steel. Same output. Less waste.

Geometrically: any point on the PPF.

Allocative Efficiency

Doing the right things

Resources flow to the mix of goods people actually want, in the quantities they want. Relevance.

Cautionary tale: a 2026 typewriter factory could be production-efficient — and still allocatively wasteful, because no one wants typewriters .

Driven by price signals — Ch 4–5.

The two ideas are independent. You can have either one without the other. A market economy needs both : minimize waste and maximize relevance.

"Drucker said it best: 'It is not enough to do things right; we must do the right things.' Production efficiency = doing things right. Allocative efficiency = doing the right things. They are INDEPENDENT. A perfectly run typewriter factory in 2026 is production-efficient AND allocatively dead. A market with millions of bidders + millions of producers tends toward allocative efficiency because price signals redirect resources to where people actually want them. The PPF model in §3.2 is a production-efficiency model. We'll see allocative efficiency formally in Chapter 5 when we put willingness-to-pay against marginal cost."

The Long Arc of Production

For nearly all of human history, production grew slowly. A medieval English peasant had roughly the same yearly output as her great-grandmother — for centuries on end. Generations passed; the typical person’s standard of living barely moved. Then, around 1800 , the Industrial Revolution (Arkwright’s water frame, 1769; Watt’s steam engine, 1776) multiplied what a single hour of labor could produce.

Figure 3.1 — World Average GDP per Capita, 1500–2000 (log scale, 1990 international $)

Caption: World average real GDP per capita on a log scale. For three centuries before 1800, output per person barely moved — humanity lived close to subsistence. The vertical guide marks the onset of the Industrial Revolution; the line bends upward and rises by roughly an order of magnitude in two centuries. Each technology leap (steam, electricity, internal combustion, computing) shifts the world’s production possibilities frontier outward, and the same hours of labor produce far more output. Source: Maddison Project Database (Bolt & van Zanden 2024); DeLong (1998) for early-period estimates. Values in 1990 international dollars.

"Look at the chart. Y axis is on a LOG scale, which means equal vertical distances correspond to equal MULTIPLICATIVE changes — a flat-looking line still represents slow positive growth, and a steep one means doublings. From 1500 to 1800: world GDP per capita went from $566 to $712. That's 25% across THREE CENTURIES. Then a vertical line: 1800. In just two centuries, output per person rose from ~$700 to over $6,000 — a 9-fold increase. The four factors of production didn't change. The TECHNOLOGY linking them did."

Tech Leaps Shift the PPF Outward

The four factors of production didn’t change after 1800. The technology linking them did — each leap added a multiplier to what the same hour of labor could yield. World GDP per capita went from ~$700 (1800) to over $6,000 (2000), a ~9× rise in two centuries.

In PPF terms: each tech leap shifts the entire frontier outward. Same workers + same land + same capital, more output. The PPF isn’t fixed forever — it’s a snapshot of today’s technology.

Four generations — same hole, dozens of times the output. (1) Hand brace (1700s, ~20 holes/hr); (2) geared hand drill (late 1800s — gears multiply each crank); (3) corded electric drill (early-mid 1900s); (4) cordless lithium-ion (today — battery chemistry traces to NASA’s Apollo-era energy-storage R&D).

"Watch the drill story across FOUR generations: (1) 1700s — a carpenter bored a hole with a HAND BRACE, a wooden crank, real upper-body effort, ~20 holes/hour. (2) Late 1800s — the GEARED HAND DRILL (eggbeater) added a gear train so each turn of the handle drove the bit faster. Same human muscle, more output. (3) Early-mid 1900s — electric motors gave us the CORDED ELECTRIC DRILL — same hole in roughly a tenth the time. (4) Late 20th century — lithium-ion battery technology, descended from NASA's Apollo lunar-equipment energy-storage research, produced the CORDLESS DRILL in every American garage today. A weekend DIYer in 2026 carries a tool that, in raw productivity, is dozens of times what an early-20th-century professional had access to. Each transition: the same human hand, multiplied. Multiply across millions of tools and you get the bend in the GDP chart on the previous slide."

The Box That Changed the World

April 1956. The Ideal X — a converted oil tanker — sails Newark to Houston with 58 metal boxes on board. Each box packed at the shipper’s loading dock and craned straight onto truck trailers in Houston. No stevedore opens a single box.

Cost: 16¢/ton , vs. industry average of $5.83. A ~30× drop. (Brainchild of trucker Malcolm McLean .)


Production efficiency ↑

Trans-Pacific shipping cost fell ~90% (1960–2000). NYC longshoremen: 35,000 (1955) → <4,000 (2010), while cargo volume rose by orders of magnitude.


Allocative efficiency ↑

Shirts sewn in Dhaka, worn in Boston. Grapes from Chile in February. iPhone parts across 12 countries. Goods flow to whoever values them most.

"Containerization is the supply-shifter we'll meet again in Ch 4. Before the box: gangs of stevedores hand-loading mixed cargo, ships in port for a week, theft + damage endemic, labor was 60-70% of total cost. After: standardized 20- and 40-foot boxes, ships turn around in HOURS, cranes move tens of thousands of tons per day. Both efficiencies improved at once. This is also why iPhones are assembled across 12 countries — that pattern isn't possible without cheap shipping."

The Production Possibilities Frontier

LO 3.2 — Draw and interpret a PPF

What Is a PPF?

Imagine an economy producing only wheat and steel with fixed resources.

Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF) — a curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are used efficiently.

The PPF combines Ch 1 opportunity cost + Ch 2 models into one tool.

Drag the sliders to shift the PPF (technology) or move the production point (along the curve).

Figure 3.2 — Interactive PPF (textbook). Sliders ↓ below the chart.

"Same SVG as the textbook's interactive PPF figure. Walk through the three regions: yellow point inside (idle resources), red point outside (unattainable without growth), green dot on the curve (efficient). The bowed shape comes from increasing opportunity cost — workers aren't equally good at all tasks."

Three Types of Points

An economy that produces only Goods + Services

A On the frontier

Efficient. All resources in use. More of one good = less of the other.

B Inside the frontier

Inefficient. Idle resources — could produce more of both goods AND services.

C Outside the frontier

Unattainable — need growth (technology, capital, education) to reach it.

COVID-19 example: April 2020 U.S. capacity utilization crashed to 64.2% — the economy was at Point B. Recovery = moving back to the frontier.

"Real economies produce GOODS (cars, food, phones) and SERVICES (haircuts, education, healthcare). The PPF traces the trade-off. After the pandemic shutdowns, many economies were operating well inside their PPFs (Point B). Recovery wasn't inventing new technology — it was putting existing resources back to work."

Shifting the Frontier

Tech / capital / trade → PPF shifts outward

Recovery moves an economy back to the PPF. To get past it, the whole frontier has to shift outward.

Three ways the PPF shifts out:

  • Technology — same workers, more output.
  • Capital & education — better tools, better-trained workers.
  • Trade itself — cheaper inputs free up labor for higher-value work.

Asymmetric shifts (only services improve → PPF stretches in one direction) are common in real life.

Real-world shifters

U.S. CHIPS Act (2022): $52B for domestic semiconductor capacity → PPF shifts outward in chips.

Generative AI (2023–): code, copywriting, design productivity step-change → service-sector PPF shifts.

Texas grid storms (2021, 2024): negative shocks shift the PPF inward until repaired.

"Recovery vs. growth distinction matters for policy. Stimulus moves us back to the frontier; R&D, education, and trade openness shift the frontier itself. The CHIPS Act is a deliberate attempt to shift the frontier in semiconductors. AI is doing it organically across white-collar work."

Why the PPF Bows Outward

The slope of the PPF = opportunity cost .

Increasing opportunity cost: Resources aren't equally suited to all tasks. Shifting the worst wheat farmers to steel first is cheap. Shifting the best farmers later is expensive.

This is why most real PPFs are bowed outward (concave to the origin).

"If resources were identical, the PPF would be a straight line — constant OC. But in the real world, some workers are great steelworkers and terrible farmers, so each additional ton of steel costs more wheat."

Which Point Is “Better”? — Consumption vs. Saving

All three points below are equally efficient (on the PPF). The difference: how you split today’s output between consumption (food, rent, fun) and saving / investment (cash, stocks, bonds, retirement). Today’s choice sets tomorrow’s frontier.

🍕 Live for Today

Today: max happiness, dine out, latest gadgets.
Tomorrow: small PPF growth. Lean retirement, fragile to shocks.

⚖️ Balanced

Today: comfortable, some splurges, some saving.
Tomorrow: steady growth. Solid retirement on track.

💰 Tighten the Belt

Today: frugal, fewer splurges.
Tomorrow: largest PPF shift. Wealthy retirement, financial cushion.

Same logic at the country level: national debt is a country choosing “more consumption today, less for tomorrow.” The bill comes due on future generations — you all . Every $1 of deficit consumed today is $1 of investment your generation didn’t get to make.

"All three of these points are EFFICIENT — they're all on the PPF. The PPF can't tell you which is 'better.' That's a values question. But economics CAN tell you the consequences. More consumption today = smaller PPF tomorrow. More saving = bigger PPF tomorrow. Same logic for countries: U.S. national debt is now ~$36T. That's consumption pulled forward. The interest payments come out of YOUR future wages and YOUR future government services. This is why retirement saving matters — and why fiscal policy matters."

Section 3

Absolute vs. Comparative Advantage

LO 3.3 — Calculate from a productivity table

Two Concepts That Sound Similar...

Absolute Advantage

Can produce more with the same resources.

Compares productivity levels .

Comparative Advantage

Can produce at lower opportunity cost .

Compares what you give up .

David Ricardo, 1817. Tested by Costinot & Donaldson (2012): R² = 0.93 across 55 countries. A 200-year-old theory that still works.

"Absolute advantage is intuitive: who's faster. Comparative advantage is the real insight: who gives up less. This distinction drives all of trade theory."

Worked Example: U.S. vs. Mexico

Model assumptions

1. Two countries — United States & Mexico.

2. Two goods — smartphones (📱) & avocados (🥑).

3. Each country has 100 workers — workers are the only input we model.

4. Constant returns to labor — doubling workers doubles output (linear PPF).

Strip everything else away — capital, land, prices, tech — to isolate the comparative advantage insight.

Output per worker per year

📱 Smartphones 🥑 Avocados (tons)
🇺🇸 United States 10 ✓ 5 ✓
🇲🇽 Mexico 2 4

Absolute advantage = who produces MORE per worker

📱 U.S. : 10 phones/worker > Mexico: 2 → U.S. wins by 5×.
🥑 U.S. : 5 tons/worker > Mexico: 4 → U.S. wins by 1.25×.

The U.S. has absolute advantage in both goods. Same workers, more output of everything.

So... why would the U.S. ever trade with Mexico?

"Strip everything down to the bare model: 2 countries, 2 goods, only labor matters. The table shows: U.S. is better at BOTH — 5x better at phones and 1.25x better at avocados. Most students' intuition: no reason to trade. Build that tension — we'll resolve it on the next slide with opportunity cost."

Calculating Opportunity Costs

🇺🇸 United States

Step 1 — trade-off identity

With all 100 workers on one good: 10 📱 per worker → 1,000 📱 OR 5 🥑 per worker → 500 🥑 .

1,000 📱 ≡ 500 🥑

Step 2 — OC of 1 📱

Divide both sides by 1,000:

1 📱 = 500 / 1,000 =   0.5 🥑

Step 3 — OC of 1 🥑

Divide both sides by 500:

1 🥑 = 1,000 / 500 =   2 📱

🇲🇽 Mexico

Step 1 — trade-off identity

With all 100 workers: 2 📱 per worker → 200 📱 OR 4 🥑 per worker → 400 🥑 .

200 📱 ≡ 400 🥑

Step 2 — OC of 1 📱

Divide both sides by 200:

1 📱 = 400 / 200 =   2 🥑

Step 3 — OC of 1 🥑

Divide both sides by 400:

1 🥑 = 200 / 400 =   0.5 📱

Comparison — who has the LOWER opportunity cost?

OC of 1 📱 OC of 1 🥑
🇺🇸 U.S. 0.5 🥑  ✓ LOWER → CA in 📱 2 📱
🇲🇽 Mexico 2 🥑 0.5 📱  ✓ LOWER → CA in 🥑
"Walk through the SAME 3 steps for each country: (1) trade-off identity from the productivity table — if all workers go to one good vs the other; (2) divide for OC of 1 phone; (3) divide for OC of 1 avocado. Different countries, same algebra. Then compare: U.S. gives up only 0.5 avocados per phone vs Mexico gives up 2. U.S. has LOWER OC for smartphones → CA in phones. Mirror image for avocados: Mexico LOWER → CA in avocados. THAT's comparative advantage. Note that even though the U.S. has absolute advantage in BOTH (previous slide), each country still has CA in exactly ONE good."

Reality: Comparative advantage is always reciprocal . If you have it in one good, your partner has it in the other.

"This is the aha moment. Even though the U.S. is better at BOTH, Mexico gives up less for avocados. Trade lets each country focus on its RELATIVE strength."

The Trade Price Must Be in the Zone

Recall — each country’s OC (from p. 32)

🇺🇸 U.S.: 1 📱 = 0.5 🥑 (or 1 🥑 = 2 📱)
🇲🇽 Mexico: 1 📱 = 2 🥑 (or 1 🥑 = 0.5 📱)

Trade price must be between the two domestic OCs:

0.5 < P trade < 2

Below 0.5 → U.S. refuses (would rather make at home).
Above 2.0 → Mexico refuses (same reason).

Why 1 📱 = 1 🥑 is reasonable

1 is the midpoint of the OC range — geometric mean of 0.5 and 2 = √1 = 1 . At 1:1:

  • U.S. pays 1 🥑 per phone vs domestic OC 0.5 — gains 0.5 🥑/phone
  • Mexico pays 1 📱 per avocado vs domestic OC 0.5 — gains 0.5 📱/avocado

Both sides gain symmetrically — that’s why this lecture uses 1:1.

The mutual-gains zone for an avocado-per-smartphone trade price.



Where the actual market price lands inside this zone depends on bargaining power — the supply & demand mechanism in Chapter 4 .

"Walk through: (1) Recall the two countries' OCs from page 32 — these define the zone. (2) Trade price must lie strictly between those OCs: 0.5 < P < 2. Below 0.5 the U.S. would rather make at home; above 2 the same is true for Mexico. (3) Why 1:1 in this lecture? It's the GEOMETRIC mean of 0.5 and 2 — symmetric midpoint. Both sides gain by 0.5 units per traded unit. (4) Where the actual market price lands inside the zone depends on bargaining power — Ch4 supply-demand will give us the mechanism. Trade in the Real World

LO 3.5 — Winners, losers, and the iPhone

Why Countries Specialize

Comparative advantage isn’t random — it grows out of four concrete sources:

Resources & geography

Saudi oil, Colombian coffee, Canadian lumber, Chilean copper. Climate and natural endowments lock in low OC for some goods.

Human capital & technology

Taiwan in semiconductors (TSMC), South Korea in shipbuilding, Israel in cybersecurity. Years of investment in skills and R&D.

Labor cost structure

Bangladesh garments, Vietnam manufacturing. When wages are low relative to productivity, the OC of labor-heavy goods is low.

Proximity & logistics

Mexico nearshoring (passed China as #1 U.S. supplier in 2023). Shipping costs and supply-chain resilience become CA in their own right.

These sources change over time — CA isn’t fixed. Investment in capital and skills can build it; tariffs and policy can shift it.

"Comparative advantage isn't a national personality trait. It emerges from concrete things: rocks, climate, schools, roads. South Korea in 1960 was poorer than Ghana — by 1990 it was a shipbuilding giant. They invested. Conversely, oil-rich countries can lose CA in non-oil goods because the currency rises (Dutch disease). The map redraws itself."

Economist at Work — David Autor and the China Shock

Autor, Dorn & Hanson (2013, 2021): U.S. manufacturing employment, 1999–2019, by China-import exposure.

Theory says trade is positive-sum. The China Shock literature asks the harder question: positive-sum for whom ?

U.S. exposed to Chinese imports (1999–2011) lost 1–2 million manufacturing jobs. Wages stayed depressed for two decades. Disability claims, divorce, and "deaths of despair" rose.

Recovery was generational , not the textbook’s "frictional" few months. Displaced workers never reached pre-shock wages.

Counter-view: Robert Kaestner (NBER, 2023) argues the magnitudes shrink with proper controls; aggregate U.S. consumers saved $400–$500/year on goods.

Positive analysis: total pie grew.   Normative question: distribution — we revisit it in Ch 7 (policy) and Ch 14 (labor).

"Autor, Dorn, and Hanson built a county-level instrument exploiting variation in pre-shock industrial mix. Their claim: aggregate trade gains coexisted with concentrated, persistent losses. Kaestner's critique is that some of the labor-supply response should be attributed to other forces (opioid crisis, automation). Both can be right — the point is that the textbook 'compensate the losers' line is doing a lot of work that policy hasn't done. This is the bridge to Ch 7 (transfers) and Ch 14 (labor markets)."

The Dark Side of "Gains from Trade"

China Shock showed losers in our labor market. Other losers live in the countries that became "specialized" — without bargaining power, enforced labor laws, or unions. Three stories Ricardo doesn't tell:

👕

Fast fashion

Bangladesh garment workers stitch for Western retailers at roughly $0.13/hour. The 2013 Rana Plaza factory collapse killed 1,134 workers in a single day — while brands disputed which orders had been made there.

Banana republic

Guatemala, 1954. The United Fruit Co. lobbied the CIA to overthrow a democratically-elected government that threatened banana-plantation profits. The phrase "banana republic" was coined here — trade-dependent regime captured by one buyer.

How Guatemala fell

🍫

Dark chocolate

Roughly 1.5 million children work the cocoa farms of Ivory Coast and Ghana — the source of most of the world's chocolate. Major brands pledged in 2001 (Harkin-Engel Protocol) to end child labor and have missed every deadline since.

The Dark Side of Chocolate

Hold two ideas at once. Ricardo's math says trade is positive-sum — the total pie is bigger. How the pie gets split depends on bargaining power, enforceable laws, and capital mobility. If almost all the gains accrue to shareholders and consumers in rich countries, is that your definition of "gains from trade"?

"I want you to leave this lecture holding two things at once. Ricardo is right: there ARE absolute gains from specialization — total output grows. AND the distribution of those gains depends on who has bargaining power. Bangladeshi seamstresses don't bargain against Walmart. Guatemalan farmers in 1954 couldn't bargain against the U.S. government. Ivorian cocoa farmers can't pick up their land and move to a country with better wages. We'll come back to this in Ch 7 (transfers and policy) and Ch 14 (labor markets and unions). For now, just know that 'positive-sum' is a positive-analysis claim about the total — not a normative claim that everyone is better off. The three resources on this slide are optional viewing/reading; if you watch one, the Bangladesh fashion documentary is the shortest and the most direct connection to clothes you probably own."

Beyond Ricardo: Why Similar Countries Trade

Ricardo’s theory says different countries trade. So why do Germany and the U.S. ship cars to each other? Why does Japan import wine from France and export sake to France?

Intra-industry trade: countries trading the same kind of good back and forth. Now ~25% of world goods trade. Ricardo can’t explain it — we need Krugman’s "New Trade Theory" (1979) .

Krugman won the 2008 Nobel Prize for this.

Two new ingredients

  • Economies of scale. Larger production runs lower average cost. The world supports a few global aerospace winners (Boeing, Airbus) — not 195 national ones.
  • Variety preference. BMW ≠ Toyota ≠ Honda. Consumers value choice; trade lets each producer specialize in a niche while serving global demand.

Result: similar countries gain from trade even when their PPFs look identical.

"Half the trade you actually see — Germany shipping cars to the U.S. and the U.S. shipping cars to Germany — is invisible to Ricardo. Krugman's contribution: scale economies + variety preference. Both can be net gainers when their auto industries each specialize in a niche (German luxury, U.S. SUVs, Japanese small-engine). Trade isn't only about WHAT we make — it's also about how MANY varieties we get to consume."

The Case For and Against Free Trade

For

  • Specialization grows the total pie
  • Lower prices → higher real wages
  • >1B people lifted out of extreme poverty since 1990
  • Variety & innovation pressure on domestic firms

Against

  • Job displacement is local, generational
  • National security (chips, pharma, energy)
  • Reshoring limits if foreign suppliers refuse
  • Environmental cost of long supply chains
  • "Race to the bottom" on labor & environment

Most economists agree: the net gains are real, but compensation for the losers requires policy — trade alone won’t do it.

"This is where the chapter ends pedagogically. The economic logic of trade is rock-solid. The policy debate is about WHO bears the costs and HOW we redistribute the gains. Tariffs and reshoring trade aggregate efficiency for visible local protection. Sometimes that's the right call (national security on chips). Often it's not. The mantra: 'compensate the losers' is right; the practice is hard."

Zero-sum fallacy: “If they win, we lose.” Comparative advantage shows the opposite — both gain because each gives up less by specializing in what it’s relatively best at.

The PPF shows trade-offs. On = efficient. Inside = wasteful. Outside = growth needed.

OC increases. PPF bows out because resources aren't equally suited to all tasks.

Comparative advantage drives trade. Even if one side is better at everything, both gain.

Trade expands consumption beyond the PPF. Both parties consume more than they could alone.
