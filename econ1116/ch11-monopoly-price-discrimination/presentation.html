The Polar Opposite of Perfect Competition

Last chapter

Price-taker. Identical product, free entry, sells all it wants at the market price — but can’t charge a penny more. Flat demand: P = MR.

This chapter

Monopoly. The sole seller, no close substitutes. It is the industry, so it faces the entire downward-sloping demand curve.

Monopoly — a single firm is the only seller of a good with no close substitutes.  Market power — the ability to raise price without losing all your customers (a downward-sloping firm demand curve). A monopolist has the maximum of it.

Market power is a spectrum: perfect competition (none) → monopolistic competition & oligopoly (some, Ch 12–13) → monopoly (the most).

Anchor the two definitions side by side. The single most important structural fact: a monopolist faces the WHOLE market demand curve, which slopes down. That one fact drives everything in §11.2–11.3. Market power is not all-or-nothing — it's a dial we'll turn through the next three chapters.

Barriers to Entry — the Moat

Barriers to entry — obstacles that keep new firms out, letting incumbents sustain economic profit in the long run.

20th century

Physical moats — railroads, oil wells, steel mills. Barriers made of capital.

21st century

Digital moats — software ecosystems, data, network effects. Lock in billions at near-zero marginal cost.

The barrier is the THING that separates monopoly from competition. The 20th-vs-21st-century framing (LO 11.1) recurs all section: the form of the barrier changed from physical capital to digital lock-in, but the economics is identical — rivals can't enter, so profit survives.

Five Sources of Monopoly Power

Most real monopolies combine several — but each starts here.

1. Control of a key input. Own a resource rivals can’t replicate — ore, land… or software.

2. Switching costs & network effects. The cost of leaving — and the pull of where everyone already is.

3. Product differentiation & branding. Make consumers see your product as genuinely one-of-a-kind.

4. Economies of scale & natural monopoly. One firm serves the whole market more cheaply than two.

5. Government-created monopoly. Patents, franchises, licenses — the state grants exclusivity.

The five-source list is the spine of LO 11.1. Preview that we'll do one slide each, every example pairing an old-economy case with a 2020s digital one. Number 4 (natural monopoly) is the one with a real cost-curve story; the others are mostly about exclusion.

2 · Switching Costs & Network Effects

Switching cost — what you pay in money, time, or lost data to leave. Years of apps, an Apple Watch, iMessage, the blue bubble … ~58–60% of US users stay on iPhone, so Apple charges a 15–30% App Store cut.

Network effect — the product is worth more as more people use it. Consoles, social media, messaging → the market tips toward one platform, with no patent or grant required.

Distinguish the two cousins: switching cost is about the pain of LEAVING (iPhone ecosystem); network effect is about the pull of where everyone ALREADY is (consoles, social). Barriers to Switch — the Walled Garden

“It’s fine to build a beautiful garden for users to enjoy — but a company should also leave a window to the outside world, because there may be a better view beyond the wall. If the firm keeps the door open, it will greatly increase consumer surplus.”
— Dr. Piao
The takeaway of the switching-cost story. 4 · Economies of Scale & Natural Monopoly

Natural monopoly — one firm can supply the whole market at lower average cost than two could. Arises with very high fixed cost + very low marginal cost.

Electric grid. ~$21.9B/yr US fixed cost; the cost of one more kWh is tiny. Two competing grids = both charge more.

Starlink. ~$10B+ sunk, 8,300+ satellites, 10M+ subscribers (2026). One more subscriber ≈ the cost of mailing a dish. A rival must rebuild the whole constellation.

This is the only source with a genuine cost-curve story (ties straight back to Ch 9 LRATC falling continuously). The test: does ATC keep falling across the whole relevant range? If yes, one firm is cheaper — splitting the market raises everyone's cost. Starlink is the 21st-century natural monopoly “in the making.” This sets up the regulation discussion in §11.5. Example: Electricity Transmission

Generation → Transmission ($21.9B/yr) → Distribution — one company can wire all of Boston’s 279,495 homes.

A second $21.9B grid would split the customers → both firms’ ATC rises. One firm is cheaper — a natural monopoly.

Natural Monopolies

A natural monopoly is an extreme economy of scale: LRATC declines continuously as output rises.

Efficient as one firm — cheapest for a single firm to make the whole industry output.

High fixed cost, low MC — the grid is huge to build, ~free to use.

Splitting raises cost — two firms each duplicate the fixed cost: ATC jumps $100 → $200.

The diagram behind the electricity example. LRATC slopes down the whole way, so bigger = cheaper. One firm at point b serves all 279,495 at $100/mo. Split the market and each firm sits at point a — half the output, $200/mo. Competition here would DOUBLE costs. That’s why regulators grant (and then regulate) a single provider.

The Math of Zero Marginal Cost

The competitor’s dilemma: a rival spends $10B to build a parallel constellation, then must split the global customers. Neither firm gets enough to cover its $10B debt — both go bankrupt.

The math dictates only one survives. That is a natural monopoly.

Why no one challenges Starlink. The cost curve is an L: a $10B wall up front, then a flat ~$0 floor. A rival who duplicates the $10B and splits the market can’t cover the debt at half the subscribers — so both fail. The fixed-cost wall + zero MC is exactly the natural-monopoly condition; the “market” picks one winner.

The USPS holds an exclusive franchise for delivering regular first-class mail to your mailbox.

USPS is the classic government-granted monopoly — but only on FIRST-CLASS mail to your mailbox. The contrast is the lesson: where the government DOESN'T grant exclusivity (packages, overnight), competition (FedEx/UPS) thrives. The monopoly is exactly as wide as the legal grant.

Government IP — Copyright

A copyright protects ideas — books, music, art, software code — so creators benefit from their work.

It lets Dan Harmon & Justin Roiland, creators of Rick & Morty, earn royalties on every episode (new or repeated).

Copyright = a government-granted monopoly over a specific creative expression. The royalty stream (every airing of every episode) is the monopoly profit the grant creates — the incentive to make the show in the first place. Same logic as patents, applied to creative work.

Government IP — Patents

Patents give inventors of new products or processes legal protection for a limited period — usually 20 years.

Vital in pharma, tech, and auto manufacturing — the patent window is what makes huge R&D bets worth taking.

Patents are the most economically consequential government grant (the Ozempic $969 example). The 20-year window is the deliberate trade-off: a temporary monopoly (with DWL) buys the R&D incentive. Connects directly to the innovation defense in §11.3 and the pharma/patents discussion in Ch 16.

Beyond Patents — Trade Secrets

Coca-Cola’s formula — the classic trade secret.

A trade secret gives a competitive edge because the information isn’t publicly known — and it can outlast a 20-year patent.

Not registered with any government agency — protection relies entirely on keeping it confidential.

The contrast within the IP discussion: a trade secret is the ONE IP protection the government does NOT grant or register. Coca-Cola never patented its formula (a patent would publish it and expire in 20 years) — secrecy can protect it forever, but only as long as no one leaks it. Patent = disclose + 20-year monopoly; trade secret = never disclose, monopoly for as long as you can keep the secret.

Licensing & Government Regulation

NYC taxi medallions — a license that capped the number of cabs.

How a Monopolist Sets Price

One demand curve, one rule: MR = MC — and why MR is always below price

The Monopolist’s Demand Curve

One island, one airline — fill all 50 seats only by dropping the fare to $200.

The monopolist’s demand curve slopes downward: to sell more, it must cut the price (unlike Ch 10’s flat price-taker demand).

A monopolist faces the market demand curve — it is the sole producer, so market demand = the firm’s demand.

The clean intro to §11.2, before the quantity-vs-price-effect detail. The single structural fact: a monopolist IS the industry, so it faces the entire (downward-sloping) market demand curve — market demand = firm demand. This is the opposite of the Ch 10 price-taker’s flat demand. Everything in §11.2–11.3 follows from this one downward slope. The $200 @ Q=50 ties to the airline example on the next slides.

The intuitive setup for the MR=MC table. The Monopolist Faces the Whole Demand Curve

To sell one more unit, it must cut the price — on every unit. That splits each new sale into two opposing forces:

Quantity effect (+) — revenue from the new unit sold.

Price effect (−) — revenue lost on all the units that used to sell at the higher price.

The price effect always drags revenue down, so  MR < P  at every quantity. The MR curve lies below demand, with twice the slope.

Contrast with Alex's pizza (Ch 10): when Alex sells her 42nd slice she gets $10 and her earlier 41 customers pay nothing less — no price effect, so P = MR. A monopolist selling its 51st unit cuts the price on ALL 51. That single difference (price effect) is why MR < P and why monopoly restricts output. The “twice the slope” rule is worth stating now; we'll see it on the chart.

The Five-Step Method — Any Monopoly Problem

  1. Find the quantity where MR = MC.
  2. Read Q* off the horizontal axis.
  3. Read P* up on the demand curve (not MR!).
  4. Find ATC at Q*.
  5. Profit = (P* − ATC) × Q* — the rectangle.

Smooth curves land at Q* ≈ 45, P* = $218; the table’s 10-seat batches round to Q = 50, P = $200. Same logic.

Common Misconception

“A monopolist charges the highest possible price.”

It could charge $344 — but then it sells only 10 seats and earns just $1,440. The profit-maximizing price is $200, well below the top of the demand schedule.

A monopolist doesn’t charge as much as possible — it charges where MR = MC, which pins it to one specific point on the demand curve. Higher price ⇒ too few buyers; lower price ⇒ margin too thin.

The intuition: maximize the AREA of the profit rectangle, balancing margin against volume. Charging the choke price maximizes per-unit margin but kills volume. This misconception is on every exam — have them state the corrected version in their own words.

Monopoly Is Not a Guarantee of Profit

If demand sits entirely below ATC, the monopolist still produces at MR = MC — but the price it reads off demand is below ATC. It loses money.

Short run: keep operating if P > AVC. Long run: exit — exactly like a competitive firm.

Monopoly gives the power to restrict output and raise price. It does not give the power to create demand that isn’t there. Being the sole seller of something nobody wants is not profitable.

Also a myth: “Monopoly is illegal.” Having one is legal. Abusing anticompetitive tactics to get or keep one is not — that’s §11.5.

Monopoly Breaks the First Welfare Theorem

P > MC means the last buyer values the good above its cost — yet the sale is blocked.

The Societal Tax: A 30% Price Floor

Apple extends its monopoly from the iPhone into the App Store — the only channel to reach an iPhone user. Its marginal cost to host one more app is fractions of a cent, yet it takes a 30% cut of every sale.

Consumers would value those apps above the cost to make them but below the post-cut price — so the transaction never happens. The software never built, the utility never felt, is the deadweight loss.

An Attempt at First-Degree PD — Amazon, 2000

Could a seller read each shopper’s willingness to pay online — and quietly charge each a different price? In September 2000, Amazon tried.

Shoppers who deleted their cookies (so Amazon no longer recognized them as regulars) saw lower prices. One customer watched a DVD fall from $26.24 → $22.74.

That’s textbook first-degree PD: a personalized price aimed at each buyer’s own WTP.

The digital version of the car-dealer example. Amazon's 2000 "price test" effectively charged identifiable repeat customers more; clearing cookies (becoming an unknown shopper) revealed a lower price. The DVD anecdote ($26.24 vs $22.74) is the canonical story. Sets up the backlash next.

The Aftermath

The backlash was immediate — customers felt cheated, and the story made national news.

“We have never tested and we never will test prices based on customer demographics.”

— Jeff Bezos, 2000

The reputational cost of visible personalized pricing. Bezos's flat denial + refunds. Even though firms have always price-discriminated, doing it openly on named customers triggers a fairness backlash (the Internet Policy Review survey: most people call online PD "unfair and unacceptable").

Amazon’s Strategy Today

After the backlash, Amazon and most e-tailers avoid true first-degree PD. Two tools do the work instead:

1. Dynamic pricing (looks second-degree) — the price moves for everyone with demand, rivals, time of day, and inventory. Not personalized — just a hyper-fast algorithm.

2. Personalized marketing — your history triggers a coupon or recommendation aimed at you. Same effect as a personal price — but framed as a discount for you, never a surcharge.

Amazon Personalize — your data drives what you see (and the deal you get)

The two legal, low-backlash substitutes for first-degree PD. Dynamic pricing = same price to all, changing fast (Uber surge is the extreme). Second-Degree — Build a Menu, Let Buyers Sort Themselves

The firm can’t tell who you are — so it designs options and lets you reveal your type by choosing.

Quantity discounts. Costco 50-bag box at $0.45/bag vs $0.50 at the corner store.

The defining feature: SELF-SELECTION. The seller doesn't observe your type; the menu makes you reveal it. Netflix is the cleanest modern case and ties back to elasticity (Ch 5): the ad tier captures the elastic segment without dropping price for everyone. Bundling's CD→iTunes→Spotify arc is a great 25-year story.

Buying in bulk → a lower cost per unit. The big-box buyer reveals low per-unit WTP; the convenience buyer pays more. Pricing by quantity is second-degree PD.

Quantity discounts are the most everyday second-degree case: the firm posts a price schedule that falls with quantity, and buyers sort themselves by how much they purchase. Costco's whole model is the bulk version for low-per-unit-WTP shoppers; the corner store serves the high-convenience single-bag buyer. Same chips, different per-unit price — set by the buyer's own choice.

Bundling — the CD-Era “Forced Bundle”

The album bundles 1 hit with 11 filler tracks into one $15 product. Want just the hit? You still buy all 12.

How the bundle captures consumer surplus:

  • A fan who loves all 12 songs would gladly pay $15 — nothing extra captured.
  • A casual listener who wants only the hit also pays $15 — the firm captures the surplus she'd have kept if she could buy one song.

In the 1990s the marginal cost of pressing & shipping a CD was high — selling single songs wasn't worth it, so the bundle was the product.

Bundling is second-degree PD because it screens on WTP without observing type. The low-demand buyer who values only one song is forced to pay the full bundle price, surrendering surplus. Simplified for class: a bundle lets the firm charge one high price that the low-demand buyer still pays, transferring her surplus to the producer.

Tech Unbundles — iTunes à la Carte

The MP3 pushed the marginal cost of one more song to ≈ $0. iTunes (2003) sold songs individually at $0.99.

  • Consumer surplus ↑: the one-song buyer pays $0.99, not $15.
  • Producer revenue ↓: buyers who'd have paid $15 now grab just their 3 favorites = $2.97.

Unbundling handed surplus back to consumers — and collapsed total industry revenue.

Unbundling reverses the CD-era surplus grab: once a single song costs ~$0 to deliver, competition (and Apple) forces a la carte pricing, returning surplus to low-demand buyers and erasing the bundle premium. Technology (MC → 0) reshapes which PD strategy is feasible.

The “All-You-Can-Eat” Bundle — Spotify

A subscription is a new kind of bundle: instead of 12 songs, Spotify bundles all 100M songs for one flat monthly price.

Self-selection (versioning):

  • Low-WTP / price-sensitive → the free, ad-supported tier.
  • High-WTP → Premium ($10.99/mo) — ad-free, offline, any order.

It re-bundles the unbundled customer — a stable, high-value revenue stream from the highest-WTP listeners.

Spotify's tiers are textbook second-degree versioning: the free/ad tier and the $10.99 premium tier are the menu, and listeners self-select by WTP. The all-you-can-eat subscription also solves the unbundling revenue problem — it re-aggregates demand into a recurring, high-margin stream.

Versioning — Buyers Self-Select

Offer a menu of versions and let each buyer voluntarily pick the one best for them — revealing their type. The firm prices by the version chosen, not by who you are. To make buyers split, firms widen the gap between tiers — sometimes intentionally limiting the cheaper one (the “damaged-goods” idea: stripped Basic Economy, a deliberately crippled chip).

Spotify is textbook versioning: Individual ($11.99), Duo ($16.99 / 2 accounts), Family ($19.99 / up to 6 accounts). Households self-select by size, and the cheaper tiers are deliberately capped (one account, same-address rules) so higher-value households pay more.

This is the mechanism behind all second-degree PD: incentive-compatible menus. The counter-intuitive result — firms sometimes spend money to make a product WORSE (damaged goods: disabled chip features, stripped Basic Economy) — because too small a quality gap lets high-WTP buyers buy the cheap version. Degrading the low version protects the high-margin sale. Spotify's Individual/Duo/Family menu is the same logic — versioning by household size, with the cheaper tiers capped.

The “Stripped” Economy — Damaged Goods

The flip side of versioning: a firm will deliberately make the cheap version worse — spending nothing, or even money, to degrade it — so high-WTP buyers refuse to trade down and pay for the premium tier.

Then. 1840s railways ran roofless third-class cars. Dupuit (1849): they did it “not to hurt the poor, but to frighten the rich” into buying first class.

Now. Ever-tighter “stripped” economy seats (and Basic Economy) keep the cheap tier just painful enough to upsell.

The "damaged goods" strategy — the most counter-intuitive result in PD. A profit-maximizing firm spends real resources to make a product WORSE (rip the roof off, shrink the seat, disable a chip feature) because a too-small quality gap would let high-WTP buyers buy the cheap version. Dupuit's 1849 railway observation is the founding example and still the clearest: the degraded third class exists to protect the first-class fare. Modern version: airlines' Basic Economy and the recurring "double-decker / ultra-dense" seat proposals.

Self-Sorting Captures the Entire Demand Curve

Highly price-inelastic. Cinephiles & high-income households want 4K HDR — they pay $22.99+/mo and refuse to watch ads.
Highly price-elastic. Budget households & students — captured by the $6.99 ad-supported tier (~70M monthly active users by 2024).

To fund $17B+/yr of content like Stranger Things, Netflix uses the identical core product to monetize the bottom of the curve without sacrificing the margins at the top.

The payoff of versioning: one product, a menu of prices, and the tiers let buyers self-sort along the demand curve. The inelastic top (cinephiles) is monetized at $22.99; the elastic tail (students, budget households) is captured at $6.99 with ads — revenue Netflix would otherwise leave on the table. Ties straight back to elasticity (Ch 5): charge the inelastic segment more, the elastic segment less, without a single price cut for everyone.

Product Versioning — Tesla Model Y

One car, four software- and hardware-gated versions. Buyers self-select from $39,990 to $57,490 — same shell, very different price.

Classic versioning: Tesla differentiates the same Model Y platform into four tiers, several of them gated by software (range, acceleration, even cabin-power features). The performance buyer's extra $17,500 over the Standard is almost pure surplus capture. The wider the feature gap between tiers, the more cleanly high- and low-WTP buyers separate.

Two-Part Tariffs — the Gym

Part 1: Membership = $300 / year
Part 2: each class = MC = $10 / class

The annual fee captures consumer surplus up front; the per-class price = marginal cost, so members keep using the gym efficiently instead of being priced out.

Two-part tariff = a fixed entry fee + a per-unit price. Set the per-unit price at MC so the buyer consumes the efficient quantity, then use the membership fee to scoop up the consumer surplus that efficient consumption generates. The gym membership + per-class (or included-class) structure is the textbook case.

Two-Part Tariffs — the Buffet

Part 1: entry = $30 / person
Part 2: each plate = MC = $0 / plate

Once the trays are cooked, unsold food is thrown out anyway — so the marginal cost of one more plate ≈ $0. The buffet charges $0 per plate and captures all the surplus in the entry fee.

The buffet is the MC≈0 special case of the two-part tariff. Because an extra plate from already-prepared food costs the restaurant almost nothing, charging per plate would only deter eating and shrink the experience customers are paying for. So Part 2 = $0 and the entire margin lives in the door price.

Two-Part Tariffs — the Local Car Wash

Wages and rent are already paid to open the doors — so the marginal cost of one more wash ≈ $0. The unlimited monthly fee captures the surplus; the per-wash price ≈ $0.

Same MC≈0 logic as the buffet, but for a service: the staff and lease are fixed costs that are sunk the moment the location opens. One extra automated wash adds only a few cents of water and soap. The unlimited monthly plan is a two-part tariff with Part 2 ≈ $0 — and it also smooths revenue and raises switching costs. (Waves also versions the one-time wash: Basic/Extreme/Ultimate.)

Third-Degree — Different Price to an Observable Group

Spotify. $11.99 Individual vs $5.99 Student. Students have higher elasticity; a .edu check prevents arbitrage. Charge them full price and you lose them entirely.

Airlines. Business travelers (last-minute, inelastic) pay premium fares; leisure travelers (flexible, elastic) pay discounts. The ticket is non-transferable — arbitrage blocked.

The logic is always elasticity: charge the inelastic group more, the elastic group less. Segment by an observable trait — age, student status, location, timing — and stop resale.

Key contrast with second-degree: here the seller observes your group and assigns the price; there, you pick from a menu.

The 2nd-vs-3rd distinction is the single most-tested classification (Poll 2 next). Third = seller observes a verifiable group trait. Second = seller offers a menu, buyer self-selects. The elasticity logic (Ch 5/6) is the engine of both. Same molecule across countries (Ozempic) is also third-degree — tie back.

Third-Degree — the Student Discount

Definition. Pricing is differentiated for different consumer groups based on an observable characteristic (a proxy for their elasticity) — e.g. the Student Discount.

The cleanest third-degree case: Spotify can't read your WTP, but it CAN verify a .edu enrollment. Third-Degree — Student Platforms (UNiDAYS)

Platforms like UNiDAYS gate deals (HP, Microsoft 365) behind a verified student ID. The long-run twist: once students learn these tools, they keep using them at work — becoming more inelastic later, when they'll pay full price.

The strategic kicker is dynamic: discounting students today builds habit and lock-in, lowering their elasticity tomorrow — so the "loss" on student pricing is really an investment in future inelastic demand. Third-Degree — Movie Tickets (by Age)

Movie theaters charge different ticket prices for children, adults, and seniors — age is the observable trait, and the ticket is checked at the door (no resale).

Age-based pricing is the textbook third-degree example: kids and seniors have lower WTP / higher elasticity (more substitutes for their time), so a discount fills seats that would otherwise sit empty — at near-zero marginal cost — without cutting the adult price. The age check at the door is the arbitrage barrier.

Third-Degree — Airline Fares

Airlines charge varying fares to leisure (elastic) vs business (inelastic) travelers.

  • Prices rise as the departure date nears (late buyers = business).
  • Lower fares on non-peak days & with advance booking (leisure).
  • The ticket must be used by the purchaser — arbitrage blocked.
Airlines run the most sophisticated third-degree (and intertemporal) PD on earth: fences like advance-purchase requirements, Saturday-night stays, and non-refundable/non-transferable tickets sort business (inelastic, book late, won't stay the weekend) from leisure (elastic, flexible). Same seat, very different fare — and the named, non-transferable ticket stops resale.

Public Policy

Regulation of Natural Monopolies

Goal: low price + low cost

Regulating the Natural Monopolist

Natural monopolies have economies of scale so large that one firm serves the whole market at lower cost than several could. You can’t just “add competition” — so policy must pick how to regulate:

1

Public ownership — the government owns and operates the monopoly. (e.g., municipal water)

2

Regulated private ownership — a private firm operates under government price & quantity limits. (e.g., the electric utility)

3

Franchise the right to operate — award the market to the firm offering the most competitive price and output.

Whichever path, the regulator’s real lever is the price it lets the monopolist charge — so the next question is: which price?

A natural monopoly exists when economies of scale are so large that average cost keeps falling over the whole relevant range — so one firm is the cheapest way to serve the market (utilities: water, electricity grid, gas pipes). You can't just "add competition," so policy picks among public ownership, regulated private ownership, or franchise bidding. The next two slides are the two textbook pricing rules.

Regulating the Natural Monopolist

Average-Cost Pricing Rule

  • Require the monopolist to set price = average total cost (PR = ATC, at point b).
  • The firm earns a normal profit, but price is higher and output lower than under marginal-cost pricing.

Regulation in Practice

  • Often rate-of-return regulation (prices that ensure a normal return on investment),
  • or price caps PR (maximum price limits).
  • Estimating demand and cost curves accurately is challenging.
Average-cost pricing (P = ATC at b) is the workhorse of real regulation: it forces price down from the monopoly level (a) while still letting the firm break even (normal profit) — no subsidy required. The catch is informational: the regulator must estimate the firm's true costs, and rate-of-return rules can invite gold-plating (the Averch-Johnson effect). Price caps push the firm to cut costs but risk under-investment.

Regulating the Natural Monopolist

Marginal-Cost Pricing Rule: set price = marginal cost (PC = MC, at point c) — socially optimal, but if price < ATC the firm runs a loss, so a government subsidy may be needed.

Amtrak is the textbook case: it runs persistent operating losses and depends on federal subsidies — price-near-MC made real.

Marginal-cost pricing (P = MC at c) is efficient — the last unit's price equals its cost, no deadweight loss — but for a natural monopoly with falling ATC, MC lies below ATC, so the firm loses money on every unit (the shaded subsidy rectangle). Someone must cover it: a government subsidy (Amtrak, transit, rural broadband) or a two-part tariff (a fixed connection fee + per-unit price = MC, as utilities actually bill).

Antitrust & Measuring Market Power

The law targets the abuse of power — not the power itself

Three Statutes Behind US Antitrust

Sherman Act (1890). Makes “restraint of trade” and “monopolization” illegal felonies.

Clayton Act (1914). Targets actions — especially mergers — that may “substantially lessen competition.”

FTC Act (1914). Bans “unfair methods of competition” and “deceptive acts.”

The principle: having market power is legal; abusing anticompetitive tactics to get or keep it is not.

Three statutes, three targets: Sherman = monopolizing conduct; Clayton = mergers (preventive); FTC = unfair/deceptive practices. The legal/illegal line (built a better product = legal; exclusionary contracts = illegal) is the throughline. Next two slides give landmark cases for each.

Sherman Act — From Standard Oil to Google

Standard Oil, 1911

Bought rivals, controlled transport, predatory pricing → broken into 34 firms (today’s ExxonMobil, Chevron).

AT&T “Ma Bell,” 1982

Owned local lines, long distance, and equipment. Settlement split off 7 “Baby Bells.”

US v. Google, 2024–25

Paid Apple ~$20B/yr for default search. Ruled an illegal monopoly (§2).

Structural vs behavioral remedy. DOJ sought a structural fix (sell Chrome). In Sept 2025 the judge chose a behavioral one: no exclusive default deals, mandatory data sharing, 6-year oversight. Google may still pay Apple — just not exclusively.

The structural-vs-behavioral distinction is examinable (Review Q6). Structural = change the firm's assets (break-up/divestiture, permanent). Behavioral = regulate conduct (reversible, targeted). Google's $20B default deal is the modern barrier-to-entry: it starved rivals of the search data needed to compete — a self-reinforcing loop.

How Standard Oil Built Its Cost Advantage

Its real weapon wasn’t only buying rivals — Standard Oil relentlessly drove its average total cost (ATC) to the lowest in the industry, then undercut everyone.

Standardize the oil container → the 42-gallon barrel
↓
Uniform & stackable → far lower handling & trading cost
↓
Volume + vertical integration → the lowest ATC possible
↓
Undercut & absorb rivals → ~90% of US refining

It paired that cost edge with hardball tactics — secret railroad rebates, controlling the pipelines & transport, and buying out competitors. The cost advantage was lawful; the tactics are what drew the antitrust suit.

The economics lesson: Standard Oil is partly a story of genuine efficiency. The 42-gallon barrel (still the unit oil trades in today) standardized handling, and vast scale plus vertical integration pushed ATC below every rival's — a real cost advantage that benefits consumers. The problem was the conduct used to entrench it (rebates, transport control, predatory buyouts), which is what §1/§2 of the Sherman Act target. Separating "good" scale economies from "bad" exclusionary conduct is the core difficulty of antitrust.

Standard Oil v. United States (1911)

The Sherman Act makes “restraint of trade” and “monopolization” illegal felonies.

The case. The government sued Standard Oil, arguing it had become an illegal monopoly — using anti-competitive tactics (buying out rivals, controlling transport) to crush all competition.

The outcome. The Supreme Court agreed this was a restraint of trade and ordered Standard Oil broken into 34 independent companies — which eventually became firms like Exxon, Mobil, and Chevron.

The first landmark §2 case and the template for structural remedies. Note the "rule of reason" born here: the Court said only UNreasonable restraints are illegal — size alone isn't — which still governs antitrust today. The 34 spin-offs seeded much of the modern oil industry; the diagram traces them to today's ExxonMobil, Chevron, BP, etc. Contrast with the behavioral remedy chosen in US v. Google (prior slide).

Sherman Act — United States v. AT&T (1982)

The problem (monopolization). For decades AT&T (“Ma Bell”) was a legal monopoly — it owned nearly all local lines, the entire long-distance network, and the equipment maker (Western Electric).

The restraint of trade. It used that local-line monopoly to kill competition elsewhere: it refused to let rivals like MCI connect, and forbade customers from attaching non-AT&T devices.

The outcome (the breakup). The 1982 settlement forced AT&T to divest its 22 local companies → 7 regional “Baby Bells” (Bell Atlantic, BellSouth, NYNEX…); the “new” AT&T was left a long-distance company forced to compete fairly.

The classic structural remedy. AT&T's local-loop monopoly was the bottleneck it leveraged into long distance and equipment; the divestiture split the natural-monopoly piece (local wires) from the competitive pieces. Irony for students: the Baby Bells largely re-merged over 30 years into today's AT&T and Verizon — the diagram traces that consolidation.

Sherman Act — United States v. Google (2024)

  • The allegation (monopolization). The DOJ argued Google illegally maintained its ~90% search market share.
  • The restraint of trade. Google paid billions/yr to Apple, Samsung & Mozilla to be the default search engine — locking out rivals like Bing & DuckDuckGo, who couldn't afford to compete.
  • The outcome (Aug 2024). Judge Amit Mehta ruled Google violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act as an illegal monopolist.
  • The remedy (Sept 2025). DOJ wanted a structural fix (sell Chrome); the judge called that “disproportionate” and imposed a behavioral one — no exclusive default deals, must share some search data, but Google may still pay Apple, just not exclusively.
The modern §2 case and the structural-vs-behavioral debate in one slide. The barrier to entry is data scale: default placement feeds Google the query data that makes its algorithm better, which wins more defaults — a self-reinforcing loop rivals can't break. The behavioral remedy (keep Chrome, ban exclusivity) is far milder than the AT&T/Standard Oil break-ups; whether it restores competition is the open question.

Clayton & FTC — Mergers and Dark Patterns

Clayton — mergers

AT&T / T-Mobile, 2011. The DOJ sued to block the $39B merger — it would cut four major carriers to three. AT&T abandoned the deal.

FTC — unfair / deceptive

FTC v. Amazon, 2023–25. “Dark patterns” trapped users in auto-renewing Prime; algorithm “Project Nessie” tested whether rivals would follow price hikes. Record $2.5B settlement, 35M consumers.

Clayton is preventive (stop the merger before it concentrates the market); the FTC polices conduct (how the firm treats consumers and rivals).

Project Nessie is a striking case — an algorithm engineering a higher market equilibrium by predicting competitor responses. The $2.5B is the largest FTC consumer-protection penalty ever. Clayton's preventive logic (block before harm) is what makes HHI merger review — the next slides — matter.

The Clayton Antitrust Act (1914)

What it does. Makes actions illegal if their effect is to “substantially lessen competition” — it is preventive (stops harm before it happens).

AT&T's attempted merger with T-Mobile (2011)

  • The case. The DOJ sued to block the $39B merger of the 2nd- and 4th-largest US mobile carriers.
  • The reasoning. It would “substantially lessen competition” in wireless → higher prices, fewer choices.
  • The outcome. The challenge was so strong that AT&T abandoned the deal — a merger stopped before it could harm competition.
Clayton §7 is the merger statute: it acts before concentration occurs, unlike Sherman (which punishes existing conduct). The 4→3 reduction in national carriers was the crux — fewer rivals, easier tacit coordination, higher prices. AT&T walked rather than litigate, paying T-Mobile a ~$4B break-up fee that ironically helped T-Mobile become a stronger competitor.

The Federal Trade Commission Act (1914)

What it does. Makes “unfair methods of competition” and “deceptive acts” illegal — the FTC's consumer-protection role.

FTC v. Amazon (2023)

  • The case. The FTC sued Amazon over its Prime subscription practices.
  • The accusation. Amazon used manipulative “dark patterns” to trick millions into auto-renewing Prime, then made cancellation overly complicated.
  • A textbook case of the FTC stopping deceptive acts — a record $2.5B settlement.
The FTC Act is the conduct/consumer-protection complement to Sherman & Clayton. Dark patterns — confusing cancellation flows ("Iliad" was Amazon's internal name for the deliberately epic cancel process) — are "deceptive acts." The $2.5B settlement (2025) is the largest FTC consumer-protection penalty, and signals regulators treating UX manipulation as an antitrust-adjacent harm.

Economist at Work — The Antitrust Economists

Behind every landmark case is an economist building the analytical framework. Fiona Scott Morton (Yale SOM; former DOJ Deputy Assistant AG for Economics) shaped the reasoning in multiple Google and platform investigations.

Her 2025 work compared US and EU approaches and concluded the US trailed Europe by ~a decade on platform dominance — but was reaching the same conclusions through the courts.

These are the people who decide whether a merger creates efficiency or destroys competition.

Humanize the field — antitrust economist is one of the most consequential public-policy roles, and a live career path for these students. The US-vs-EU gap motivates why the DMA (EU) and recent US cases are converging. Good moment to note the quantitative tools (HHI) come next.

Measuring Concentration — CR4 vs HHI

CR4 = sum of the top four firms’ shares. Easy — but blind to how lopsided they are.

HHI = sum of squared shares (0 → 10,000). Squaring makes a dominant firm jump out. The DOJ uses it in every merger review.

Same CR4 = 85%, very different worlds: one 65% giant (HHI 4,600) vs four near-equals (HHI 2,050).

The whole point: CR4 can't tell “one giant + three minnows” from “four equals.” HHI can, because 65² = 4,225 dominates the sum. This is why the DOJ standardized on HHI. Next slide: the thresholds and a live merger calculation.

Contestable Markets — When the Threat Is Enough

Baumol, 1982

Even a single firm prices competitively if entry is credible. With low sunk costs, a “hit-and-run” entrant disciplines the incumbent. A small-town gas station may price like a competitor.

When it breaks down

Contestability assumes low sunk costs. A frontier AI run (~$1B), hyperscale data centers, gigawatts of power — none recoverable if you fail. The desert highway’s lone station can charge monopoly prices.

The real question isn’t how concentrated a market is — it’s whether a credible challenger could actually enter. When the moat costs billions to cross, pure contestability is an illusion.

Contestable-markets theory is the counterweight to “concentration = market power.” Microsoft used it in 1998 (Schmalensee) — and it proved prophetic (Chrome displaced IE). But the 2020s digital economy is precisely the high-sunk-cost case where it fails. The number of sellers is an OUTCOME, not the cause — what matters is the cost of entry.

Monopoly or Contestable Market? — Microsoft, 1998

In 1998 the DOJ called Microsoft an illegal monopoly — bundling Internet Explorer with Windows to crush Netscape. Microsoft’s defense: the market is contestable — new technology could displace us at any time. Who was right?

The Microsoft case is the most famous contestability argument. The DOJ saw a 90%+ OS monopoly leveraging into browsers; Microsoft (via economist Richard Schmalensee) argued the software market was so dynamic that today's dominance is fragile — entry by unknown future tech keeps it disciplined. The next slide shows who turned out to be right.

Contestable in Action — the Browser Wars

“Of far greater concern to Microsoft is the competition from new and emerging technologies, some of which are currently visible and others of which certainly are not… This array of known, emerging, and wholly unknown competitors places enormous pressure on Microsoft to price competitively and innovate aggressively.”
— Schmalensee, 1999 (Microsoft’s economist)

He was right: IE’s near-monopoly was displaced by Chrome. The threat of entry — even from a company not yet visible — was real.

The punchline: Schmalensee's contestability claim proved prophetic in this market. Chrome (launched 2008) crushed IE within a few years — exactly the "wholly unknown competitor" he described. Low sunk costs in software (no factories, instant distribution) make browsers genuinely contestable. Contrast with the high-sunk-cost digital platforms where the same argument fails.

Contestable Today? — Cloud Computing

Just like frontier AI today, the cloud once had massive sunk costs (data centers, fiber). But as the industry matured, Microsoft and Google entered — Amazon’s lead narrowed (53.7% → 47.8%). High sunk costs can fall over time, turning a near-monopoly into a contestable market.

The forward-looking case. AWS looked unassailable (first mover, vast data centers). But cloud sunk costs, while huge, are increasingly shared/standardized, and deep-pocketed rivals (Azure, Google Cloud) could enter — so Amazon's share is eroding and it must price/innovate to defend it. The lesson for AI: today's ~$1B training runs look like an impassable moat, but if those costs fall (better chips, efficiency), AI could follow the cloud's path from sunk-cost fortress to contestable market.

Appendix — The Lerner Index of Market Power

Lerner Index: L = (P − MC) / P. The share of price that is pure markup. 0 = price-taker, 1 = total power.

The identity L = 1 / |elasticity| ties it all together: inelastic demand → bigger markup. That’s exactly why price discrimination works — charge the inelastic group a higher markup.

Ozempic: 0.995 in the patent-protected US vs 0.643 against Indian generics. The patent is the gap.

The Lerner Index quantifies the chapter's one idea: market power = pricing above MC. It closes the loop on the Ozempic table (§11.1) and connects power directly to elasticity (Ch 6) and to PD (§11.4). Abba Lerner, 1934. Caveat for Google Ads: marginal cost must include the infrastructure, not just per-impression cost — a naive L overstates.

Key Takeaways

1. Barriers make the monopoly. Key inputs, switching costs/network effects, branding, scale, or government grants — physical moats became digital ones.

2. MR < P, so produce where MR = MC. Read price up on demand. The monopolist restricts output and charges above MC.

3. Monopoly costs society. Deadweight loss + rent-seeking + X-inefficiency — possibly offset by funded innovation.

4. Price discrimination captures surplus — first (personal), second (menu/self-select), third (observable group). AI is closing in on first.

5. Antitrust polices abuse, not power. Sherman / Clayton / FTC; CR4 & HHI measure concentration; contestability can discipline — until sunk costs get too big.
