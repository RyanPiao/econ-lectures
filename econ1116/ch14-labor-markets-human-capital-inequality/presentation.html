Where the Labor Market Lives

Product market (top): firms sell, households buy goods & services.

Factor (labor) market (bottom): the roles flip — households supply labor, firms buy it.

This whole chapter zooms into the bottom loop. The wage is its price.

Orient students: the labor market is just the bottom half of the circular flow. The key role-reversal — in everyday life you BUY from firms; in the labor market firms BUY from you. The price in this market is the wage, set by supply (households) and demand (firms), exactly like any other market in Ch 4.

The Work–Leisure Trade-Off

Time is a finite resource. Each hour you must “trade” between two uses:

Work — time sold as labor, which generates income (money).

Leisure — resting, hobbies, watching YouTube — which generates utility.

The opportunity cost of one hour of leisure is the wage you gave up by not working it.

Higher wage → leisure is more “expensive” → people supply more hours → an upward-sloping labor supply curve.

The 615.6M hours on "Baby Shark" was a collective choice to value two minutes of leisure over the money/output that time could have produced. Formally: the wage is the price of leisure, so a higher wage raises the opportunity cost of leisure and (substitution effect) pulls more hours into work — the upward-sloping supply of labor. (At very high wages the income effect can bend it backward; flag but don't derail.)

The Substitution Effect

Higher wage: Ford’s famous 1914 raise — $2.34/day → $5/day.

Work becomes more attractive — each hour now yields more money, so the opportunity cost of leisure rises.

Substitution effect: when wages rise, the opportunity cost of leisure increases, making leisure relatively more expensive — so people substitute toward work.

The Income Effect

Higher wage: Ford’s 1914 raise again — $2.34/day → $5/day.

A higher wage lets Timmy afford more of everything — and leisure is one of those things.

Income effect: when wages rise, Timmy earns more for the same hours — so he can “buy back” leisure and work fewer hours.

The Individual Labor Supply Curve

It shows the hours a person will work at each wage rate — and the two effects pull against each other.

Low wages → substitution wins: wage ↑, hours ↑ (curve slopes up).

High wages → income wins: wage ↑, hours ↓ (curve bends back).

The famous backward-bending labor supply curve. Below the turning point (W3=$60 at 80 hrs) the substitution effect dominates and the curve slopes up. Above it the income effect dominates and the curve bends back — higher wages, fewer hours. Lottery winners and high earners cutting hours are the intuition.

The Market Labor Supply Curve

The total labor supplied to an occupation or industry at each wage.

Upward-sloping — even though individual curves bend back. Higher wages attract more applicants from other jobs.

Reservation wage (WR): the lowest wage at which someone will take the job — here $15/hr. Below it, no one supplies labor.

Adding up individuals gives the market supply, which IS upward-sloping because higher wages draw workers in from other occupations even if some incumbents cut hours. The reservation wage is the floor — the lowest wage that pulls the first worker in. It varies by person and rises with outside options (e.g., after graduation).

The Demand for Labor

From product markets to labor markets — firms as buyers

Why Ford Needed More Workers

As the Model T’s real price fell ($850 in 1908 → ~$260 by the 1920s), demand exploded. To build a million cars a year at low cost, Ford needed a giant plant and an army of workers.

Highland Park: 90 acres under roof, capacity 1,000,000 cars/yr — up to 25,000 workers (~1912); 14,000 in 1914, most foreign-born.

The hook for labor demand: Ford's falling Model T price drove huge product demand, which drove demand for workers. To minimize cost (min ATC) at that scale, Ford built Highland Park and hired tens of thousands. This is derived demand in action — more cars sold means more workers needed.

Timmy on the Line

Each worker Ford adds produces more cars — that’s the productivity side.

But Ford doesn’t hire Timmy out of charity — it hires him because customers want cars.

So labor demand is a derived demand — derived from the demand for the product.

Timmy reappears on the demand side. Two ideas seeded here: (1) each worker adds output (productivity → MPP), (2) the firm only wants that output because there's product demand (derived demand). Both combine into MRP over the next slides.

The Demand for Labor

Derived demand: a firm’s demand for labor comes from two things —

the demand for its product and the productivity of labor.

No demand for the product → no demand for the labor that makes it.

Put them together and you get a worker’s value to the firm: productivity × product price. That’s the next two steps.

Merged derived-demand slide. The firm's labor demand = product demand × productivity. Starbucks hires baristas because people buy lattes; Ford hires Timmy because people buy cars. The two factors (product price, productivity) multiply into Marginal Revenue Product — the firm's actual labor demand curve.

Competitive Labor Demand

In a competitive product market the firm is a price-taker, so MR = P.

Then MRPL is the Value of the Marginal Product: VMPL = MPPL × P.

Hire one more worker while VMPL ≥ wage.

Diminishing returns: early workers may add more, but eventually each adds less — VMPL falls.

For a price-taker, MR = P, so MRP_L = MPP_L × P = the Value of the Marginal Product (VMP_L). Same numbers as the engine example (P=$25): VMP rises to $12.50 then falls. Hire while VMP_L ≥ wage; on the downward portion that pins down how many to hire (30 workers at a $5 wage).

The Firm’s Demand Curve for Labor

The downward-sloping part of VMPL is the firm’s labor demand curve. Hire to MRPL = W.

If the wage falls, firms demand more labor — the MRP of extra workers now exceeds the lower wage.

What Shifts Labor Demand? Productivity

One John Deere harvester can replace ~10 lumberjacks.

Short run: machines do the work → labor demand ↓ (e1 → e2).

Long run: cheaper lumber → more output & new jobs → labor demand ↑ (e1 → e3).

Where did the 10 go? Not gone — they move to running & servicing the harvesters, sawmills, log trucking, and the homebuilding boom cheaper lumber sets off.

~16,000 board feet of lumber frames one average home. Cheaper lumber → more houses built → more carpenters, framers & electricians than the forest ever lost.

Second shifter: productivity. A productivity jump (automation) cuts the labor needed per unit in the SHORT run (D shifts left, fewer workers — the John Deere harvester replacing lumberjacks, e1→e2). But the displaced workers don't vanish: they move into running and servicing the machines, sawmills, log trucking, and especially the homebuilding that cheaper lumber unleashes. With ~16,000 board feet of lumber framing an average home, lower lumber prices mean more houses built and more construction trades hired. In the LONG run, lower costs → lower prices → more output and new complementary jobs, so labor demand rises overall (D shifts right past the original, e1→e3). The LR arrow runs parallel to the labor-supply curve. The net effect is the central AI-and-jobs debate (the next slide repeats this exact pattern for computers; Section: AI & the Future of Work).

Same Story, New Tech: Computers & “Excel Skills”

Spreadsheets like Excel were once feared as the ultimate office “job killer.”

Short run: software does the routine number-crunching → demand for bookkeeping & data-entry labor ↓ (e1 → e2).

Long run: cheap, powerful computing → whole new industries → labor demand ↑ (e1 → e3). “Excel skills” went from exotic → expected on almost every job posting.

And it created high-pay jobs that couldn’t exist before computers: software developers (~1.9M in the US, median ~$130k, BLS), data analysts, financial modelers.

The productivity story isn't only about robots in forests — it has already played out in offices. When PCs and spreadsheets (VisiCalc, Lotus 1-2-3, Excel) arrived, people feared mass white-collar unemployment. In the short run, routine clerical work did shrink — bookkeeping, data-entry, and manual ledger jobs declined (e1→e2). But computing was so cheap and powerful that it expanded output and spawned entirely new occupations (e1→e3): "Excel skills" became a baseline expectation on most job postings, and brand-new high-paying roles appeared that literally could not exist before computers — software developers (~1.9 million US jobs, median ~$130k, BLS), data analysts, financial modelers, IT support. This is the optimistic historical analogy people invoke in the AI debate — with the caveat that the transition is painful for displaced workers and the long-run jobs require new skills. Will AI & Robots Destroy Jobs? It Depends.

A breakthrough changes labor demand depending on whether capital (AI, robots) is a substitute for or a complement to the worker — and on the time horizon and the nature of the work.

Short run — substitute. For repetitive tasks an AI or humanoid can already do, capital replaces labor → labor demand ↓.

Long run — complement. New products, new markets, and deeper, more complex work emerge → labor demand ↑, with new kinds of jobs.

The central AI-and-jobs question. Capital can be a substitute (replaces the worker), a complement (raises the worker's value), or roughly 1:1 — and which one dominates depends on the task and the horizon. Short run: routine, codifiable tasks get automated, so labor demand for those falls. Long run: cheaper output and entirely new product spaces raise total labor demand. The car and the recipe slides make this concrete.

History Rhymes: The Automobile

1. Substitute. The car replaced the horse coach — coach drivers feared losing their jobs.

2. Demand explodes. Cheaper transport → new industries to build, sell, maintain, fuel, race cars.

3. Spillovers. Cars reshaped agriculture, food & restaurants — millions of new jobs.

4. Complements. How many cars now? Labor & capital became complements.

Jevons paradox: efficiency often means we use more, not less. Replace “car” with “AI” — same picture.

The automobile is the cleanest historical analogy for AI. A more efficient substitute (car for horse) terrified incumbents, but the efficiency gain exploded demand, spawned vast new industries, and ultimately created far more jobs than it destroyed — Jevons paradox. Swap "car" for "AI" and the same logic applies: short-run displacement, long-run complementarity and growth.

Same Output, Different Recipes: Labor vs. AI

The same work can be produced with very different mixes of labor and AI/capital. Firms pick the recipe that’s cheapest given the wage vs. the cost of AI.

Labor-intensive — AI << Labor

Mostly people. Cheap labor or work that’s hard to automate keeps humans on the task.

Balanced — AI ≈ Labor

People augmented by AI tools — each worker does far more, AI handles the routine.

AI-intensive — AI >> Labor

Mostly automated. When AI is cheap and the task is codifiable, capital does the bulk of the work.

Mirrors the Ch 3 Ford-recipes idea: the same output, three input mixes. As AI gets cheaper relative to wages, firms slide from the labor-intensive recipe toward the AI-intensive one — exactly the substitution we draw as a movement along (and shift of) labor demand. The 'balanced' recipe (humans + AI tools) is where most knowledge work is heading.

Progress Is a Long Road — Problems to Tackle

The long-run story is optimistic — but the road from a breakthrough to broadly shared gains is long and bumpy. Real problems have to be solved along the way:

Displacement & timing. Workers in automated tasks lose jobs now — the transition to new ones can take years, or a whole career.

Re-skilling. New jobs need new skills — training and education have to keep up with the technology.

Inequality & power. Who captures the productivity surplus — and who owns and controls the AI and the capital?

Trust & safety. Bias, errors, and the accountability of the new technology.

Balance the optimism: long-run job creation is real, but it doesn't make the transition painless. The genuine hard problems — displacement timing, re-skilling, inequality, safety, and the concentration of who owns the technology — are exactly the policy questions the rest of the course (wages, inequality, the AI section) takes up.

Compensating Differentials

When Do People Invest in Human Capital?

Education is an investment, so the choice turns on its opportunity cost. Three things shift that cost — and the decision:

1. Time horizon (age). Younger workers have more years to earn the return — so they’re more likely to invest.

2. The earnings gap. The bigger the college–high-school wage premium, the bigger the payoff → more people attend.

3. Discount rate. The more you value the present over the future, the less a delayed-payoff investment is worth → less schooling.

Human-capital theory treats schooling as an investment governed by opportunity cost. Three comparative statics: (1) age — younger people have a longer horizon to collect the return and lower forgone earnings, so they invest more; (2) the size of the college wage premium — a bigger gap raises the benefit, drawing more people in; (3) the discount rate — people who heavily discount the future (present-biased) value the delayed payoff less and invest less. Becker’s Theory: How a Wage Gap Appears

Taste-based discrimination (Becker, 1957): some employers simply prefer one group, so equally-skilled workers get split into two markets.

Non-preferred group: employers demand them less → their wage falls to WF.

Preferred group: employers demand them more → their wage rises to WM.

Same skills, same productivity — yet a wage gap (WM − WF) opens up, and groups get segregated into different occupations.

Becker's taste-based model in plain terms. Employers with a "taste for discrimination" act as if non-preferred workers cost more, so they demand fewer of them — pushing the non-preferred group's wage down and (by crowding them out of preferred jobs) the preferred group's wage up. Two otherwise identical labor markets end up with different wages. The vertical supply just fixes the number of workers; the action is the demand shift. Result: a wage gap and occupational segregation, even with equal productivity. Next: why Becker thought competition would erode this.

Becker’s Surprising Prediction

Discrimination is costly for the discriminator — so competition should erode it.

Discriminating firm: insists on “preferred” workers → pays a higher wage bill → higher costs, lower profit.

Non-discriminating firm: hires the cheaper, equally-skilled group → lower costs, higher profit.

Becker’s conclusion: in the long run, market competition should drive discrimination toward zero — non-discriminators out-compete discriminators.

But it persists — limits to the theory:

high adjustment costs of hiring/firing · customer or coworker prejudice · statistical discrimination (group averages as a proxy) · segregated networks & occupations.

The optimistic prediction: a discriminating employer voluntarily pays more (only hiring the preferred group), so it has higher costs than a rival that hires the cheaper, equally-productive non-preferred workers. In competitive markets the non-discriminator earns higher profit and expands, so discrimination should be competed away over time. Reality: it persists, because of adjustment/turnover costs, customer and coworker prejudice (not just the employer's), statistical discrimination (using group averages when individual quality is hard to observe), and entrenched networks. Monopsony: The Breakdown of the Free Market

The free market assumes competition. But when the employer holds all the cards — no competitor across the street — supply and demand break down.

Definition: Monopsony

The exact inverse of a monopoly — a market with a single buyer for your labor, giving the employer the power to depress wages below a worker’s MRP.

Monopsony = one buyer (mirror of monopoly = one seller). A monopsonist faces the whole upward-sloping labor supply curve, so hiring one more worker raises the wage for everyone — the marginal cost of labor exceeds the wage. The profit-maximizing monopsonist hires fewer workers and pays a wage BELOW marginal revenue product. With few outside options, workers can't bid the wage back up. This is a real-world reason wages can sit below MRP — a market failure, not the competitive ideal.

The Evolution of Monopsony Power

The Monopsony Spectrum

Absolute / Historical (Early 1900s)

Appalachian company towns — the employer owned the jobs, the housing, and the grocery stores. Total entrapment.

Geographic / Modern

Hospital systems dominating a 50-mile radius — geographically trapping specialized nurses who can’t uproot their families.

Monopsony isn't just a history-book idea. The old form was absolute: the company town, where one firm owned your job, your house, and the store you bought food from — you literally couldn't leave. The modern form is geographic and occupational: a dominant hospital system in a rural region, where a specialized nurse has essentially one employer within commuting distance and can't relocate the whole family. Same economics — limited outside options give the employer wage-setting power.

Monopsony in Practice: Amazon

From ~17K workers (2007) to ~1.5 million (2023), Amazon is the dominant buyer in many local labor markets. Warehouse pay in high-earning Amazon counties runs ~18% lower (≈\$822/month) than in comparable non-Amazon counties — a gap that didn’t exist before Amazon arrived. (NELP, 2023)

Amazon is the clearest modern example of labor-market concentration. As it scaled to ~1.5 million employees, in many metro and rural areas it became the dominant — sometimes the only large — warehouse employer. Recent research (cited by Timothy Noah, 2023) finds that warehouse wages in a local market tend to fall after Amazon enters, consistent with monopsony power: fewer competing buyers of warehouse labor means lower wages, even with no change in workers' productivity.

Non-compete clauses have exploded — criminalizing the ability to seek better wages, trapping warehouse packers, security guards, and fast-food workers.

Non-compete clauses — once reserved for executives with trade secrets — now bind roughly 18% of US workers, including fast-food and warehouse staff, legally barring them from taking a better-paying job in the same field. That artificially removes outside options, the very thing that gives workers bargaining power. The FTC estimated banning them would raise worker earnings by about \$300 billion a year — a direct measure of the wage leverage these clauses strip away.

The Lorenz curve is the standard picture of inequality. Order households poorest-to-richest and plot the cumulative share of income they hold. Perfect equality is the 45-degree line (any x% of households hold x% of income). Real distributions bow below it; the bigger the gap between the line and the curve, the more unequal. The two-person economy makes it concrete: 50/50 sits on the line (a); a 25/75 split bends down to b; the worst case runs along the bottom then up the right edge.

Measuring Inequality: The Gini Coefficient

Gini = A / (A + B)

A = area between the Lorenz curve & the line of equality. B = area below the Lorenz curve.

Gini = 0 — perfect equality (A = 0; curve = the line).

Gini = 1 — maximum inequality (B = 0).

Lower = more equal, higher = more unequal. U.S.: 0.394 (1970) → ~0.485 (2023).

The Gini coefficient turns the Lorenz picture into a single number: the ratio of the gap area (A, between the equality line and the curve) to the whole triangle (A+B). Gini 0 = the curve IS the equality line (everyone equal); Gini 1 = one household has everything. It lets us compare inequality across time and countries. The US income Gini rose from ~0.39 in 1970 to ~0.485 in 2023.

Misunderstanding the Model: Klarna (2024)

Klarna thought it had cracked the labor cheat code — but it miscategorized non-routine work as routine.

The Action

Paused hiring, cut staff 5,500 → 3,400, and replaced 700 service agents with an AI chatbot to maximize efficiency.

The Implosion

AI trapped users in logic loops; loyalty plummeted, wait times soared — Klarna was forced to rehire humans.

You can’t automate humanity. AI excels at routine (“what’s my balance?”, “reset my password”) but fails at non-routine — de-escalating a terrified customer whose identity was stolen and savings locked.

A real cautionary tale (2024). Klarna's leadership treated all customer service as routine-cognitive and swapped 700 agents for an AI chatbot, cutting headcount from 5,500 to 3,400. It backfired: complex, emotional, non-standard cases (the non-routine core of support) tanked service quality, and Klarna had to rehire. The lesson maps to the matrix: simple data-retrieval queries are routine (AI wins), but crisis de-escalation needs empathy, nuance, and judgment to override protocol (AI fails). The Historical Panic Cycle

Apocalyptic fears of mass unemployment accompany every paradigm shift — and never materialize. Technology destroys old tasks, lowers prices, and spawns new industries (e.g., the AI prompt engineer).

Fear of permanent unemployment → creation of new industries.

The real challenge isn’t a lack of total jobs — it’s the agonizing pain of the individual transition (the people who lose jobs aren’t always the ones who get the new ones).

The long view debunks "AI = permanent mass unemployment." Every major technology — the spinning jenny, the PC, now AI — triggered the same fear, and every time net employment recovered and entirely new job categories appeared (in 2000 there were no social-media managers, data scientists, or app developers; tomorrow's example is the AI prompt engineer). Tech destroys specific tasks but lowers costs and creates demand elsewhere. The genuine policy problem is distributional and temporal: the displaced worker and the new-job holder are often different people, so the transition — retraining, relocation, time — is where the real pain lives.

The Ultimate Compensating Differential

If AI makes routine logic practically infinite and free, the relative value of scarce, genuine human connection must skyrocket.

Abundant & Cheap

Logic, math, coding, massive data sorting — value falls.

Scarce & Premium

Deep empathy, localized trust, physical care — value rises.

Could the future wage gap between a \$95k junior software developer and a \$48k dedicated teacher fundamentally flip?

A provocative closer that ties AI back to compensating differentials and scarcity. Basic economics: when something becomes abundant and cheap (routine logic, code, data crunching — AI's strengths), its market price falls; what stays scarce commands a premium. The scarce thing in an AI world is genuine human connection — empathy, trust, hands-on care — which AI can't replicate. So the relative wage of "human" work (teaching, caregiving, skilled trades) could rise versus codifiable knowledge work. The closing question (could a teacher out-earn a junior dev?) is a discussion hook, not a prediction.

Labor demand = MRP curve. Hire until MRP = wage.
