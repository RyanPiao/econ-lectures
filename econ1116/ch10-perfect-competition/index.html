Hook — Fifty Pizza Shops, One Price

The puzzle: Within 3 miles of Huntington Ave, ~50 shops sell a cheese slice for $3.50–$4.50. Nobody charges $12. Nobody charges $1.

Ask Alex why she charges $3.95 and she laughs: “Because that’s what everyone charges. Higher, customers walk two blocks. Lower, I bleed money.”

Nobody is in charge — yet:

  • Pizzas get made at roughly the right price
  • In roughly the right quantity, every single night
  • No planner decided how many Boston needs at 11 PM — the number emerged

Section 1 · LO 10.1

Market Structure Analysis

One map for the entire back half of the course

The Five Industry Characteristics

Commit these to memory — you will apply them four times across Part III.

1. Number of firms. A few giants? Hundreds of small shops? One sole provider?

2. Nature of the product. Homogeneous (perfect substitutes) or differentiated (each its own thing)?

3. Barriers to entry. Enter tomorrow with modest capital — or need patents, billions, licenses, network effects?

4. Control over price. Accept whatever the market sets (price-taker) or post your own (price-maker)?

5. Long-run economic profit. Does free entry compete profit to zero, or do barriers sustain it?

These five characteristics are the analytical engine of Part III. Number 4 (price control) is really a consequence of 1–3, but it’s the behavior we observe. Number 5 is the long-run prediction. Everything downstream is an application of this list.

The Corn Market — A Perfect-Competition Case

Score the five characteristics on US field corn (No. 2 Yellow) — every one lines up.

1. Many firms — ~300,000 US corn farms; one grower planting more never nudges the price. ✓ very many

2. Homogeneous product — “No. 2 Yellow Corn” is a federal grade; every farm’s bushel is a perfect substitute. ✓ identical

3. Free entry & exit — no patents or licenses; rent land, buy seed, plant. ✓ low barriers

4. Price-takers — farmers accept the Chicago Board of Trade price (~$4.70/bu, 2026); they choose how much to plant, never the price. ✓ no control

5. Zero long-run profit — good years lure entry & expansion → supply rises → price falls back to min ATC. ✓ competed away

Verdict → Perfect Competition — the textbook 8–9 / 10 case

The concrete payoff of the previous slide: run the abstract five-characteristic checklist on a real market and watch it score a near-perfect 10. Corn is the canonical example (echoes §10.6). Key teaching beats: (1) ~300k farms, so each is a tiny share — condition 1; (2) federal grading (No. 2 Yellow) is WHAT makes the product homogeneous — condition 2; (3) no legal barrier to planting — condition 3; (4) the farmer reads the CBOT/elevator price off a screen, can’t set it — condition 4; (5) free entry competes profit to zero over years — condition 5. Then contrast with the next slide’s table where the other three structures fail one or more of these. Video: 100s corn-field loop, muted, auto-loops.

The Coffee-Shop Market — Monopolistic Competition

Same five characteristics — but two answers flip, and that flip is the whole difference.

1. Many firms — a dozen cafés within a few blocks of campus; no single shop dominates. ✓ many

2. Differentiated product — each shop has its own roast, menu, baristas, and vibe; students have favorites. Δ not homogeneous

3. Free entry & exit — lease a space, buy an espresso machine, open. ✓ low barriers

4. Some price control — differentiation tilts demand: raise the latte $0.25 and you lose some — not all — customers. Δ price-setter

5. Zero long-run profit — entry still competes profit away (now with excess capacity). ✓ competed away

Verdict → Monopolistic Competition — many sellers + differentiation → a sliver of pricing power (Ch 12)

Deliberate contrast with the corn slide: run the SAME five-characteristic checklist on coffee shops and only two answers change — product (now differentiated) and price control (now a sliver). Those two flips are the entire definition of monopolistic competition. Teaching beats: (1) still many sellers; (2) differentiation is the headline break — roast/menu/vibe/loyalty; (3) entry still easy; (4) differentiation → slightly downward-sloping firm demand → tiny price-setting power (contrast corn’s flat demand); (5) free entry STILL drives long-run profit to zero, but at excess capacity (the Ch 12 result). Amber Δ marks the two conditions that differ from perfect competition; green ✓ marks the three that match. Verdict badge is blue to match the Monopolistic-Competition column in the next slide’s table. Video: mc.mp4 espresso-extraction loop, muted.

A Spectrum of Market Power

“Which one am I in?” is rarely black-and-white. Real markets drift between structures as technology and regulation evolve.

What matters is learning to read the characteristics. Say “many sellers, differentiated products, low barriers” and you’ve identified monopolistic competition — and you know the predictions that follow.

The skill is reading the five characteristics, not memorizing which firm goes in which box. Once you can score a market on the five, the structure—and its predictions—follow. Mention CR4 and HHI as the quantitative tools (covered formally in Ch 11/13); qualitative is enough for now.

Section 2 · LO 10.2

What Makes a Market
Perfectly Competitive?

The frictionless plane of economics

Perfect Competition — The Benchmark

Perfect competition: a market so crowded, so commoditized, and so frictionless that no single buyer or seller can move the price.

1. Many buyers & many sellers — no one is big enough to matter

2. Homogeneous products — buyers don’t care whose they buy

3. Free entry & exit — no patents, licenses, or huge sunk costs

4. Price-takers — firms choose quantity, not price

Like a frictionless plane: an idealization — but a clean yardstick for every other market.

Stress: it’s an idealization, NOT a claim about reality. Condition 4 is really a consequence of 1–3. Real markets rarely tick all four boxes — but a surprising number come close (wheat, T-bills, the fish pier). The benchmark’s value is comparison.

Conditions 1 & 2 — Many Sellers, One Product

Many sellers: if one wheat farmer plants half an acre more, the global price doesn’t budge. 50 pizza shops clears this bar; two airlines on a route does not.

Homogeneous: a bushel of Kansas wheat is a bushel of Kansas wheat. Grade-A Large eggs from Iowa = from Pennsylvania. Buyers genuinely don’t care.

Once buyers start caring about brand, recipe, or “vibe,” we’ve moved toward monopolistic competition (Ch 12).

The key test for condition 1 isn’t the raw count but whether any single participant has market share large enough to matter. Homogeneity is what makes the product a commodity — and what kills any one seller’s ability to charge more.

Conditions 3 & 4 — Free Entry, Price-Takers

Free entry & exit: a firm that spots profit can enter; a firm losing money can leave. This is what drives the long-run zero-profit result later in the chapter.

Price taker: a firm that must accept the market price as given. It chooses its quantity; it cannot influence the price.

A price-taker is not weak — she is rational. Charge above market → lose every customer. Charge below → light money on fire.

You met “price taker” loosely in Ch 4; here we make it precise. Condition 4 follows from 1–3: with many sellers of an identical good, no one can deviate from the going price. Free entry/exit is the engine of §10.5.

5 AM at the Boston Fish Pier

Vessels offload their catch; buyers bid by species and grade. A fisherman with 3,000 lbs of cod cannot demand $3/lb if the clearing price is $1.75 —

… but he also never has to beg. He will always get $1.75 if he’s willing to sell. The market guarantees it.

Discuss

What psychological experience does the fisherman have that your cable company never has? How would that shape your advice on running his business?

The fisherman’s experience: total powerlessness over price, total certainty he can sell at the going price. A monopolist (cable co.) has the opposite: power over price but must worry about losing quantity. Advice to the fisherman: you can’t out-price rivals — compete on COST. Tee up the firm-level decision.

The Firm’s Demand Curve Is Flat

Market demand slopes down. But the demand facing one firm is a horizontal line at the market price. How can both be true?

Because the firm is a tiny share. If Alex raises her price, she loses all 49 rivals’ worth of customers. If she lowers it, she can sell everything she makes without moving the market.

Marginal revenue (MR) = revenue from one more unit. For a price-taker, every unit sells at the same price, so…

P = MR

The single most important equation in the chapter. Everything builds on it.

Figure 10.1. The flat firm demand = the firm’s MR line. Drive home P = MR for the price-taker: sell the 41st slice at $3.95 and the 42nd at $3.95, TR rises by exactly $3.95 each time, so MR = price. This is what makes the next section’s MR=MC collapse to P=MC.

The Competitive Firm’s Decision

Alex can’t choose her price — only her quantity

Alex’s NY Pizza at a $10 Price

FC = $50/day (rent, oven, POS). Labor is variable. Each worker makes 10 slices/hr.

Profit peaks at $120. The 41st–50th slices each contribute $0 at the margin — so Alex makes all 50 (the higher tied quantity). Make 60 and the last slices lose money.

Walk the marginal columns. MC starts at $9 (10 slices), dips to $5 (diminishing returns not yet biting), rises to $12 (kitchen crowds). Profit is flat-topped at Q=40 and Q=50 because slice 41–50 break even. Rule: keep going while the marginal slice at least breaks even → produce 50. ATC at Q=50 = $380/50 = $7.60, profit = (10−7.60)×50 = $120. Matches.

The MR = MC Rule

  • 40 → 50: adds $100 revenue, $100 cost — breaks even
  • 50 → 60: adds $100 revenue, $120 cost — loses $20
  • 30 → 40: adds $100 revenue, $80 cost — earns $20

Keep producing while MR > MC; stop when they’re equal. For a price-taker, since P = MR, this is just P = MC.

Figure 10.2. The rule is embarrassingly simple. Because P = MR for the price-taker, MR = MC collapses to P = MC. The intersection where MC is RISING is the profit max (the lower crossing on the dip is not). The firm reads its quantity off where the flat MR line meets the upward MC.

Five Steps to Profit-Max on a Graph

1

Find MR = MC. The intersection gives the profit-maximizing quantity Q*.

2

Read Q* off the quantity axis — that’s how much to produce.

3

Read the price off the MR line (P = MR for a competitive firm).

4

Read ATC at Q* — go straight up from Q* to the ATC curve.

5

Profit = (P − ATC) × Q* — the profit rectangle.

Alex at Q=50: ATC = $380/50 = $7.60 → profit = ($10 − $7.60) × 50 = $120 ✓

This is the exam procedure. Drill it. The most common error is reading price off the ATC or MC curve instead of the MR line. P is ALWAYS the height of the flat MR line for a competitive firm.

Shutdown, Loss-Min, and the Three Price Zones

Zone 1 — P > min ATC: economic profit. Produce, pocket it.

Zone 2 — min AVC < P < min ATC: losing money, but stay open — you still cover some fixed cost.

Zone 3 — P < min AVC: every slice loses money before rent. Shut down.

Figure 10.3. The gold star is break-even (min ATC); the red dot is the shutdown floor (min AVC). The counterintuitive zone is 2: keep operating at a loss because FC is sunk in the short run — revenue above AVC chips away at the fixed cost you’d otherwise swallow whole.

Why Shutdown Is About AVC, Not ATC

Rain drops the price to $7.50. Alex loses money — should she close? No. FC = $50 whether she opens or not. Open and she loses less than $50.

Every slice above AVC contributes toward that fixed cost. Fixed costs are sunk in the short run.

Misconception

“Shut down the moment you start losing money.”

No — losing $25 by staying open beats losing $50 by closing. The question is not “am I profitable?” but “am I covering variable costs?”

The single most counterintuitive result in the chapter. Anchor it: the most you can lose by shutting down is your fixed cost. As long as P ≥ AVC, operating loses you LESS than that. Below AVC, operating actively subtracts money — close the kitchen.

Sidebar — The Hotel Room at 1 AM

A room will sit empty all night. The variable cost of renting it once more — housekeeping, toiletries, laundry, power — is about $40.

A traveler offers $100. Since $100 > $40 (above AVC), take it… right?

The subtlety: if the hotel is known to slash prices at 1 AM, tomorrow’s full-price travelers wait until 1 AM too. Expected future revenue falls by more than the $100 picked up tonight.

So the hotel rationally refuses — “shutting down” the room to protect the whole future price curve.

The shutdown logic at the right edge: even when a one-night calc says “sell,” reputation/dynamic-pricing effects can make the rational choice “don’t.” This is a nice bridge to revenue management and the idea that very low marginal cost doesn’t automatically mean “sell at any price above MC.”

The Ghost Kitchen Bust (2023–2026)

Delivery-only restaurants looked low-cost: no storefront, no dining room, no waitstaff. The catch was variable cost — delivery platforms take 15–30% of every order (~22% typical).

$50K monthly revenue → $7.5K–$15K in delivery fees alone, before a single ingredient.

When demand softened in 2024–25, many fell below AVC. Nov 2025: CloudKitchens shut a 21,000-sq-ft Oakland complex that once held 42 operators.

They didn’t fail because the food was bad. At the market price, they couldn’t cover the variable cost of an order.

The shutdown condition in the wild. When P drops below AVC, you close — not because you give up, but because you’re being rational. Great real example of variable cost dominating a “low fixed cost” business model.

Section 4 · LO 10.4

Short-Run Supply

Where the Chapter-4 supply curve actually comes from

From MC to the Firm’s Supply Curve

Tell Alex the price is $7 — she finds where MC = $7 and produces that. Tell her $8 — she walks up her MC curve. As price rises, she traces out her MC curve.

A competitive firm’s supply curve = its MC curve above min AVC. Below that floor, it shuts down and supplies zero.

This is the bridge: cost curves (Ch 9, from technology) become a supply curve (Ch 4, drawn as if from the sky).

This is the conceptual payoff of Ch 9. The firm always sets P=MC, so feeding it different prices traces the MC curve — that IS supply. The only exception is the shutdown region below min AVC, where supply drops to zero. Chapter 10 is the bridge between cost theory and the supply curve.

From One Firm to the Market

For any price, ask each firm how much it supplies and add up the answers. The market supply curve is the horizontal sum of individual firm supplies.

At $10, each of 50 shops wants 50 slices → market supply = 2,500 slices. At $11 → ~2,750. Trace it → upward-sloping market supply.

Built from nothing but individual MC curves.

Horizontal sum: fix the price, sum the quantities across firms. The market supply slope is flatter than the firm’s because the x-axis is scaled by the number of firms. This is the supply side of the S&D diagrams from Ch 4–7, now derived from first principles.

Data Snapshot — The HPAI Egg Shock

2024–25: avian flu forced culling of 38.4M egg-layers. A Grade-A Large egg is genuinely homogeneous — no brand loyalty to cushion the loss. Supply vanished.

Eggs hit $4.95/dozen in Jan 2025 (+50% YoY). Consumer-surplus loss in 2024 ≈ $1.41 billion.

Why no fast self-correction? A replacement chick takes ~6 months to lay — short-run supply is near-vertical.

Try it: FRED series WPU017107 (PPI: Eggs), Jan 2024 – Feb 2026. By Feb 2026 a flock rebuild crashed wholesale below $0.92.

A perfectly competitive market meeting a biological supply ceiling. The steep (inelastic) short-run supply is the whole story: a 12% quantity cut along inelastic demand produced a 50% price spike. Then over-rebuilding overshot the other way. The companion FRED series lets students measure the spike themselves.

The Soybean Farmer on the Trade Precipice

China bought >60% of globally-traded soybeans — mostly from the US. 2024–25 tariffs pushed the duty to 34%. By the 2025–26 season, China had ~zero new US orders, shifting to Brazil.

The puzzle

Spring price ~$10.54/bu, cost of production ~$12.05/bu — negative economic profit — yet farmers kept farming. Why?

Because in the short run the alternative was worse. Seed, fertilizer, fuel, land — already sunk. Tearing up the crop recovers none of it.

As long as harvesting covers the remaining variable costs, bring the beans to market even at a loss. The shutdown condition, across an entire industry.

Same shutdown logic as the pizza shop, scaled to a whole industry and triggered by a demand collapse rather than a supply shock. The sunk planting costs are the fixed cost; harvesting is the variable cost. P ≥ AVC → harvest and sell. Sets up the long-run adjustment: persistent losses eventually drive exit.

Section 5 · LO 10.5

Long-Run Equilibrium

The invisible hand at work

Entry, Exit, and the Zero-Profit Result

Start in long-run equilibrium: every shop earns zero economic profit (owners earn exactly their next-best alternative). Nobody enters, nobody leaves.

A new dorm opens → demand shifts right → price rises $8→$10 → Alex now earns $120. That’s the signal.

Entrepreneurs see the profit → new shops open → supply shifts right → price falls → back to zero profit. Overshoot? Some exit, price rises. It settles.

Long-run analysis = let the number of firms change. Positive profit is a magnet for entry; losses drive exit. The “dance” around equilibrium is the invisible hand — no planner, just profit signals. Zero ECONOMIC profit (not accounting) is the resting point.

Entry Drives Profit to Zero

Left: entry shifts market supply right (S₀→S₁), price falls $11→$8.

Right: the firm’s green profit rectangle shrinks until P = min ATC — the gold star — where profit = 0.

The market ends at the price where every surviving firm sits at the bottom of its ATC curve — its efficient scale.

Figure 10.5. Watch the two panels move together: market price falling as supply expands, firm profit rectangle collapsing to zero. Only efficient-scale firms (min ATC) survive in the long run. A less-efficient firm gets undercut and exits.

The Long-Run Equilibrium Condition

P = MR  —  every firm is a price-taker

P = MC  —  every firm is profit-maximizing

P = min ATC  —  entry/exit drove profit to zero

∴ MC = ATC at each firm’s output — the GPA-analogy crossing from Ch 9

All four hold simultaneously, only in long-run perfect competition.

String the equalities together. The last line is a geometric fact: MC crosses ATC at the ATC minimum (GPA analogy from Ch 9). In long-run perfect competition, every firm operates at exactly that point — productive efficiency built in.

The Trucking Freight Recession (2023–2025)

Pandemic goods boom → freight rates spiked → new owner-operators piled in (2022 entry at historic highs), most on multi-year truck loans.

Then demand normalized, but the trucks remained. Spot rates fell to ~$1.75/mile — below many operators’ cost.

Thousands of small carriers exited in 2023–24. Average age of exiting fleets: only 3 years — recent entrants who miscalculated breakeven.

Each departure shifts market supply left. By 2025, enough exit to stop the bleeding. Prices rise back to min ATC; profit returns to zero. The dance restarts.

The exit phase of the long-run adjustment, cleanly observed. Over-entry during the boom → losses → exit → supply contracts → price recovers to min ATC. The young exiting fleets make the point: these weren’t failing dinosaurs, just miscalculated breakevens.

Zero Profit Is a Feature, Not a Bug

1. Zero economic ≠ zero accounting. A trucking owner-operator at zero economic profit still takes home a paycheck — exactly what she’d earn driving for someone else. Nobody works for free.

2. Zero profit is exactly what you want. Persistent positive profit → consumers overpaying. Persistent losses → firms subsidizing consumers. Zero is the Goldilocks result.

Consumers pay exactly what production costs; producers are exactly compensated for what they give up.

Students find zero profit depressing until you separate economic from accounting profit. The deeper point: zero economic profit is the socially optimal resting point — price equals true opportunity cost. This is the welfare punchline that motivates the next slide.

The Efficiency Payoff

Productive efficiency

Each firm produces at min ATC — the smallest possible resources per unit. No waste.

Allocative efficiency

P = MC — the marginal buyer’s willingness to pay equals the resource cost. No valuable trade left undone.

Both kinds of efficiency, achieved automatically. This is the First Welfare Theorem (Arrow & Debreu, 1950s) that Ch 6 previewed — now in its home habitat.

Connect back to Ch 6 total surplus: surplus is maximized where MB (demand) = MC (supply). Perfect competition gets there automatically because P = MC for every firm. This is the formal sense in which the invisible hand “works.” First Welfare Theorem.

Economist at Work — Vernon Smith, 1962

Does the theory actually work with real, non-calculating people? Smith handed out index cards — private max prices to buyers, min prices to sellers — and ran a double oral auction.

He predicted chaos. He was wrong. Even uninformed traders converged to the theoretical equilibrium — with only a handful of participants.

Launched experimental economics. Nobel Prize, 2002. We’ll run a version in this chapter’s attendance activity.

The empirical validation of the whole model. Nobody could see the full set of cards, yet prices converged to the hidden equilibrium within a few rounds. Far from needing infinite participants, competitive equilibrium emerges from almost any public-price, no-collusion trading environment. Forward-pointer to the in-class experiment.

Technology Permanently Lowers the Price

A pure demand shock leaves the long-run price unchanged — entry just expands the market. But a cost-reducing shock is different.

Automation, cheaper energy, better software → the whole cost family shifts down → entry drives price to the new, lower min ATC. Consumers keep the gap forever.

Same mechanism (entry → zero profit), very different welfare outcome.

Figure 10.6. The subtle point that long-run entry doesn’t always return the market to where it started. Demand shift: price unchanged, market larger. Cost shift: price permanently lower, consumers capture the entire efficiency gain. The ghost dashed curves are the pre-shock baseline.

Section 6 · LO 10.6

Is Perfect Competition Realistic?

Why study a frictionless plane?

Markets That Come Close

Agriculture

Millions of farmers, identical grain, zero pricing power vs the CBOT spot price. Corn ~$4.70, soy ~$10.54, wheat ~$6.19/bu. 8–9 / 10.

Financial

A T-bill is a T-bill; one dollar is one dollar. Tiny transaction costs, near-perfect info. The closest real approximation.

The Fish Pier

A display auction calling out the clearing price for cod or haddock. The physical manifestation of perfect competition.

These markets tick most boxes: many tiny sellers, homogeneous product, transparent public prices, no pricing power. Financial markets are the closest because the product is the most homogeneous and info is nearly perfect — which is why economists use them as testing grounds.

Markets That Break the Model

Campus Coffee & neighbors — many sellers ✓, but products differentiated (menu, vibe, baristas). Each faces a slightly downward demand → monopolistic competition (Ch 12).

Amazon Marketplace — looks competitive, but brand & reviews differentiate, and the algorithmic Buy Box decides who sells. Platform design beats the “many sellers” headline (Ch 19).

The streaming wars — a few players, non-substitutable libraries, huge fixed costs. Netflix passed 325M subs and raised price to ~$19 losing almost no one — real market power (Ch 13).

The benchmark is what lets us say “slightly different” — we know what homogeneity predicts, so we can measure the deviation.

For each, name WHICH condition fails: coffee & Amazon fail homogeneity (condition 2); Amazon also fails price-taking (condition 4 via the Buy Box); streaming fails number-of-firms and free entry. The benchmark’s power is comparison — you can’t measure a deviation without it.

The Great Markup Debate

De Loecker, Eeckhout & Unger (2020): US markups rose from 21% → 61% above MC (1980–mid-2010s). Evidence of growing market power.

Basu / Traina & others: measurement confuses rising fixed costs (software, biotech, platforms) with markups. Albrecht & Decker (2024): the high-markup industries also had rising dynamism.

Serious economists read the same data and reach opposite conclusions. But you can’t take a side without first knowing what “markup = 0” looks like.

Misconception

“Zero economic profit means businesses make no money.”

It means the owner earns exactly her next-best alternative — a real paycheck. Zero economic profit is what fair compensation looks like.

The payoff of the whole chapter: perfect competition is the yardstick. Whatever you think of the markup debate, you need the P = MC benchmark to even define a “markup.” Close on the misconception — zero economic profit = fair compensation, not poverty.

Key Takeaways

1. Five characteristics, four structures. Perfect competition: many firms, homogeneous product, free entry, price-takers, zero long-run profit.

2. P = MR = MC. The firm faces flat demand and produces where MR = MC — equivalently P = MC.

3. Shutdown is about AVC. Operate whenever P ≥ min AVC, even at a loss. Supply = MC above min AVC.

4. Entry & exit → P = min ATC. Free entry competes profit to zero. The invisible hand, no planner required.

5. The benchmark matters even when reality falls short — productive (min ATC) and allocative (P = MC) efficiency, both at once.
