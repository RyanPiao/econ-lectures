The Basics of Production — Final Goods

Production describes the process by which a person, company, government, or non-profit agency uses inputs to create a good or service for which others are willing to pay.

Final good — a good that is bought by a consumer.

Example: the Apple I, sold at $666.66 in July 1976.

The Apple I went on sale in July 1976 at a price of US$666.66

Open the “Basics of Production” mini-section right after the Production video lead-in. Definition slide focused on the final-good concept. The Apple I is the canonical example for the chapter — students should walk away knowing that a “final good” is whatever the consumer ultimately buys (the working computer), not the components that went into it. Forward-pointer to next slide: we contrast with intermediate goods, then identify the factor inputs.

The Basics of Production — Intermediate Goods

Production describes the process by which a person, company, government, or non-profit agency uses inputs to create a good or service for which others are willing to pay.

Intermediate good — a good that is used to produce another good.

Example: the bare PCB and the MOS 6502 chip are intermediate goods used to produce the Apple I.

The intermediate/final distinction matters because it determines what gets counted in firm output. We measure Q as final goods; intermediate goods are inputs absorbed by the production process. The 6502 chip was sourced from MOS Technology in Pennsylvania at ~$25 each; the bare PCB was designed by Wozniak and fabricated by an outside contractor. Both feed into the Apple I (the final good). For the rest of the chapter K and L will be the two inputs we track explicitly — capital and labor — but intermediate goods are implicitly absorbed into K when we cost them as purchased inputs.

The Entrepreneur — The Risk They Bear

If the project fails, the entrepreneur eats the loss. Three flavors of risk:

1. Risk of failure — bankruptcy, lawsuits

U.S. business bankruptcy filings: 13,481 (2022) → 23,107 (2024). +71% in two years.

2. Capital risk — financial

Funding comes through financial markets. VC firms accept big risk in exchange for ownership shares.

3. Production risk — labor & materials

Use inputs inefficiently → more efficient rivals drive you out. Profit hinges on price, quantity sold, and costs.

By year 10, ~2 in 3 small businesses have folded. The flip side of high reward.

This is why economic profit — not accounting profit — is the right metric. It already subtracts the opportunity cost of bearing all three risks.

Risk is the price of entrepreneurship. Three categories: (1) Risk of failure — the most visceral. Cite the +71% jump in U.S. bankruptcy filings from 2022 to 2024 (post-zero-rate hangover + rising rates). (2) Capital risk — raise from VCs at the cost of equity; if it fails, the equity is worthless. (3) Production risk — competitors with lower costs eat your margin (segue to perfect competition in Ch 10). The chart shows the cumulative failure rate: 20% by year 1, 49% by year 5, 65% by year 10. Closing callout bridges back to Maria’s bakery: when she gave up the $150K salary, she took on all three risks; economic profit subtracts that opportunity cost so we can see whether the gamble was worth it.

What Is a Firm?

— and why does it exist at all?

Business Structures — Who Owns the Firm?

SOLE PROPRIETORSHIP

  • One owner manages the business
  • Easy to establish but limited capital
  • Owner has unlimited liability

~70% of U.S. businesses

PARTNERSHIP

  • Two or more owners share responsibilities + division of labor
  • Easier to raise capital than sole prop
  • Joint unlimited liability

Apple in the garage was one

CORPORATION

  • Legal entity with rights similar to a person
  • Can issue stock to raise capital
  • Shareholders have limited liability but less direct control

~90% of U.S. sales

Three legal forms, each with its own tradeoffs between control, liability, and capital. Sole prop: easiest to start, but personal assets are on the line (unlimited liability). Partnership: pool labor + capital, but each partner is on the hook for joint liability — one bad decision sinks both. Corporation: legal personhood means it can sign contracts, sue/be sued, own property, and most importantly issue stock to raise capital. Shareholders have LIMITED liability (you only lose your investment, not your house). Trade-off: most owners (shareholders) have very little direct control — that’s why we have principal-agent problems (Ch 18 behavioral). Note the inversion on the bottom row: sole props are the MAJORITY of firms (70%) but corporations do the MAJORITY of sales (90%) — a few giants dominate revenue, lots of small operators dominate counts.

Business Structures — By the Numbers

Pass-throughs exploded — sole props + S-corps + partnerships now dwarf C-corps in number of returns. Pass-through income is taxed at the owner’s personal rate, not the corporate rate.

But corporations still dominate the dollar. Most U.S. firms are tiny solo operations; most U.S. revenue flows through a small number of giant corporations.

The two-chart payoff: count vs. dollars. Sole proprietorships are by far the most common business form (73%) and the area chart shows them growing fastest since 1980 thanks to the rise of freelance/contract/Etsy/Uber-driver-style work. But that ~73% of firms generates a small fraction of U.S. economic output. Corporations (just 5.6% of firm count) generate 90% of sales. Pedagogical bridge: when we model "the firm" in this course we usually mean a representative profit-maximizer that could be any of these structures — but in policy debates the structure matters a lot (tax incidence, regulation, liability law). The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act lowered both rates but with different mechanisms for C-corps vs. pass-throughs; the long-tail of small businesses (sole props + partnerships) is where most of the political pressure for "small-business relief" comes from.

Coase 1937 — The Nature of the Firm

If markets are so efficient…

Why does Apple employ 164,000 people in a hierarchy instead of posting each task on a freelance marketplace?

Coase’s answer: Using the market is expensive. Search, negotiate, monitor, enforce — those transaction costs can exceed the savings from buying through a price.

Nobel Prize, 1991. He was 27 when he wrote it.

Frame: every chapter so far has been "what does the market do." Today we ask "where does the firm come from?" Coase's 1937 paper is the canonical answer — read it as a college junior on a London School of Economics traveling scholarship to American factories. The puzzle of why firms exist is more profound than it sounds; before Coase, economists basically didn't ask. After Coase, transaction-cost economics became a whole field (Williamson, Hart, Holmström — all later Nobels).

Asset Specificity & the Hold-Up Problem

Williamson (Nobel 2009): Transaction costs are especially high when one party makes an investment that’s valuable in one relationship but nearly worthless outside it.

The Risk

A semiconductor fab built for one client’s custom chips creates a hold-up problem: each side fears the other will renegotiate after the relationship is locked in.

The Solution

Vertical integration: bring the relationship inside the firm. Apple-designed chips, Tesla-owned battery cells, Amazon-built warehouses.

Modern twist: Uber and DoorDash use independent contractors instead of employees — algorithmic matching + GPS + automated pay have driven transaction costs low enough that market beats hierarchy again. (We’ll revisit in Ch 14.)

Asset specificity is the modern version of Coase. The trade-off is dynamic: when monitoring tech improves, the firm boundary shrinks (gig economy). When monitoring is hard, the firm grows (Apple acquires its chip designer). Williamson's 2009 Nobel was for formalizing this.

Costs That Matter for Decision Making — Opportunity Costs

Economic costs:

  • Explicit costs: out-of-pocket expenses.
  • Implicit costs: opportunity cost of using a firm’s resources.

Economic Costs = Explicit + Implicit costs

Start-up: Restaurant / Boba Tea Shop

Define both cost categories side by side and anchor with a concrete case: the boba tea shop. Explicit (the green icons): rent on the storefront, taxes, employee paychecks, milk + tea leaves, electricity, water, internet — everything you swipe a card for. Implicit (the red icons): the salary the owner could have earned working somewhere else, and the investment returns on the capital tied up in the business. Both kinds of cost are REAL economic costs even though only the explicit ones show up on a P&L. The next two slides compute accounting profit (explicit only) and economic profit (explicit + implicit) for this exact shop.

Accounting Profit

Accounting profit = Total revenue − Explicit costs

  • TR = P × Q = $25 × 40,000 = $1,000,000
  • Total Explicit Costs = $800,000
  • Accounting profit = $1,000,000 − $800,000 = $200,000

Start-up: Restaurant / Boba Tea Shop

Explicit Costs: $800,000

$25 average ticket × 40,000 transactions/year = $1M revenue. Subtract $800K of explicit operating costs (rent, payroll, ingredients, utilities). Accounting profit = $200K. By accounting standards, the shop looks profitable. That’s the trap. The next slide adds the implicit costs and the picture flips.

Economic Profit

Economic profit = Total revenue − Explicit − Implicit costs

  • Total revenue − Explicit = $1,000,000 − $800,000 = $200,000
  • Implicit costs = $100,000 (forgone salary) + $100,000 (forgone investment return) = $200,000
  • Economic profit = $1,000,000 − $800,000 − $200,000 = $0

Start-up: Restaurant / Boba Tea Shop

Explicit Costs: $800,000

Implicit Costs: $200,000

The punchline: the same boba tea shop that looked like a $200K winner on accounting books is generating ZERO economic profit. Why? Because the owner gave up $100K of salary she could have earned elsewhere AND $100K of investment return on the capital she tied up in the shop. Zero economic profit means she’s exactly indifferent between running the shop and the alternatives — not a loss, but not value-creating either. Connect to Maria’s bakery (next slide pair): same logic, different numbers, and Maria’s situation ends up at a NEGATIVE economic profit. Big idea: when the news says “50% of small businesses fail in 5 years,” many of the “survivors” are actually breaking even or losing money in economic terms.

Explicit vs. Implicit Costs

Explicit costs

Out-of-pocket payments. Show up on a financial statement.

Wages, rent, ingredients, equipment…

Implicit costs

Opportunity cost of resources you already own — your forgone next-best alternative.

Owner’s salary elsewhere, interest forgone on capital…

Connection to Ch 1 opportunity cost: same logic applies to firms. Implicit costs are real economic costs even if they never appear on a tax return. This distinction will drive the Maria example next.

Two Franchise Models — Same Logic

Chick-fil-A

  • $10,000 to become an operator
  • Company owns building + equipment + land
  • Earn $200–$240K/yr on $6.7M unit volume
  • No equity — can’t sell, can’t pass to kids

McDonald’s

  • Total investment $1–$2.7M
  • $45K franchise fee + $500K personal capital
  • Variable income — own the upside
  • Build equity in a real asset

Why pay $2.7M when you can pay $10K? — the Chick-fil-A operator’s implicit cost is the equity they never accumulate.

Both decisions are rational — they’re just optimizing different cost structures. CFA = low explicit, high implicit (lost equity). MCD = high explicit, lower implicit. Either can be right depending on your capital position and risk tolerance.

Production in the Short Run

Inputs in → output out — with one input held fixed

Short Run vs. Long Run

Short Run

At least one input is fixed — usually capital (the building, the machines).

Can hire more cooks; can’t expand the kitchen overnight.

Long Run

All inputs are variable — the firm can pick its size.

Move locations, buy new machines, even open new factories.

“Short” vs “long” is not about clock time — for a restaurant SR ≈ months; for a semiconductor fab SR ≈ 5 years (a new fab takes that long to build).

Reframe time horizon: it’s about whether the firm can adjust its capital stock, not calendar months. For TSMC, "short run" is half a decade.

The Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns

Definition

As additional units of a variable input are added to a fixed input, the marginal product of the variable input eventually declines.

It’s about the fixed input, not lazy workers

Workers crowd around one espresso machine. Add more bodies — physics doesn’t add machines.

Diminishing ≠ falling total

Total output keeps rising while MP > 0. It only falls once MP turns negative (worker 6 at Campus Coffee).

Common student error: "diminishing returns means output falls." Correct it now — diminishing means the rate of increase slows. Output falls only when MP becomes negative. Use the table: at worker 3, MP=12 (down from 18) — that’s diminishing returns. Total still rising. Only at worker 6 does total fall.

Diminishing Returns in the Wild

Amazon warehouses

1.56M workers, 20-25M packages/day, but each fulfillment center has ~55 loading docks and fixed floor space.

Serious injury rate 5.9 vs 3.0 per 100 — pushing labor past the fixed-capacity ceiling.

Brooks’s Law (1975)

“Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.”

Communication channels grow as n(n−1)/2: team of 3 → 3, team of 10 → 45. New hires steal time from veterans.

Agriculture

USDA: 0→50 lbs nitrogen/acre huge yield gain. 143→153 lbs barely moves yield.

Beyond that: negative MP — soil toxicity, runoff, lower yields.

Coffee shops, code, corn fields, cars — same law, every time.

These three are the canonical examples — services, knowledge work, agriculture. The general principle: any time you can scale labor faster than capital, you eventually hit a wall. The Amazon injury data is the social-cost version: when MP falls, it doesn't just hurt the spreadsheet — it hurts the workers.

Every point on the production function implies a cost — let’s build the cost curves

Fixed Costs vs. Variable Costs

Fixed costs (FC)

Don’t vary with Q. Must be paid even at Q = 0.

Rent ($3,000) + equipment lease ($500) + insurance ($200) + phone ($50) = $3,750/mo

Variable costs (VC)

Change with Q — ingredients, hourly wages.

Total, Variable, and Fixed Cost

FC — flat. Doesn’t care about Q.

VC — starts at 0, rises with Q.

TC = FC + VC — always $25 above VC.

Watch the slope of TC rise sharply past Q=42 — that’s diminishing returns showing up as steeper costs. The 5th worker adds only 2 drinks but costs $15/hr in wages, so VC rises $18 while output rises only 2 drinks → MC explodes to $9.

Per-Unit Costs — The Four Cost Curves

AFC = FC / Q

Average fixed cost — always falls as Q rises (spreading FC).

AVC = VC / Q

Average variable cost — typically U-shaped.

ATC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC

Average total cost — U-shaped. Always above AVC.

MC = ΔTC / ΔQ

Marginal cost — cost of one more unit. The most important cost for decisions.

When MP is high → MC is low  ·  when MP is low → MC is high. They’re mirror images.

Define each, but emphasize MC. Connect to the production function: MC = wage / MP for a variable input. The same diminishing returns that lowered MP in §2 raise MC here.

The Canonical Cost Curves

MC slices through both AVC and ATC at their minima — an identity that holds for every firm.

This is the chart from the textbook (Fig 9.2). Every cost-curve question on the exam ties back to this picture. Make sure students can identify each curve, name the intersection points, and explain why MC must cross the averages at their minima.

Why Is ATC U-Shaped?

Why ATC falls first

  • AFC drops fast — FC spread over more units
  • Early specialization keeps AVC tame (2nd barista)

The “spreading FC” force dominates.

Why ATC rises later

  • Diminishing MP pushes AVC up sharply
  • AFC is now tiny — nothing to offset rising AVC

The “rising VC” force takes over.

The bottom of the U is the efficient scale — the firm’s lowest possible per-unit cost.

For Campus Coffee: 42 drinks/hr with 3 baristas, ATC = $3.17.

U-shape comes from a race between two forces: spreading FC (pulls ATC down) and diminishing MP (pushes ATC up). At low Q, the first wins. At high Q, the second wins. Where they balance: ATC minimum = efficient scale.

MC & ATC — The GPA Analogy

Your cumulative GPA = ATC

Your next term’s GPA = MC.

Next term > cumulative ⇒ cumulative rises

Next term < cumulative ⇒ cumulative falls

Next term = cumulative ⇒ turning point (cumulative is flat)

Same idea for cost

When MC < ATC: marginal unit costs less than the average, so it pulls average down.

When MC > ATC: marginal unit costs more, so it pulls average up.

When MC = ATC: ATC must be at its minimum.

GPA is the killer analogy for this. Every student has felt it: one bad semester drops the GPA; one great one raises it; the turnaround is when your new term matches your cumulative. Same identity governs MC and ATC — it has to be true mathematically.

Poll — The MC/ATC Check

A bakery’s ATC = $4. The MC of the next loaf is $3. What happens to ATC if the bakery makes that next loaf?

A. ATC rises (more cost means higher average)

B. ATC falls (next unit costs less than the average)

C. ATC stays at $4 (averages don’t move from one unit)

Vote — results above. (Hint: GPA analogy.)

Correct: B. MC=$3 < ATC=$4, so the next unit pulls the average DOWN. (D is a Ch 10 question — profit maximization — but here we’re asking purely about averages.)

Common Misconception — FC and Profit-Max

The myth

“If my rent goes up, I should make more drinks to spread the cost.”

The truth

Profit-max Q depends on MR = MC — and FC has zero effect on MC.

Higher rent → lower profit, but the same profit-maximizing quantity. (We’ll prove this in Chapter 10.)

Critical concept that students get wrong on exams. FC affects the LEVEL of profit, not the OPTIMAL Q. The negotiated lower rent makes you richer; it doesn’t make you serve more coffee. Tee up Ch 10 firmly.

Section 4 · LO 9.4

Long-Run Costs & Economies of Scale

When the firm can choose its plant size, what shape does cost take?

From Short Run to Long Run

Small Campus Coffee

1 machine, 400 ft². Low ATC at low volumes; espresso machine becomes a bottleneck quickly.

Bigger Campus Coffee

3 machines, 1,200 ft². High ATC at low volumes (too much rent); lower ATC at high volumes (no bottleneck).

Each plant size traces its own short-run ATC curve. The LRATC is the lower envelope — the lowest achievable cost across every plant choice.

In the long run the owner picks the plant size that minimizes cost for the volume she expects. The "envelope" idea: don't be stuck on one short-run ATC — pick the best of all of them.

The LRATC Envelope

The bottom of LRATC is the Minimum Efficient Scale (MES) — the smallest output that achieves the lowest possible unit cost.

Define MES out loud: it’s the smallest scale at which the firm reaches the lowest LRATC. Firms below MES are at a cost disadvantage. MES is a market-structure variable too — industries with high MES (semiconductors, airlines) end up concentrated (few firms); industries with low MES (restaurants) end up fragmented.

Why Bigger Can Be Better — 4 Sources of Economies of Scale

1. Spreading fixed costs

Billions in licensing → tiny per-subscriber. Spotify, AI training, drug development.

2. Specialization

3-person shop: everyone does everything. 30-person factory: specialists. Adam Smith’s pin factory, 1776.

3. Bulk purchasing

Chipotle’s 3,600 locations negotiate ingredient prices no single restaurant ever could.

4. Technical efficiencies

A 2×-wide pipeline carries 4× the volume (the “two-thirds rule”). Physics rewards scale.

4 distinct mechanisms — students often blur them. Spreading FC is the most powerful in software/digital. Specialization is what Adam Smith was on about in Wealth of Nations. Bulk purchasing is contract economics. Technical efficiency is engineering.

The Most Extreme Economies of Scale Ever

Training (FC)

  • Gemini Ultra: ~$191M
  • GPT-4: ~$78M
  • Llama 3.1 405B: ~$170M
  • Anthropic 2026 training budget: $12B

Inference (MC)

Inference cost fell 280× for GPT-3.5-level performance between Nov 2022 and Oct 2024. Output tokens as low as $0.42/M.

The structural implication

Massive FC + tiny MC ⇒ you must achieve gigantic scale to amortize the training run.

Only a few firms can clear MES ⇒ the frontier-AI market naturally consolidates into an oligopoly.

Same cost-structure logic we’ll see in Ch 11 (natural monopoly) and Ch 13 (oligopoly).

Frame AI as the limiting case: highest FC-to-MC ratio ever. The market shape (a few mega-firms) is downstream of the cost shape. Tee up Ch 11 + Ch 13.

TSMC — $165B for Economies of Scale

$165B committed to a 6-fab “giga-cluster” in Phoenix, AZ. Each 2nm fab: $25–$35B.

Why cluster 6 fabs?

Share advanced packaging, engineers, chemical supply chain, logistics across 6 plants. Direct labor < 2% of cost — almost everything is FC that loves scale.

Per-wafer cost so low that in-house fab is irrational for everyone but Intel and Samsung. MES as a moat.

TSMC physically embodies LRATC + MES + scale-as-strategy. Apple, Nvidia, AMD all buy from TSMC because they can’t match TSMC’s per-wafer cost. This is what it looks like when MES is huge and only a couple firms can clear it.

Why Bigger Isn’t Always Better — 3 Sources of Diseconomies

1. Coordination costs

More layers, slower decisions, mistakes propagate further. (See Brooks’s Law — same logic at the org level.)

2. Bureaucratic complexity

Procedures, approvals, compliance overhead — the cost of being big enough to need rules.

3. Loss of focus

Conglomerates often find managing diverse businesses creates more complexity than synergy. (Berkshire Hathaway is the exception that proves the rule.)

Healthcare mergers (2024-25): 30.6% of deals involved distressed sellers. Research finds horizontal/vertical hospital mergers raise prices 6-65% with no quality improvement — coordination + IT integration costs push LRATC up.

If economies were unlimited, we’d have one giant firm per industry. We don’t — because of these three frictions. Healthcare consolidation is the worked example: bigger hospital system, higher prices, no better outcomes — diseconomies on display.

Craft Brewers vs. Anheuser-Busch

AB InBev: 0.1–0.3 workers per 1,000 barrels. ~10,000 craft brewers survive at 1–2 workers per 1,000 barrels.

How do they survive?

Mass-producing a hazy IPA kills the local/artisanal positioning that justifies the premium price. Diseconomies of authenticity.

Higher ATC + higher P → thrives next to the giant. We’ll formalize this in Ch 12 (monopolistic competition).

Surprise twist: scale is not always the right strategy. Differentiation lets a small firm live happily inside the big firm’s shadow. Bonus: SSRN 2024 paper found diseconomies at Netflix too — content costs growing faster than subscribers. Digital doesn’t guarantee infinite scale.

Section 5 · LO 9.5

Unit Economics & Break-Even

Does the firm make money on each transaction?

Contribution Margin

Contribution margin = Price per unit − variable cost per unit. Each unit’s margin contributes to covering FC; once FC is covered, every additional margin is profit.

Campus Coffee at efficient scale (3 baristas, 42 drinks/hr)

$5.25 (P) − $2.57 (AVC)

= $2.68 / drink

What it means

Every drink throws $2.68 at the $3,750/month fixed-cost pile.

Once the pile is paid off, every additional $2.68 is pure profit.

Bridge: now we’ve seen FC, VC, MC. Unit economics is the practitioner’s toolkit that puts those concepts to work in real business decisions.

The Break-Even Formula

Break-Even Quantity

QBE = FC ÷ Contribution Margin

Campus Coffee:

$3,750 ÷ $2.68

= 1,399 drinks/month

Open 25 days/mo → 56 drinks/day → ~8/hour across a 7-hour day.

Easy in the morning rush; tough during a 2 PM lull.

Below Q_BE: every drink is a loss. Above Q_BE: every drink is pure profit (contribution margin times # drinks above break-even). That’s why coffee shops live for the morning rush — fixed costs get paid off there; afternoon drinks are all upside.

Break-Even, Visualized

Every drink above QBE = 1,399 adds $2.68 directly to profit.

The visual makes break-even concrete: TR is a line through the origin with slope = price. TC is a line starting at FC with slope = AVC. The crossover is break-even. Above: green. Below: red.

SaaS — LTV vs. CAC

CAC

Customer Acquisition Cost — marketing + sales + trial cost to win one customer.

LTV

Customer Lifetime Value — total revenue a customer generates before they cancel.

Healthy SaaS rule of thumb: LTV : CAC ≥ 3 : 1

2024–2025 reality: Average payback period = 23 months. CAC up 40–60% since 2023 (privacy rules, rising ad costs). If a customer churns before month 23 → loss on every acquisition.

LTV:CAC is the SaaS version of contribution margin per customer divided by acquisition fixed cost per customer. Same break-even logic, longer time horizon.

Behavioral Lens — The Sunk Cost Fallacy

Sunk costs are already spent and unrecoverable. A rational decision-maker should ignore them. Only forward-looking revenue vs. forward-looking cost matters.

But humans fall for the fallacy: “We’ve already invested too much to walk away.”

2024 AI industry: companies poured billions into training models that were quickly leapfrogged. The right move is to cut losses; the psychological pull of a $100M sunk cost makes that hard. Behavioral biases at firm scale — more in Ch 18.

This is the bridge to Ch 18 (behavioral). Firms aren’t immune to behavioral biases; their managers fall for them just like consumers do. The model is the rational benchmark; behavioral economics catalogs the deviations.

Key Takeaways

1. Firms exist because transaction costs make hierarchy cheaper than markets (Coase). Economic profit subtracts all opportunity costs.

2. Diminishing MP in the short run drives rising MC. Production and cost are mirror images.

3. ATC is U-shaped: spreading FC wins early, rising VC wins late. MC slices both AVC and ATC at their minima.

4. LRATC envelopes short-run ATCs. Economies of scale shape industry structure: high MES → few firms; low MES → many.

5. Contribution margin and break-even are the practical workhorses — coffee shops, DoorDash, SaaS, food trucks all live or die by them.
