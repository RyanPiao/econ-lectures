Economics Is Everywhere · Handout Practice: Opportunity Cost of Working vs. Studying

Set-up Tonight you can pick up a 4-hour Uber Eats shift at $18/hr (totaling $72). Alternatively, you can study for tomorrow's econ midterm. From experience, every hour of focused studying raises your expected exam score by 1.5 points on a 100-point scale.

Show your work for each part:

a

Identify the explicit cost and implicit cost of working the shift instead of studying.

b

What is the opportunity cost of working the shift, measured in expected midterm grade points?

c

If you value each expected grade point at $5 (e.g., better grade → better job prospects), at what hourly wage would you switch to studying?

d

In one sentence, explain why “the shift pays $72 cash, so I should always take it” is bad reasoning.

Answer Sketch

(a) Explicit: $0 (no money leaves your wallet to study). Implicit: $72 forgone wages from skipping the shift, OR if you take the shift, the implicit cost is the lost grade points. (b) 4 hrs × 1.5 pts = 6 expected grade points forgone. (c) Studying gives 1.5 pts/hr × $5/pt = $7.50/hr of value. You switch to studying when wage drops below $7.50/hr. (d) The $72 is the explicit benefit; you also need to subtract the implicit cost (forgone grade points worth $30) and compare net values.

Thinking Like an Economist · Handout Practice: Spotting Reasoning Errors

Set-up A think-tank op-ed claims: “Cities with more bike lanes have lower obesity rates. Therefore, building more bike lanes will reduce obesity.”

Show your work for each part:

a

Is the second sentence (the “therefore”) a positive or normative claim?

b

List three plausible alternative explanations for the bike-lane / obesity correlation that don't involve bike lanes causing weight loss.

c

Which causal method (RCT, natural experiment, DiD, A/B test) would best test whether bike lanes cause lower obesity? Briefly justify.

d

Suggest one specific natural experiment that could test the claim.

Answer Sketch

(a) Positive — it's a falsifiable causal claim about the world. (b) (i) Reverse causation — healthier people lobby for bike lanes. (ii) Omitted variable — wealthier cities have both more bike lanes AND lower obesity (income drives both). (iii) Self-selection — bike-friendly cities attract residents who already exercise. (c) Natural experiment or DiD — cities don't randomly get bike lanes (no true RCT), so identify a city that built bike lanes due to a policy quirk (mayoral election, federal grant) and DiD vs a similar city that didn't. (d) Compare obesity before/after a city installs a bike-lane network (treated) vs a similar city that didn't (control). E.g., Minneapolis vs. Indianapolis after 2010.

Gains from Trade · Handout Practice: Comparative Advantage — Pizzeria vs Salad

Set-up In 1 hour, Tony can make 8 pizzas OR 16 salads. In 1 hour, Anna can make 4 pizzas OR 12 salads.

Show your work for each part:

a

Who has absolute advantage in pizzas? In salads?

b

What is Tony's opportunity cost of one pizza? Of one salad?

c

What is Anna's opportunity cost of one pizza? Of one salad?

d

e

Answer Sketch

Set-up Original cocoa market: Demand: P = 30 − 0.05Q and Supply: P = 5 + 0.05Q (P in $/ton, Q in thousand tons).

Show your work for each part:

a

Find equilibrium P* and Q*.

b

Calculate consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus.

Calculate the change in total surplus caused by the shock.

Top 10 Mistakes to Avoid

1

Shift vs. movement. A price change ⇒ movement along the curve. Anything else ⇒ shift of the whole curve.

2

Tax legal payer ≠ economic burden. Whoever's more inelastic bears more of the tax — not the legal payer.

3

Forgetting |·| on PED. Demand elasticity is negative. Always take absolute value before comparing to 1.

4

OC excludes implicit costs. Time, forgone wages, forgone interest — these are opportunity costs.

5

CS/PS triangle errors. Base is along Q-axis. Height is the vertical distance between the curve and equilibrium price.

6

Sunk cost fallacy. Money already spent should not affect forward-looking decisions.

7

Sign of cross-price elasticity. Positive = substitutes; negative = complements. Easy to flip.

8

Budget line slope. Slope = −(price of X-axis good) / (price of Y-axis good). Watch which good is on which axis.

9

Equimarginal: compare MU/$, not MU. Shift toward the good with higher MU/$ until they equalize.

10

Efficiency ≠ fairness. A market can be 100% efficient and still leave many people worse off — that's equity, not efficiency.
