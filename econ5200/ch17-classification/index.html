1  •  Why Not Just Run OLS?

Two defects. One cosmetic, one structural — and the logit fixes only one of them.

The Linear Probability Model, on Real Data

Code the outcome 1/0 and regress it on the spread. A straight line does not stop at 0 or 1 — and this is not an edge case:

84 months

get a negative predicted probability of recession. Range: −0.067 to 0.242.

The ordinary result of fitting a line to a variable that takes two values — not a contrived example.

3 min. Run this live if you can. The objection is usually taught as a hypothetical and it is not one: 84 of 523 months, on Federal Reserve data, from the standard model. Note the sigmoid is already on the plot — do not explain it yet, just let them see that it never leaves the box.

The Second Defect, and the Fix

Structural, and you already know it. \(\operatorname{Var}(y) = p(1-p)\) depends on the mean, so LPM errors are heteroscedastic by construction — Ch 14's problem, arriving by design rather than by accident. HC3 patches that. Nothing patches a negative probability.

Keep the linear combination of features. Pass it through a function that maps the whole real line into \((0,1)\):

$$\hat{P}(Y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p)}}$$

In words. The inside is the linear predictor \(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) — exactly Ch 13's object. The sigmoid around it is S-shaped: very negative maps near 0, very positive near 1, and zero maps to exactly 0.5. It cannot leave \((0,1)\) — by construction, not by luck.

3 min. Draw the sigmoid by hand and mark where the linear predictor is minus three, zero, plus three. The heteroscedasticity point is a one-line callback and should be delivered as "you already know this one." Set up the poll by asking, casually, whether the logit has now fixed both defects.

Poll: Which Defect Did We Actually Fix?

The logit link replaces the straight line with a sigmoid. Which of the LPM's two defects does that repair?

A) Both — impossible predictions and heteroscedasticity
B) Only the impossible predictions; the variance still depends on the mean
C) Only the heteroscedasticity; probabilities can still leave \([0,1]\)
D) Neither — the logit is a cosmetic reparameterisation
1 min. Students click to vote on their devices; results appear on the next slide. Expect a large A share — that is the productive error, because "the logit fixes the LPM" is exactly how the result is usually summarised.

Answer: B — Only the Impossible Predictions

A Bernoulli outcome still has variance \(p(1-p)\), and that still varies with \(x\). The logit does not remove the heteroscedasticity — it absorbs it into the likelihood, which weights each observation by \(p_i(1-p_i)\). You will see those weights again in the Advanced Treatment.

Why A is tempting but wrong: the summary "the logit fixes the LPM" is true and imprecise. Only one defect is impossible afterwards; the other is handled, which is a different verb.

2  •  Odds, Log-Odds, Coefficients

Three scales, three interpretations — and confusing them is the commonest error in applied classification.

The Rearrangement That Explains Everything

Take the sigmoid and solve for the linear predictor:

$$\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p$$

In words. \(p/(1-p)\) are the odds — a probability of 0.2 is odds of 0.25, “one to four.” Its log is the logit.

Logistic regression is a linear model. It is linear in the log-odds and in nothing else — and that single fact governs every interpretation you will ever make of it.

So a coefficient is a change in log-odds, which nobody thinks in. Exponentiate it and you get something usable:

$$e^{\beta_j} = \text{the factor by which the ODDS multiply, per one-unit increase in } x_j$$
3 min. Do the algebra on the board — three lines — so that "linear in log-odds" is a derived fact rather than an assertion. Students who watch the derivation stop misreading coefficients.

On the Real Model

coefficient on spread: -0.8354   (p = 4.88e-08)
odds ratio: exp(-0.8354) = 0.434

A curve one percentage point steeper multiplies the odds of a recession within twelve months by 0.434 — a 57% reduction in odds.

That is the sentence you would say to a risk committee. Rehearse it.

Note what it is not. It is not a 57% reduction in probability, and it is not 83 percentage points of anything. Odds and probabilities coincide only when both are small.

3 min. Work it out loud: minus zero point eight three five four, exponentiate, zero point four three four, "a 57 percent reduction in odds." Then make the class state it in words before you do. The fragment is the misconception; land it here rather than letting them carry it to the poll.

The Marginal Effect Is Not Constant — and That Is a Feature

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j} = \beta_j \, p(1-p)$$

The multiplier \(p(1-p)\) is largest at \(p = 0.5\) and vanishes as \(p\) approaches 0 or 1.

Economically sensible. If a recession is already near-certain, more bad news barely changes the forecast. The model's diminishing sensitivity at the extremes matches how information actually works.

Practical consequence: report the marginal effect at a stated point — not a coefficient nobody can convert in their head.

3 min. Students find non-constant marginal effects unsettling until they hear the economic reading, which lands immediately. Contrast explicitly with Ch 12: one more room adds the same amount to predicted value everywhere; here it does not, and that is information rather than an annoyance.

The Interview Question They Use Deliberately

“A coefficient of −0.84 means the probability falls by 84%.”

No. The log-odds fall by 0.84 per unit, so the odds are multiplied by \(e^{-0.84} = 0.43\) — a 57% reduction in odds.

The effect on the probability is \(\beta\,p(1-p)\), which depends on where you start: about 21 percentage points at \(p = 0.5\), about 4 at \(p = 0.05\).

This is a standard screening question in data science interviews, and the wrong answer is given confidently often enough that interviewers use it on purpose.

2 min. Say the wrong sentence out loud in a confident voice first, then correct it. Students remember the correction better when they have heard the error delivered plausibly.

Why A is tempting but wrong: it is the single most common error in applied classification work, and it is wrong by a variable amount — \(\beta p(1-p)\) — not by a fixed one.

3  •  Fitting, and Reading the Output

No closed form — which costs you almost nothing, until it costs you everything.

Maximum Likelihood

OLS has a closed form. Logistic regression does not — there is no formula for \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\).

$$L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}, \qquad p_i = \hat{P}(Y=1 \mid x_i)$$

In words. Each observation contributes \(p_i\) if it was a 1 and \(1 - p_i\) if it was a 0. Maximising picks the coefficients under which the data we actually saw is most probable. In practice we maximise the log-likelihood: the product becomes a sum, and the problem becomes globally concave — a unique optimum, no local maxima, and any competent solver finds it in a fraction of a second.

So what does “no closed form” cost you? Almost nothing — except that convergence can fail, and failure is diagnostic. The usual causes: perfect separation (the coefficient runs to infinity), wildly different feature scales, or near-collinearity. A convergence warning is information about your design matrix, not a nuisance to suppress.

3 min. Students over-read "no closed form" as a limitation, so kill that immediately: two analysts running the same model get the same coefficients to many decimal places. The fragment is where the real content is. Separation returns in the Advanced Treatment with the reason the penalty fixes it.

Two Libraries, Two Purposes — and a Trap

# Inference — standard errors, z-statistics, p-values
logit = sm.Logit(y, X).fit()

# Prediction — the ML pipeline ecosystem
clf = LogisticRegression(penalty=None, solver='lbfgs').fit(X, y)

penalty=None is not optional if you want to match statsmodels. scikit-learn applies \(L_2\) regularization by default — sensible for prediction, startling for an econometrician who asked for maximum likelihood and got a penalised estimate without being told.

Worse: the strength parameter is C, which is the inverse of the penalty. Small C means heavy regularization. Ch 16 catalogued three conflicting uses of “alpha” across scikit-learn, glmnet and the econometrics literature. C is a fourth — running in the opposite direction to all of them.

3 min. This deserves the full slide: students will get different coefficients in the lab and assume they made an error. Say "penalty equals None" out loud twice. The four-conventions joke is the memory hook — and it is not a joke.

4  •  Why Accuracy Is the Wrong Score

This is the chapter. Accuracy is least informative exactly where classification matters most.

The Arithmetic Behind 93.1%

'always no recession':  accuracy 93.1%
logistic at p >= 0.5:   accuracy 93.1%
recessions caught:      0 of 36

Accuracy's floor is the base rate. Only 6.9% of months precede a recession, so “no” every time is right 93.1% of the time for free. With imbalanced classes that floor is high enough to disguise total uselessness.

0.5 is a threshold nobody chose. It is a library default inherited from a two-class problem with a 50% base rate. Here the predicted probability rarely climbs above 0.5, so the rule almost never fires.

Two defaults, neither examined, and between them they destroy the model.

3 min. Make them compute 487 over 523 in their heads before you show it. The two boxes are separate failures that happen to compound — keep them separate, because Section 5 fixes only the second one.

The information was entirely present. Accuracy destroyed it by collapsing a well-ordered set of probabilities into a yes/no call at an arbitrary cutoff.

The Formal Statement: Accuracy Is Not a Proper Score

A scoring rule is strictly proper if it is uniquely optimised by reporting your true beliefs. The Brier score and log-loss are; accuracy is not — shading a genuine 0.45 up to a confident 1 pays off whenever the outcome occurs, and accuracy cannot tell a calibrated 0.45 from an overconfident 0.95.

$$\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat{p}_i - y_i\right)^2$$

In words. Squared error, applied to probabilities. It rewards being both discriminating (separating the classes) and calibrated (when you say 30%, it happens 30% of the time). Accuracy rewards only landing on the right side of a cutoff.

The rule. Score classifiers with the Brier score or log-loss. Report accuracy as a summary for stakeholders, always next to the base rate — never as an objective to optimise.

The proof that Brier is strictly proper is two lines and it is in today's Formal Sketch.

3 min. Flag forward to the Formal Sketch explicitly — graduate students should not be asked to accept "strictly proper" as a label when the argument fits on one slide. If someone asks why anyone reports accuracy at all: because a stakeholder understands it, which is a communication reason and not an evaluation reason.

There is no statistical answer, because the question is not statistical.

3 min. Build this live if the room can take it. The inversion is visible in one column and it is the whole argument — do not paraphrase it, let them read down the accuracy column while the caught column climbs.

Accuracy Falls as the Model Becomes More Useful

Any procedure that tunes a threshold to maximise accuracy on imbalanced data will drive you to the top row — and a model that never fires.

So which row? It depends entirely on who is asking.

Central banker preparing contingency policy. Cheap false positive (wasted planning); expensive false negative (unprepared for a contraction). Wants the bottom row — 94 false alarms over forty years is a price worth paying.

Asset manager liquidating equity on the signal. Acting on a false alarm means missing a bull market — very expensive. Wants a much higher bar.

Same model, same probabilities, different thresholds — and both are correct.

3 min. Take a show of hands on which desk is doing statistics wrong BEFORE revealing the fragment. The model's job is to produce a well-calibrated probability; choosing what to do at each probability is a decision problem and it belongs to the decision-maker.

Derived, Not Guessed

If a false negative costs \(C_{FN}\) and a false positive costs \(C_{FP}\), expected cost is minimised at

$$t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

In words. The optimal threshold depends only on the ratio of the costs — not their levels, not the sample size, not the model. If missing a recession is nine times worse than a false alarm, \(t^* = 0.1\): the bottom row of the table, derived rather than guessed.

The asymmetry is usually large and usually unstated. In lending, a false negative costs the outstanding principal — thousands. A false positive costs the foregone margin on one loan — tens. Ratios of 50:1 or 100:1 are ordinary, implying thresholds around 0.01 to 0.02. Nowhere near 0.5.

The cost ratio is the modelling assumption; the threshold is just its arithmetic consequence. If the analyst does not choose it, the library has already chosen it at 0.5 — silently asserting the two errors cost the same.

3 min. Derive it at the board — it is two lines and it converts an apparently arbitrary choice into an economic one. This is the "Think Like an Economist" moment of the lecture: writing down what each error costs is a task economists are unusually well equipped for and most classification work skips entirely.

Discussion: Name the Cost Ratio

Two desks, one model, one set of probabilities, two different thresholds.

  1. Write the cost ratio each desk is implicitly asserting.
  2. The default 0.5 asserts what, exactly?
  3. Your risk committee refuses to name a dollar cost. What goes in the memo?

Debrief. (1) Central bank ≈ 1:9 or lower, so \(t^*\) near 0.10; asset manager perhaps 3:1, so \(t^*\) near 0.75. (2) That the two errors cost exactly the same — almost never true. (3) Report the whole threshold–cost curve and the break-even ratio at which the decision flips. You are not required to know the costs. You are required not to hide that you assumed them.

4 min. Part 3 is the professional skill and the one they will actually use: a committee that will not name costs will still recognise a break-even ratio when you put it in front of them. Timer key 3.

6  •  When the Feature Stops Meaning What It Meant

The part that makes this an economics chapter.

784 Days Inverted, and No Recession

The curve inverted from July 2022 to late 2024 — the longest inversion in modern history, though not the deepest. Real GDP grew 2.9% in 2023 and 2.8% in 2024.

2023-01  spread -1.16   P 31.1%
2023-05  spread -1.73   P 42.3%
2023-12  spread -1.42   P 36.1%
2024-06  spread -1.20   P 32.0%

Above 42% at the peak, sustained for two years, and wrong.

3 min. Let them sit with a model that was confidently and sustainedly wrong. Do not offer an explanation yet — ask what went wrong first, and let them propose overfitting, leakage, a bad threshold. Every tool from the last three chapters, and none of them is right. That realisation is the point of the section.

The Model Was Not Broken. The Feature Was.

Every Chapter 14 diagnostic passes. The likelihood was correctly maximised, the coefficient correctly estimated, the probabilities correctly computed from the coefficient.

A ten-year yield decomposes into expected future short rates plus a term premium — the extra compensation for holding duration risk. Years of quantitative easing compressed that premium: NY Fed estimates put it under 0.8 percentage points of a 4.77% ten-year yield. So the curve inverted partly because the premium had been squeezed toward zero and inflation expectations were normalising — not because markets expected a contraction.

The spread was numerically the same. What it indicated was not.

Not overfitting, not leakage, not miscalibration, not a bad threshold — the four failures the last three chapters taught you to look for. It is a structural break in the relationship between a feature and the outcome, and finding it requires knowing what a term premium is.

3 min. Deliver the fragment lines slowly. This is the chapter's argument for why economists belong in this work, and it deserves real weight: a model is a claim that a historical relationship will continue, and deciding whether it will is a question about the economy, not about the model.

How You Would Actually Catch It

Undetectable from performance metrics — but not undetectable. Three habits, and all three are economics rather than statistics.

1. Decompose the feature. The spread is a sum of components that move independently and mean different things. The Adrian–Crump–Moench decomposition existed long before the inversion; using it required someone to ask what the spread was made of.

2. Ask what mechanism the model encodes. “Inverted curve predicts recession” is a correlation. The mechanism is that markets expecting a downturn bid up long bonds in anticipation of rate cuts. Written that way the question becomes testable — and in 2022–24 it was substantially something else.

3. Track the regime, not just the residuals. Errors look fine until suddenly they do not, and by then you have been wrong for two years. Monitor whether the conditions that made the feature work still hold — checkable continuously, long before outcome data arrives to prove you wrong.

None of these is a metric you can compute from the fitted object. They require knowing why the model works — which a purely predictive workflow never has to establish.

3 min. Name the three as things they ALREADY know how to do, because they do. Continuous feature validation is the maintenance task separating a model in production from a model in a paper; the Fed has not discarded its model, and neither should they.

Advanced Treatment

Where the sigmoid comes from — and what the score equation says

The Logit Is Not an Arbitrary Squashing Function

It falls out of a behavioural model. Suppose an unobserved continuous index \(y_i^*\) — a firm's distress, a household's propensity to default, the net utility of an option:

$$y_i^* = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i, \qquad y_i = \mathbb{1}[y_i^* > 0]$$

We observe only the sign. Then

$$P(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = P(\varepsilon_i > -\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) = 1 - F(-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta})$$

\(\varepsilon\) standard logistic, \(F(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\)  →  exactly the sigmoid.

\(\varepsilon\) standard normal  →  probit.

The two differ only in the assumed error distribution and fit almost identically, because the logistic distribution is close to a normal with variance \(\pi^2/3\) — which is why logit coefficients run roughly 1.6–1.8× probit coefficients.

3 min. Graduate-only. The logit-versus-probit ratio is the sort of thing referees ask about and students cannot explain; the pi-squared-over-three variance is the whole reason. Ask them to predict the direction of the ratio before you state it.

Why This Is the Same Mathematics as Discrete Choice

McFadden (1974) derives exactly this structure from random utility maximisation. The latent index is the net utility of an alternative; the logistic error is a Type-I extreme value assumption on taste shocks.

An economist's “utility of alternative \(j\)” is a data scientist's “logit score for class \(j\)”. Same object, two literatures, different vocabulary.

Which is why the coefficients carry an interpretation about preferences and not merely about fit — and why a discrete-choice economist walks into a classification problem already knowing the model. The Nobel was for what the derivation licensed, not for the functional form.

It is also why the multinomial extension later in this deck is a small step rather than a new subject.

2 min. Graduate-only, and worth naming as the discipline's advantage: your ECON 5200 room can say where the functional form comes from, which the average bootcamp graduate cannot. Flag forward to the IIA slide.

The Score Equation, and Where the Heteroscedasticity Went

$$\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right], \qquad p_i = \frac{1}{1+e^{-\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}}}$$

Differentiate, using \(\partial p_i / \partial (\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta}) = p_i(1-p_i)\). The first-order conditions collapse to something remarkably clean:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{0}$$

The residuals are orthogonal to the regressors — structurally the same condition OLS satisfies, with \(y_i - p_i\) in place of \(y_i - \hat{y}_i\). There is no closed form only because \(p_i\) depends on \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) non-linearly, so the system is solved by Newton–Raphson — equivalently, iteratively reweighted least squares with weights \(p_i(1-p_i)\).

Those weights are the answer to Poll 1. The Bernoulli variance \(p(1-p)\) did not disappear — it became the weighting inside the estimator. The logit absorbs the heteroscedasticity rather than removing it.

4 min. Graduate-only and the intellectual centre of the Advanced Treatment. Close the loop with Poll 1 explicitly: students who chose A get a precise account of what they got wrong and why the intuition was almost right. The OLS parallel is worth pausing on — the score equation is the moment logistic regression stops feeling like a different technique.

A Caution: Separation

If some linear combination of features perfectly separates the classes, the likelihood is maximised by driving \(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\| \to \infty\). No finite MLE exists.

Software reports enormous coefficients with enormous standard errors, or fails to converge. It happens more often than expected with many binary regressors and few positive cases — which is to say, in exactly the rare-event settings this chapter is about.

The fix is Chapter 16's \(L_2\). The penalised log-likelihood is strictly concave, so the estimate is finite. This is one of the rare cases where regularization is not a bias–variance choice — it is the only way to obtain an answer at all.

And it is one reason scikit-learn regularises by default. The library's startling behaviour from Section 3 turns out to be defensible — it is just undocumented at the point of use.

3 min. Graduate-only. The payoff is the fragment: the penalty=None trap and the separation problem are the same design decision seen from two sides. A student who leaves able to explain WHY sklearn regularises by default has understood more than one who merely remembers to switch it off.

Formal Sketch

Proper scoring rules — and a decomposition that is not the one from Chapter 15

Brier Is Strictly Proper — in Three Lines

A scoring rule \(S(\hat{p}, y)\) is proper if expected score is optimised by reporting your true belief \(q\), and strictly proper if that optimum is unique. With true belief \(q\) and report \(\hat p\):

$$E[S] = q(\hat{p}-1)^2 + (1-q)\hat{p}^2$$

Differentiate with respect to \(\hat p\):

$$\frac{\partial E[S]}{\partial \hat{p}} = 2(\hat{p} - q), \qquad \frac{\partial^2 E[S]}{\partial \hat{p}^2} = 2 > 0$$

Zero uniquely at \(\hat{p} = q\), with a positive second derivative. Honesty is the unique optimum. The same argument runs for log-loss, \(-[y\log\hat p + (1-y)\log(1-\hat p)]\).

Gneiting & Raftery (2007), JASA 102(477).

2 min. Graduate-only. Do it at the board; it is genuinely three lines and it converts "strictly proper" from a label into a property they can verify. Then ask them to try the same differentiation on accuracy — which is the next slide, and which they will discover cannot be done.

And Why Accuracy Fails

Accuracy depends on \(\hat{p}\) only through \(\mathbb{1}[\hat{p} \geq t]\).

Any two forecasts on the same side of the threshold score identically. A genuine belief of 0.51 and a claimed certainty of 0.99 are indistinguishable — and whenever \(q > t\), reporting \(\hat p = 1\) is weakly optimal.

There is no unique reward for honesty, so accuracy is not strictly proper. Note the shape of the failure: it is not that accuracy is noisy or biased. It is that the objective does not identify the honest forecast — an entire interval of reports is optimal.

Which is why “optimise accuracy” is not merely a weak objective. It is an objective a strategic forecaster can game without lying detectably — and one an ordinary fitting procedure games accidentally.

2 min. Graduate-only. The identification framing is the one that lands with econometricians: it is a flat objective function, not a mismeasured one. If someone asks about F1 or balanced accuracy, say they are threshold-dependent summaries of the same confusion matrix and inherit the same defect — Ch 18.

The Murphy Decomposition — and What It Is Not

Murphy (1973) showed the Brier score decomposes as

$$\text{Brier} = \underbrace{\text{Reliability}}_{\text{calibration error}} - \underbrace{\text{Resolution}}_{\text{discrimination}} + \underbrace{\text{Uncertainty}}_{\text{base-rate variance}}$$

Diagnostically useful: it separates am I calibrated from do I discriminate from how hard is this problem. Uncertainty is \(\bar{y}(1-\bar{y})\) — here \(0.069 \times 0.931 = 0.0640\) — and depends on the base rate alone, so it is the irreducible term.

This is NOT the bias–variance decomposition, and it is not a classification version of one. It exists only because the Brier score is squared error — applied to probabilities. The clean additive bias–variance identity is a squared-error result and nothing more.

Extending it to 0–1 loss required separate work (Domingos 2000; James 2003), and those general-loss versions are not the same identity: the cross term does not vanish and can be negative, so bias and variance can offset. Never write “the bias–variance decomposition of the misclassification rate” — and do not write it of AUC either.

4 min. Graduate-only, and the reason this slide exists is that the Murphy decomposition looks superficially like the Ch 15 identity and is a different object. Say the three-part rule out loud: Brier decomposes because it is squared error; misclassification rate does not decompose additively; AUC is not a loss at all. This is a standard exam error and Ch 15 was corrected on exactly this point. Callback to Quick Check 1.

Going Deeper

Calibration, class imbalance, and why Basel III cares

Two Independent Properties

Calibration is the property that among cases assigned probability \(p\), a fraction \(p\) actually occur. Discrimination is whether the ranking is right. They are independent.

The proof is one line. Multiply every predicted probability by 0.5. The ranking is unchanged, so AUC is unchanged. Calibration is destroyed.

Ranking applicants by risk? Only discrimination matters. Calibration is optional.

Probability entering a formula — expected loss, expected revenue, a capital requirement? Calibration is everything.

A classifier can rank perfectly and still be useless, if the level of its probabilities is wrong. Which matters depends entirely on the use — and the use is a business fact, not a modelling one.

3 min. Graduate-only. The times-0.5 argument is the cleanest thing on this slide; make them see that AUC is completely blind to it. Then set up the next slide by asking what happens when someone multiplies a miscalibrated PD by a loan book.

Basel III Makes This a Capital Number

Under the Internal Ratings-Based approach, expected credit loss is

$$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$$

In words. Expected loss is the probability of default times loss-given-default times exposure-at-default — and the model's predicted probability is the \(PD\). It feeds directly into required regulatory capital.

A model whose PDs are systematically inflated forces the bank to hold capital against losses that will not occur — a direct and continuing cost, and invisible to any ranking metric.

Nobody in that chain reads an AUC. They read a number that came out of your model and was multiplied by a loan book.

3 min. Graduate-only, and the slide that answers "why should I care about calibration". This is a real cost on a real balance sheet, imposed by a model choice nobody in the room would have flagged in a review. Sets up the SMOTE slide, which is the same argument with a villain.

Which Is Why the Standard Imbalance “Fixes” Are Dangerous

SMOTE and naive over-sampling change the base rate the model is trained on. The resulting model estimates \(P(Y=1)\) under the resampled distribution, not the real one, and systematically overstates the rare event.

Simulation studies find these corrections damage calibration substantially while not reliably improving discrimination — the thing they were adopted to improve. And by the previous slide, an inflated \(PD\) is a capital cost.

Common misconception: “class imbalance is a problem to be fixed before modelling.” Imbalance is a property of the world, not a defect in the data. Rare events are rare. Resampling does not create information — it changes the prior the model learns, in exchange for a metric that looked better because it was the wrong metric.

Do this instead. Train on the true distribution. Use class_weight='balanced' if the loss needs rebalancing. Move the threshold, not the data — Section 5's cost-based cutoff does everything resampling was supposed to do, without corrupting the probabilities.

3 min. Graduate-only, and expect resistance — students have often been taught SMOTE as best practice. That is why the Basel slide comes first: the objection "it improved my F1" is answered by "and it cost the bank capital." Do not soften this; it is one of the most consequential habits they will carry into industry.

Repairing Calibration After the Fact

Post-hoc calibration fits a second, monotone model mapping raw scores to empirical frequencies on held-out data — Ch 15's leakage discipline, applied to the calibration step.

Platt scaling. A one-dimensional logistic regression on the scores. Parametric, stable, appropriate for small calibration sets.

Isotonic regression. A non-parametric monotone step function. More flexible; overfits badly below roughly a thousand calibration observations.

Both are monotone, so neither can change the ranking — AUC is essentially unmoved by construction. That is the diagnostic, not a disappointment. If AUC moves after calibration, something other than calibration happened.

Both available through CalibratedClassifierCV, and both require data not used for fitting. You will run both in Lab 17.

3 min. Graduate-only. The last box is the one to press: the invariance of AUC under monotone transformation is not a limitation of calibration, it is the definition of what calibration does. Warn them now that in the lab the Brier score may NOT improve on this particular data-generating process — see lab-defects.md — and that this is a property of the lab's simulation, not of their code.

More Than Two Categories

$$P(Y = j \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j}}{\sum_{m=1}^{J} e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_m}}$$

In words. Multinomial logit fits one linear predictor per category and normalises them into probabilities. Only differences between the \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_j\) are identified, so one category is fixed as the baseline and the rest are read relative to it — Ch 13's reference-category logic, now applied to the outcome.

Its famous weakness: Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives. The ratio of probabilities for any two options cannot depend on what else is available. Red bus / blue bus: a commuter split evenly between car and a red bus should not, on the introduction of an identical blue bus, end up choosing the car only a third of the time. But that is what the model predicts — it cannot know the two buses are near-substitutes. Nested and mixed logit relax it, at a cost in complexity.

For ordered outcomes — bond ratings, Likert responses — ordered logit exploits the ordering with a single coefficient vector and a set of cutpoints. More parsimonious, and usually right when the categories have a natural sequence.

The binary case carries every concept in today's lecture. Just do not assume the world is binary because the chapter was.

3 min. Graduate-only, and the contingency cut if you are running long — it is self-contained. IIA is the piece worth protecting: it is the standard exam question and the standard interview question, and the red-bus example makes it unforgettable in thirty seconds.

Putting It to Work

A six-step workflow, the lab, and the handoff

A Classification Workflow

1

Check the base rate before anything else. One line, and it determines how you must evaluate everything downstream.

2

Fit on the true distribution. Do not resample to balance. class_weight='balanced' reweights the objective without falsifying the prior.

3

Score with a proper rule. Brier or log-loss, plus a discrimination measure. Report accuracy only next to the base rate.

4

Choose the threshold from costs. \(t^* = C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\). The arithmetic is trivial; naming the costs is the hard part, and it is not a statistical task.

5

Check calibration if the probability is used as a number. If it only ranks, calibration is optional. If it feeds a formula, it is everything.

6

Ask whether the features still mean what they meant. The failure no metric detects. Schedule the question rather than waiting for the model to embarrass you.

3 min. Present as a checklist and lead with step 1, which is a habit rather than a technique and costs nothing. Step 2 is where the resistance is; you have already earned it with the Basel slide. Close on step 6 and hand off to Ch 20.
