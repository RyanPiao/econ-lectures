The Illusion of Infrastructure

You drive over a suspension bridge without wading into the water to check that the pylons reach bedrock. Infrastructure hands you a binary sense of security — it holds, or it doesn't. Economic data has no such bedrock. Its beams are best guesses, and some are missing entirely.

Every number you meet is what survives three subjective decisions:

1

What to measure. Which concept counts as “unemployed”?

2

How to measure it. Phone survey, admin record, scraped price?

3

What to ignore entirely. Who never got counted at all?

Decision 3 leaves no trace. Nothing in the file tells you what was thrown away before it was written — which is why treating the result as solid steel is the dangerous move.

Wayfair: Did the Ad Cause the Sofa?

Standard ML — an engine of correlation

Answer: B — Who Is Missing, Not How Many

Noise is an unsteady hand. Throw enough darts and the errors cancel — the average lands on the bullseye. That is what a bigger sample fixes.

Bias is someone having moved the board while you were blindfolded. Throw a million darts and every one still lands in the same wrong place.

The people who stopped answering were not a random fifth of the country. Large samples fix noise. They do nothing for bias.

Walk the two boards before you say a word of theory — point at the left cross sitting on the bullseye, then the right cross sitting nowhere near it. The sentence that does the work: "sample size cures an unsteady hand; it cannot move the board back." Debrief line to say verbatim: "Hold on to B. Three Data Structures

Three Ways of Observing the World

Photograph

Many subjects, one instant. 56 countries' Big Mac prices on 1 July 2024.

Can: compare across units at one instant

Cannot: show change, or forecast anything

Movie

One subject, many periods. US GDP, 1947 to today, quarter by quarter.

Can: trace deep history for one unit

Cannot: compare against any peer

Surveillance grid

Same 56 countries, every six months, for 25 years.

Can: do both dimensions at once

Cannot: be gathered cheaply or quickly

The structure you have determines the questions you are allowed to ask. A photograph compares but cannot show change. A movie shows change but cannot compare. Only the grid does both.

Draw the 2x2 on the board now — units on one axis, time on the other. Leave it up all session; you point at it during PPP and again during missing data.

Validity depends entirely on the structure: the same units, observed repeatedly, over the same periods.

Without panel structure you cannot separate the effect of the change from the effect of the season, the user, or the day of week. Decisions worth hundreds of millions ride on that distinction.

A Misconception Worth Killing Early

“More data always means better analysis.”

A million rows of cross-section cannot answer a question that requires time variation. Adding countries to a single-period snapshot does not help you measure how exchange rates change.

Structure beats size. A clean panel of 56 countries over 25 years is more powerful than a cross-section of 10,000 countries at one moment — because only the panel supports identification strategies that account for unobserved heterogeneity.

Someone will push back — "big data" is culturally sticky. Ask them what regression they would run on the 10,000-country cross-section to estimate the effect of a policy adopted in 2015. Let them find the wall themselves. The phrase to land it: volume without structure is dead weight — and the trade has a name, identification versus external validity. Choosing the 10,000-country snapshot trades away the ability to identify a cause in order to buy broader coverage.

Nobody said “take whichever is bigger” — and that is the whole point.

What you bought

Time variation

A Burger in Two Currencies

United States

$5.79

Switzerland

CHF 7.20

If goods markets worked perfectly — no tariffs, no transport, no local wage differences — one identical good should cost the same everywhere. So what should the exchange rate be?

The rate that equalises them: 7.20 ÷ 5.79 = 1.24

The market rate was about 0.90 francs per dollar. The franc buys more burger than it “should” — it is overvalued.

The Economist Big Mac Index, January 2025.

Do It By Hand First — Switzerland, January 2025

\[ \text{Implied PPP} = \frac{P_{\text{domestic}}}{P_{\text{US}}} \]

\[ \text{Valuation}\,(\%) = \frac{\text{Implied} - \text{Spot}}{\text{Spot}} \times 100 \]

The first asks what rate would make the two burgers cost the same? The second asks how far is the real world from that rate?

Sign convention: positive means the local currency is overvalued — the burger is dear relative to what the exchange rate implies.

Numbers before abstraction. Walk the left-hand flow by hand before you point at either equation — students should watch 7.20 over 5.79 become 1.24, then watch 1.24 measured against 0.90 become +38 per cent. The equations on the right are the same two steps written generally; say the sign convention out loud. This slide replaces the old separate "Now Generalise" slide.

Now Generalise

$$\text{Implied PPP} = \frac{P_{\text{domestic}}}{P_{\text{US}}}$$

$$\text{Valuation \%} = \frac{\text{Implied PPP} - \text{Spot}}{\text{Spot}} \times 100$$

Say the sign convention out loud. Positive means the local currency is overvalued: the burger is expensive relative to what the exchange rate implies. Negative means undervalued.

Key Concept: Tidy Data

A dataset is tidy when: (1) each variable is a column, (2) each observation is a row, and (3) each type of observational unit is its own table. (Wickham 2014)

Statistical agencies publish wide: one column per year since 1920, nested headers, merged cells, footnotes inside data cells. It reads well and scripts on it fail.

Tidy is not an aesthetic preference — it is the shape every modern tool is optimised for. If your data is not tidy, reshaping it is your first, unglamorous job.

1.5 min. Wickham's paper is short and readable; recommend it to anyone who asks for extra reading in week one. This concept is load-bearing for Topics 2-4.

Selection: .loc vs .iloc

# .loc  — LABEL-based: select by name, or by boolean condition
swiss = latest.loc[latest["name"] == "Switzerland", "dollar_price"]

# .iloc — POSITION-based: select by integer position
latest.iloc[:3, :5]

# boolean filtering: which burgers cost more than $7?
expensive = latest.loc[latest["dollar_price"] > 7, ["name", "dollar_price"]]

The rule to memorise: .loc takes labels. .iloc takes positions.

Almost every pandas error a beginner hits is one of these used where the other belonged.

The Whole Index at a Glance

Before You Trade On It

“The Big Mac Index proves currencies are misvalued.”

It illustrates PPP logic; it does not price currencies. A Big Mac is mostly non-tradeable inputs — rent, wages, utilities, tax.

Swiss burgers are expensive because Switzerland is expensive, not because the franc is 38% too strong. Use the index to reason about price levels. Do not use it to place a trade — the number you would be trading on is mostly rent.

Pakko & Pollard (2003), "Burgernomics: A Big Mac Guide to PPP," FRB St. Louis.

Rubin (1976): Three Mechanisms

MCAR — Missing Completely at Random

Unrelated to anything, observed or not. A server crashed. Benign — you lose power, not unbiasedness.

MAR — Missing at Random

Depends on observed variables. Younger respondents skip the salary question, but within an age group it is unrelated to salary. Correctable.

MNAR — Missing Not at Random

Depends on the unobserved value itself. High earners refuse because they are high earners. Dangerous — no amount of observed data fully fixes it.

The names mislead, so read them carefully. Only MCAR is random full stop. MAR does not mean the missingness is random — it means random once you condition on what you did observe. And MNAR is not “more random”: the Not attaches to at Random, so the missingness depends on the very value you are missing.

Spell the three names out loud — the letters are doing real work and students conflate them for the rest of the course. The trap is MAR: "at random" sounds unconditional, and it is not. Say it as "random given what I already know". A line that lands: MCAR is the survey van crashing; MAR is young people skipping the salary box; MNAR is the rich skipping it because they are rich. Poll 3 immediately tests whether they can tell MAR from MNAR, so do not rush this.

Poll 3: Diagnose the Mechanism

Russia's rows in the Big Mac Index stop in early 2022, after the invasion and the sale of McDonald's Russian restaurants.

Which mechanism is this?

A) MCAR — a collection accident
B) MAR — the war is observable
C) MNAR — depends on the unobserved values
D) Not missing — Russia left the sample
Students click to vote on their devices. Results render live on the next slide.

Answer: C — MNAR

MAR requires that conditional on what you observe, missingness is unrelated to the missing value. Russia's post-2022 burger prices and exchange rates were driven by sanctions — the same force that removed them from the data. Conditioning on “there was a war” does not recover them.

Why B is tempting. The war is observable, so it feels like something you can control for. But the mechanism runs through the unobserved values — and you cannot condition your way out of that.

D is not silly. If your estimand is “countries where McDonald's operates”, Russia left the population rather than going missing from it. Change the question and you change the diagnosis.

Expect about half the room on B — it is the seductive trap of controlling for an observable event, and it is worth thirty seconds. Give D an explicit nod: whether this is missing data at all depends on the estimand, and a student who says so is reasoning well, not dodging. Then hand them the decision tree on the next slide as the tool that settles it.

Two Questions, Three Verdicts

Classify These Three

A country's early rows are absent because The Economist only began surveying it in 2011. → MCAR with respect to valuation — the start date has nothing to do with the price.

Venezuela's gaps during hyperinflation, when a dollar price was not meaningfully measurable. → MNAR — the gap is caused by the extreme value it would have held.

Russia, after February 2022. → MNAR — the cause of the gap is the thing the data measures.

You can never see the missing values. You can always reason about how the data came to be — and that reasoning is the job.

Two Results Worth Carrying Forward

Heckman (1979). Sample selection on unobservables is mathematically equivalent to omitted variable bias. We observe wages only for people who work; if working correlates with potential wages, the observed average is biased upward. The two-step correction models the selection explicitly and adds the inverse Mills ratio as a regressor.

Survivorship bias. Anthropic's AI Economic Index (March 2026) found high-tenure users were disproportionately successful at complex tasks — but new users who got no value abandoned the tool and vanished from the panel. Same mechanism as mutual fund returns and clinical trial dropout.

Missing data is not noise to be cleaned away. It is a structural feature to be modelled.

Three Strategies, in Order of Intellectual Honesty

1

Flag and document (preferred). Record which observations are missing and why; carry an is_missing column. The gap carries information — do not erase it.

2

Forward-fill (cautious). Carry the last observed value forward. Defensible for slow-moving series like annual GDP. Never for exchange rates or prices.

3

Drop and document (simplest). Acceptable only if you can demonstrate MCAR — which requires testing, not assertion.

The fourth thing people actually do, which is not on the list: impute silently. It assumes MAR. Under MNAR it increases bias — and deletes the one visible clue that anything was wrong.

This is the answer to "so what do I actually do?" — the section until now has been pure diagnosis. Land strategy 1 hardest: flagging is what makes the analysis auditable by the next person. The arithmetic is the whole point of the figure: mean-filling the missing high earner writes the mean back in, so the computed average equals the imputed value by construction, and the sheet stops looking broken. Lab Part 2 asks them to judge exactly this in someone else's code.

Reproducibility Is Not a Luxury

Whenever a computation involves randomness — sampling, train/test splits, bootstrapping — set random_state=42.

Without it the generator starts somewhere new on every run, so the same script gives you one answer and your manager another. The seed makes the randomness deterministic — stable and auditable on any machine.

Brodeur, Mikola & Cook (2024) tried to reproduce 110 papers from leading economics and political science journals across 5,511 re-analyses:

A robustness check asks whether the headline survives a small, arbitrary change. If dropping Rhode Island makes the finding vanish, the finding was never there.

Half the time the re-run effect was smaller than the abstract claimed.

The gap between “the code runs” and “the conclusions hold” is wide. That gap is your job.

2 min. Land the last line and stop. On the seed: the point is not the number 42, it is that an unseeded run is unverifiable — your manager re-runs your script on Tuesday and gets different numbers from identical code, and neither of you can say who is right. This is also the standard the labs are graded against — a notebook that runs is the floor, not the deliverable.
