The Part That Should Unsettle You

The model scored well on held-out data by the standards its builders used. Two things it could not see. The market shifted structurally — that is Ch 20. And more subtly:

The model's errors were symmetric. The market's response to them was not.

Overvalue a house → the owner accepts.
The error becomes inventory.

Undervalue it → the owner walks away.
The error is corrected by the seller.

A model with unbiased errors, facing a mechanism that keeps only the expensive ones, loses money on average — and no validation score in the world reveals that, because the validation set does not get to decline your offer.

The Same Model, Two Verdicts

A city housing authority regresses rent on square footage, bedrooms, neighbourhood, building age, and distance to transit. It asks two questions of the identical fit.

"How much does a bedroom add?"

Ch 13's question. Needs the adjustment set right (Ch 10) and the standard error honest (Ch 14). Whether it predicts well is almost beside the point.

"What should this vacant unit rent for?"

The coefficients are irrelevant. If a Random Forest — which has none — predicts better, that is strictly better. Nobody asks a forest what a bedroom is worth.

Adding neighbourhood fixed effects sharpens the bedroom coefficient by absorbing confounding, and may wreck prediction by adding hundreds of parameters estimated on few observations each. You cannot optimise both.

Decide which question you are answering before you fit anything.

Common Misconception

"More variables make a model better."

This is the econometrician's instinct, and it is correct for the job econometrics does. For prediction it is wrong more often than right.

Each added variable buys flexibility, and flexibility is paid for in variance. Once the model can already represent the signal, further variables fit noise — improving in-sample \(R^2\), which cannot decrease, while degrading out-of-sample accuracy.

Ch 13 showed twenty columns of pure noise raising \(R^2\). Today you see what that cost.

The Decomposition

Three distinct failures, and only two of them are yours

Expected Prediction Error at a New Point

Truth: \(y = f(x) + \varepsilon\), with \(E[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\). Fit \(\hat f\) on a training sample; average over all the training sets you might have drawn.

$$\text{EPE}(x_0) = \underbrace{\big(E[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{E\big[(\hat f(x_0) - E[\hat f(x_0)])^2\big]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible}}$$

Bias — systematically wrong

Too rigid to represent the truth. It would miss even with infinite data.

Variance — unstable

Swings depending on which particular sample it happened to see.

Irreducible — the world

The noise in \(y\) itself. No model reduces it. Ever.

Notation: \(E[\hat f(x_0)]\) is the average prediction across all possible training samples — not something you can compute from your one dataset. Which is why we simulate.

3 min.

Read the equation left to right and say the plain-language version of each term before moving on.

  • The key conceptual move is that bias and variance are both defined by averaging over training sets you did not draw — they are properties of a procedure, not of your fit.

What That Demo Proves — and What It Does Not

The chapter's code prints bias² + variance + σ² beside actual EPE and they agree to four decimals at every degree. It is tempting to read that as empirical confirmation of the decomposition. It is not.

$$\overline{(\bar P - f)^2} \;+\; \overline{\operatorname{Var}(P)} \;=\; \overline{(P - f)^2}$$

Both sides are built from the same prediction array and the same hand-supplied \(\sigma^2\), and the equality above is an algebraic identity that holds for any array whatsoever.

6.9 × 10−18

Same check on random junk arrays:

So the demo confirms the algebra, and confirms that the simulation implements the algebra. It does not independently measure EPE. To do that you have to go and get new data.

Advanced Treatment

Deriving the decomposition — and reading off what it does not cover

Deriving It

Let \(y_0 = f(x_0) + \varepsilon\), with \(\varepsilon\) independent of the training sample. Write \(\hat f \equiv \hat f(x_0)\) and \(\bar f \equiv E[\hat f(x_0)]\), the expectation taken over training samples.

$$\text{EPE}(x_0) = E\big[(y_0-\hat f)^2\big] = E\big[(f + \varepsilon - \hat f)^2\big]$$

Expand, and use independence of \(\varepsilon\) from \(\hat f\) to kill the cross term:

$$= E[\varepsilon^2] + E\big[(f-\hat f)^2\big] = \sigma^2 + E\big[(f-\hat f)^2\big]$$

Now add and subtract \(\bar f\) inside the remaining expectation:

$$E\big[(f-\bar f + \bar f-\hat f)^2\big] = (f-\bar f)^2 + E\big[(\bar f-\hat f)^2\big] + 2(f-\bar f)\,\underbrace{E[\bar f - \hat f]}_{= \; 0}$$

The last term vanishes because \(E[\hat f] = \bar f\) by definition. Hence:

$$\text{EPE}(x_0) = \underbrace{(f(x_0)-\bar f)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{E[(\hat f-\bar f)^2]}_{\text{Variance}} + \sigma^2$$

4 min.

Two lines do all the work: independence kills the epsilon cross term, and the definition of \bar f kills the second cross term.

  • Say that out loud — if a student can name which assumption retires which term, they own the derivation.
  • Note that NOTHING here required normality, or linearity, or a correctly specified model.

Three Things the Derivation Tells You

1  It is pointwise.

It holds at each \(x_0\); the aggregate version integrates over the distribution of \(x\). So a model can be bias-dominated in one region and variance-dominated in another, and the aggregate hides that. Look at the degree-15 panel again: fine in the middle, wild at the edges.

2  It is specific to squared error.

Extending it to 0–1 loss or general losses is genuinely non-trivial and required separate work (Domingos 2000; James 2003). Classification gets no clean analogue — which is why Ch 17–18 change the machinery rather than reuse it. If you ever see "the bias-variance decomposition of the misclassification rate" stated as an identity, it is not one.

3  \(\sigma^2\) enters additively and unconditionally.

It does not interact with the model. That is the formal content of the word "irreducible": no choice you make about \(\hat f\) can move it.

4 min.

Point 2 is the one to press. A very common exam error is applying the decomposition to accuracy or AUC as if it were an identity.

  • It is not, and saying so now saves grief in Ch 18.
  • If asked what the general-loss version looks like, say: Domingos gives a unified definition where the "cross term" does not vanish and can be negative — bias and variance can offset.

Three Consequences Students Under-Absorb

A model can be bad without overfitting.

Degree 1 is genuinely poor with almost no gap. Its problem is bias, and the remedy is more flexibility, not less. "Underperforming" and "overfitting" are different diagnoses with opposite treatments, and the gap tells you which you have.

The best model is rarely the most flexible one you can afford.

Degree 15 fits the training data best of anything in the table and is the second-worst choice on new data. A procedure that rewards training fit will reliably choose the worst available model — which disqualifies in-sample \(R^2\), the statistic most economists reach for first.

Degrees 5 and 7 are indistinguishable on this split.

0.3648 vs 0.3650 — two ten-thousandths, on 48 test observations. Treating that as a ranking is reading noise. When two models are this close, prefer the simpler one: not from aesthetics, but because it has less variance and the comparison lacks the resolution to justify anything else.

\(k\)-Fold Cross-Validation

Partition the data into \(k\) folds. Fit on \(k-1\), evaluate on the held-out one, rotate through all \(k\), average.

$$CV(k) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} \text{MSE}_j$$

Where \(\text{MSE}_j\) is the error on fold \(j\) from a model fitted on the other \(k-1\) folds.

Plain reading: every observation is used for validation exactly once and for training \(k-1\) times, so the estimate does not hinge on one lucky partition. You are averaging \(k\) draws from the distribution you just saw in the histogram.

A single split samples the distribution of performance. Cross-validation estimates it.

2 min.

Introduce CV as the fix rather than as a definition, so it arrives as a solution to a problem they have just felt.

The averaging is what makes model selection reliable rather than lucky.

Formal Sketch

Why cross-validation is itself biased — and why \(k = 5\) or \(10\)

A Tradeoff Inside the Estimator Itself

Small \(k\) is biased. \(CV(k)\) estimates the error of a model trained on \(n(k-1)/k\) observations, not on \(n\). Learning curves decrease in sample size, so \(CV(k)\) is upward-biased for the final model fitted on everything — and the bias grows as \(k\) shrinks. Two-fold CV trains on half the data and can be badly pessimistic.

That argues for large \(k\), and LOOCV (\(k = n\)) minimises the bias. But LOOCV has a variance problem, and the mechanism is the point:

Its \(n\) training sets differ by one observation each, so the fitted models are almost perfectly correlated. Averaging \(B\) quantities with pairwise correlation \(\rho\) gives

$$\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$$

The second term vanishes with \(B\). The first does not. Averaging highly correlated things barely reduces variance.

Sketch of the result. Bias falls roughly as \(1/k\) while the correlation between fold-models rises toward one, so total error in the CV estimate is minimised at moderate \(k\). The flat region sits around \(k = 5\) to \(10\) — conventional rather than arbitrary. (ESL ch. 7.10)

4 min.

Graduate-only. Flag hard that the rho-sigma-squared expression returns verbatim in Ch 19: Random Forests need DECORRELATED trees, not merely more trees, for exactly this reason.

  • Students who own this formula today get Ch 19's central argument for free.
  • Note the pleasing recursion: choosing k is itself a bias-variance tradeoff, in the estimator of the thing that measures bias-variance tradeoffs.

One Special Case, and a Ch 14 Callback

For linear models under squared loss, LOOCV has a closed form requiring a single fit:

$$CV_{(n)} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{y_i - \hat y_i}{1 - h_{ii}}\right)^2$$

Where \(h_{ii}\) is the leverage of observation \(i\) — the same hat-matrix diagonal you used in Ch 14 to build HC3 standard errors.

Plain reading: deleting observation \(i\) and refitting would have moved the fit toward \(y_i\) by an amount governed by how much leverage \(i\) had. The formula corrects each residual for that pull instead of doing the refit. High-leverage points get inflated most — which is right, because they are the ones the model was bending to accommodate.

\(n\) model fits become one. The same quantity doing unrelated work in two chapters is usually a sign you have found something structural rather than a trick.

Graduate-only. If time is short this is the first slide to cut — but it is a satisfying callback and it takes ninety seconds.

  • Ask whether the formula can be applied to Ridge; the answer is yes, with the ridge hat matrix, which is a nice preview of Ch 16's effective degrees of freedom.

When the held-out data was not held out, every number above becomes decoration

Preprocessing Leakage

Scale or impute before splitting and the training data has been transformed using the test set's mean and variance. The model has seen a summary of data it should not know exists.

The effect is usually small with simple scaling on a large sample — which is precisely why it survives review. It becomes serious with feature selection, imputation or target encoding: choosing the twenty best predictors by correlation with \(y\) on the full dataset lets the test set vote on which features the model gets.

# WRONG — the scaler has already seen the test set
X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X_scaled, y)

# RIGHT — the scaler is refitted inside every fold
pipe = Pipeline([('scaler', StandardScaler()), ('model', Ridge())])
scores = cross_val_score(pipe, X_train, y_train, cv=5)

The fix is structural rather than disciplinary: a Pipeline makes the mistake impossible, not merely discouraged.

3 min.

In the extreme, selecting features on pure noise before splitting produces a model that validates well and predicts nothing — worth stating, because it is the version that shows up in student projects.

  • Manual scaling followed by cross_val_score is a bug that produces optimistic numbers and no error message.

Temporal Leakage

Randomly shuffling time-ordered data puts the future in the training set. The model learns from information it could not have had, and its measured accuracy is a forecast of the past.

A persistent series — a slow-moving level plus noise, which describes most macroeconomic data — predicted from three lags, shuffled 5-fold versus TimeSeriesSplit:

model shuffled (leaky) time-respecting inflated by
RandomForest (200 trees)1.8112.15016%
OLS (3 lags)1.5251.6296%

Both look better under shuffling — that is leakage doing what leakage does. The point is how much better. And notice the other thing in the table: OLS on three lags beats the 200-tree forest under both schemes.

3 min.

Do not stop at "shuffling inflates scores." The asymmetry is the finding, and the next slide asks whether one seed is enough to establish it.

Flag the OLS result too: on noisy, persistent, low-dimensional economic data a rigid functional form is frequently the better predictor, and that should temper expectations before Ch 16-19.

Is That One Lucky Seed?

One realisation is not a finding. Replicate the whole experiment over 40 independent draws of the DGP:

Random Forest inflated 44.5% ± 3.3
OLS inflated 11.6% ± 1.7
difference +32.9 pp ± 3.2

RF is inflated more than OLS in 95% of draws. The asymmetry is real, roughly three- to four-fold — and the chapter's single seed understates it.

Look at the scatter: individual draws range from 11% to 84% for the forest. Any one of them, reported alone, would have been a different headline.

3 min.

This is the deck's own demonstration of the discipline it preached earlier: a gap gets reported with its standard error, and a claim gets replicated before it is believed.

  • The 11-to-84 percent range is the punchline — it is why you cannot publish one seed.

Why the Asymmetry Is the Whole Mechanism

A flexible model has the capacity to exploit leaked information. A rigid one does not.

So leakage does not merely inflate scores. It inflates them differentially — manufacturing an apparent advantage for exactly the models that would otherwise be penalised for their variance.

Any comparison run on leaky splits is systematically biased toward complexity. Not noisy. Biased, with a known direction.

Which means the fix is not "be careful." If you are comparing a linear model against a boosted one and your split design is even slightly leaky, you have already decided the comparison before running it.

2 min.

Make sure the word "biased" lands with its technical weight: this is not sampling noise that averages out with more studies.

  • It has a direction, and the direction favours the fashionable answer.
  • That sets up Kapoor and Narayanan.

Grouped Leakage — The Subtlest

If a dataset contains repeated observations of the same unit — several trips by one driver, several quarters for one firm, several students in one school — a random split puts the same unit on both sides.

The model then learns to recognise that unit rather than the general pattern, and its validation score reflects an ability it will not have on genuinely new units.

The fix is GroupKFold, which keeps every observation of a group on one side of the split. Split along the dimension you want to generalise across.

In Ch 14's language: repeated measures from one unit are a panel, and pooling them violates the independence that standard CV assumes. Clustering did not disappear because the model became non-parametric.

3 min.

Lead the leakage section with THIS one rather than preprocessing — it is the one they have never considered and the one their own panel data will inflict on them.

  • The Ch 14 connection is what makes it stick for economists.

Economist at Work — The Driver In Both Sets

Cambridge Mobile Telematics (Cambridge, Massachusetts) processes sensor data from over 55 million drivers to assess driving risk. Their models predict hard braking, cornering and distraction from phone accelerometer and GPS traces, and insurers price policies on the output.

The obvious validation. Pool every trip, split randomly. A single driver contributes hundreds of trips over the same commute, the same roads, the same car, with the same habits — and appears on both sides.

The fix. Grouped cross-validation: every trip from a given driver confined to a single fold, so performance is measured on drivers the model has never seen.

The model does not have to learn the physics of risky driving. It can learn this person's commute and score brilliantly on held-out trips from a driver it has effectively memorised.

Which is not the question an insurer entering a new market is asking.

2 min.

The general lesson transfers to every panel dataset in this book.

  • If your rows are not independent, your random split is not measuring what you think it is.
  • Ask the room what the analogous unit is in their own project data — firm, county, student, household — and make them name it out loud.

B) Grouped leakage — which inflates the flexible model more

1 min.

Click to vote. Expect a real A share — a 12-point gap feels too big to be an artifact, which is exactly the intuition the differential-inflation result should have destroyed.

Answer: B — And Note Why It Is "Most Likely"

A is not impossible — and that is the trap. The evaluation as designed cannot distinguish A from B, so the honest answer is not "your model is worse", it is "your comparison carries no information."

What makes B the most likely rather than merely possible is the replication: leakage inflates the flexible model roughly three to four times as much, so a leaky design manufactures precisely this pattern. Ask for a GroupKFold re-run before you ask for anything else.

2 min.

The professional skill here is refusing a comparison rather than losing it.

  • "Your evaluation cannot support that claim" is a sentence they will need in industry, and it is stronger than arguing about model classes.

The Scale of It — and What Follows

329

17

Kapoor & Narayanan (2023) found leakage pervasive across ML-based science. In field after field — civil war prediction, health forecasting — complex models had reportedly crushed logistic regression.

Repartition the data so the future cannot inform the past and the advantage largely vanished: decades-old linear models performed comparably to the neural networks that had supposedly superseded them.

It is not that the sophisticated models were bad. The evaluation was wrong, in a way that systematically favoured flexibility.

The simplicity finding. On noisy, tabular, moderately sized economic data, simple models often win. The decomposition says why: a large irreducible component, a modest number of informative features, and \(n\) in the thousands puts the minimum of the U-curve at low complexity. Fit the simple model first, and make the complex one earn its place on held-out data.

The Outcomes You Never Observe

One failure of held-out evaluation that no splitting scheme fixes

Selective Labels

Return to the hook. Zillow's model was evaluated on houses — but which houses ended up in Zillow's inventory, the set the model was ultimately judged on, was determined by sellers deciding whether to accept the offer.

You observe the outcome only for the units where some decision went one way:

A bank observes repayment only for applicants it approved.

A court observes reoffending only for defendants released.

A hiring team observes performance only for candidates hired.

A platform observes conversion only for users shown the offer.

The training data is a non-random sample selected on the very outcome you are modelling — and the selection was performed by a decision-maker, often a previous version of your own model.

3 min.

Start with the bank example — everyone sees it immediately — then Zillow, then bail.

  • The defaults of rejected applicants are unobservable forever; that word "forever" is worth saying, because it distinguishes this from a data-collection problem you could fix with budget.

Why This Defeats Everything Above — and What Actually Helps

Kleinberg, Lakkaraju, Leskovec, Ludwig & Mullainathan (2018) set this out in the bail setting. An algorithm predicting reoffending can only be trained on defendants judges chose to release. If judges used information the data does not contain, the released population is favourably selected on unobservables — and the algorithm's apparent accuracy says little about the defendants judges detained. Which is exactly the population a proposal to replace judges would apply to.

Your split can be immaculate. Your Pipeline airtight, your GroupKFold correct, your test set untouched. And your validation score still answers a question about a population that was chosen for you, by a process that correlates with the outcome.

The remedies are not statistical hygiene. They are research design — and they are Chapters 9 to 13.

Randomise the decision

Approve some by lottery and their outcomes are unselected. Ch 9 — it is why encouragement designs exist.

Find variation in the decider

Lenient judges' marginal releases give outcomes for people a stricter judge would detain. Ch 12 — this is what judge leniency is for.

Bound what you cannot see

State what would have to be true of the unobserved outcomes to reverse you. Ch 10 E-values, Ch 11 Honest DiD, Ch 12 bounds.

  • That is destabilising, and it is the right reaction.
  • Close by naming the three remedies as things they ALREADY know — because they do.

Going Deeper

Double descent — and what it does not overturn

The U-Curve Is the Classical Picture, and It Is Incomplete

Belkin et al. (2019) documented double descent. Increase capacity past the point where the model can interpolate the training data exactly — parameters equal to observations, the interpolation threshold — and test error, having risen to a peak there, begins falling again. Modern overparameterised models often live far to the right of that threshold, fitting training data perfectly while generalising well.

This does not refute the decomposition, which is an identity and cannot be refuted. What it refutes is the assumption that variance rises monotonically with parameter count.

Past the threshold, among the infinitely many parameter settings that fit the data exactly, gradient descent reliably finds low-norm ones — an implicit regularisation that keeps effective complexity far below nominal parameter count. The noise gets distributed thinly across many directions rather than concentrated in a few wild ones.

Which is why "number of parameters" was never the right x-axis. Our own degree axis was a proxy for effective complexity, and it happened to be a good one because unregularised least squares gives you no implicit shrinkage at all.

3 min.

Graduate-only. The distinction between an identity and an empirical regularity is the load-bearing idea: the decomposition survives, the folk assumption about variance does not.

  • If someone asks whether this means "just use huge models," the answer is the next slide.

Does It Happen on Economic Data?

The natural question for economists: is this an images-and-language phenomenon, or does it reach tabular macro data?

Carriero et al. (2026) report double descent in standard macroeconomic panels (FRED-MD / FRED-QD), attributing it to their factor structure: a few strong principal components carry the signal, the residual spectrum is comparatively flat, and an interpolating estimator spreads absorbed noise across many weak directions.

Two cautions for practice.

It is a statement about a regime. The peak at the interpolation threshold is real and dangerous: a model with parameters near \(n\) is the worst place to be.

It does not license abandoning validation. It means the U-curve may not be the whole shape — so look at the actual curve rather than assuming either story.

Belkin, M. et al. (2019), PNAS 116(32), 15849–15854. Carriero, A. et al. (2026), arXiv:2605.15358.

  • If you keep it, the sentence that matters is the last one: look at the curve.
  • Both the classical story and the double-descent story are assumptions until you plot it.

In Practice

The chapter has five ideas. Here is the order to apply them in.

The Checklist Is the Deliverable

1

Split before you look. Hold out a test set as the first operation — before exploration, scaling, or feature engineering. Choosing a functional form by eye from a scatter plot of the full data is model selection on the test set.

2

Put every transformation inside a Pipeline. Not a style preference — the mechanism that makes preprocessing leakage structurally impossible.

3

Choose complexity by cross-validation, on the training set only. Five or ten folds. Never on the test set, never on training error.

4

Ask whether your rows are independent. Repeated units → GroupKFold. Ordered in time → do not shuffle at all. Random \(k\)-fold is the correct default only for genuinely independent observations — which economic data frequently is not.

5

Touch the test set once. Report that number. If you then change the model, the honest move is to say so rather than quietly re-evaluate.

6

Ask what the prediction will be used for. Squared error on held-out data is the right target only if errors are symmetric, all observations matter equally, and nobody responds to your predictions. Zillow failed on the third with every other box ticked.

Last Misconception

"Cross-validation prevents overfitting."

It detects overfitting. It does not prevent it. Cross-validation is a measurement device: it tells you which complexity generalises best among those you tried. If every candidate is too flexible, CV faithfully reports the least-bad one.

And CV itself can be overfit. Try enough model configurations against the same folds and you will eventually find one that suits those particular partitions — which is Ch 8's multiple-comparisons problem again, and the reason the final test set exists.

You saw both failure modes today: the menu determines the winner, and the fold SE bounds what the winner means.

Key Takeaways

1. EPE decomposes exactly into bias², variance and irreducible noise — for squared error. Classification has no clean analogue. Only the first two are yours to trade.

2. The irreducible term is a floor: with \(\sigma = 0.55\) the best achievable error was 0.3219 against a floor of 0.3025. Nothing crosses it.

3. Training error falls monotonically and therefore cannot select a model. The widening gap is the signature of overfitting.

4. One split is one noisy draw — 2.4× spread, wrong model 28% of the time, and the winner moves when you change the menu. CV fixes the selection; its fold SE tells you which rankings it cannot support.

5. Leakage makes every number above meaningless, and inflates scores most for the most flexible models — 44.5% vs 11.6% over 40 replications here; across 329 papers in 17 fields, ML's advantage over logistic regression largely vanished once the data respected time.

6. And none of it protects you when someone else's decision determined which outcomes you observe. That is Zillow — and it is an economics problem before it is a machine learning problem.
