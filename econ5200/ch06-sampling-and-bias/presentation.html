The Data Defect Identity

The Condition Underneath Everything

Selection bias means Cov(R, Y) is not zero — being in the data travels with the answer.

Selection bias means the chance of appearing in your data is correlated with the thing you are trying to measure:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$$

In plain English: if being in the dataset and what the answer would have been travel together at all, the sample mean sits off the population mean — and it stays off no matter how many rows arrive.

FROM THE PODCAST — The plain-English version of the covariance condition, worth saying before the algebra: there is an inclusion variable — “you either exist as a row in the spreadsheet or you don’t, a one or a zero” — and an outcome variable. “If those two travel together at all, the switch is flipped and the data is fundamentally bent.” Then the consequence, which is the thing students resist: “if the mechanism bringing people through the door is flawed, pulling more people through that exact same door just gives you more of the same flaw. Move One: The Error Is a Covariance

Let \(f = n/N\) be the sampling fraction, so \(\bar{R} = f\). Start from the definition and do nothing clever:

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N} R_i Y_i - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} Y_i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{R_i - f}{f}\,Y_i$$

Split \(Y_i\) into \((Y_i - \bar{Y}_N) + \bar{Y}_N\). The second piece carries \(\sum_i (R_i - \bar{R}) = 0\) and vanishes:

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N = \frac{1}{f}\,\mathrm{Cov}_N(R, Y)$$

Reading: your estimation error is the finite-population covariance between being observed and the outcome, divided by the fraction you sampled. No model, no distribution, no asymptotics have been assumed. It is an identity.

TIME: 3 min

Write it on the board rather than reading the slide.

  • The sentence that matters: nothing has been assumed.
  • Students trained on asymptotics expect a limit argument somewhere and there is not one, which is why the result feels so much sharper than a bound.

The Same Line, In English

Your error is not noise. It is exactly how much turning up in the data and the answer you would have given move together — divided by the slice of the population you took.

Cov = 0
Error is zero. Not small, not shrinking. Zero, for any n you like.

Cov ≠ 0
You own an error that buying more rows does not remove. That is why bias is a switch, not a dial.

And notice what we never assumed. No bell curve, no large  n , no model, no sampling distribution. This is algebra, so it is true of the dataset actually sitting on your desk — not true on average, not true in the limit.

Move Two: Dress the Covariance

Write \(\mathrm{Cov}_N(R,Y) = \rho_{R,Y}\,\sigma_R\,\sigma_Y\). Because \(R\) is Bernoulli with mean \(f\), its standard deviation is known exactly: \(\sigma_R = \sqrt{f(1-f)}\). Substitute:

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N = \frac{\rho_{R,Y}\sqrt{f(1-f)}\,\sigma_Y}{f}$$

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N \;=\; \rho_{R,Y} \;\times\; \sigma_Y \;\times\; \sqrt{\frac{1-f}{f}}$$

Reading: the error is a product of exactly three things — how bent the selection mechanism is, how variable the outcome is, and how much of the population you missed. Random sampling drives the first factor to zero by construction, the whole product collapses, and the third factor does all the work. That is the Chapter 5 story.

The Big Data Paradox, Formally

A probability sample buys ρ = 0 by design, and the MSE collapses to σ²/n.

MSE, first. Your error runs both ways — too high on one sample, too low on another — so averaging it raw lets the two cancel and a bent mechanism looks fine. Square first, then average. MSE is the average squared miss, and nothing can hide in it.

$$\mathrm{MSE} = \rho_{R,Y}^{2}\,\sigma_Y^{2}\,\frac{1-f}{f} \;\approx\; \rho_{R,Y}^{2}\,\sigma_Y^{2}\,\frac{N}{n}$$

Probability sample. \(\rho_{R,Y}=0\) by design, so this collapses to \(\sigma_Y^2/n\) — the formula you have used since undergrad.

Anything else. The MSE carries \(N/n\). The error grows with the size of the population you are describing.

The bucket and the ocean. A bucket with a coating that quietly repels red fish is nearly harmless on a backyard pond — you scoop most of it anyway. The same bucket on the Pacific is catastrophic. \(N\) is the ocean you are describing, not the bucket you are holding — which is why "we have everyone's clickstream" is not the reassurance it sounds like.

Move Three: Price It in Random Observations

Effective sample size prices your data in random observations: f divided by ρ²(1 − f).

Ask a question that turns the identity into a decision tool: how many SRS observations would carry the same MSE? Set the two MSEs equal.

$$\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{n_{\text{eff}}} = \rho_{R,Y}^{2}\,\sigma_Y^{2}\,\frac{1-f}{f}$$

$$n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{f}{\rho_{R,Y}^{2}\,(1-f)}$$

\(\sigma_Y\) cancels. The price of a bent mechanism does not depend on how variable the outcome is.

\(n\) is absent except through \(f\). Doubling \(n\) at fixed \(N\) doubles \(f\) — and roughly doubles \(n_{\text{eff}}\), from a base that may be 300.

What That Formula Is Asking

It strips away the illusion of volume and asks one question: how many perfectly random observations would buy me the same accuracy as this giant pile of biased data?

Why the world drops out. A noisy outcome punishes you and the random benchmark identically, so \(\sigma_Y\) cancels. The price is set by your mechanism alone.

Why volume barely helps. Row count enters only through f. Double your rows and you roughly double \(n_{\text{eff}}\) — but from a base that might be 300.

And it is not a polling curiosity. 400,000 Zillow rows at ρ = 0.05 and f = 40% are worth 267 random observations — so 1,500 genuinely random households carry about 5.6× more truth than a dataset 260 times their size.

Simple Random Sampling, With the Term Everyone Drops

Unbiasedness is a property of the procedure, not of the one dataset on your laptop.

\(\pi_i = n/N\) for every unit. Then:

$$E[\bar{y}] = \bar{Y}$$

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{y}) = \frac{s^2}{n}\left(1 - \frac{n}{N}\right)$$

Reading the first: unbiasedness is a property of the procedure, not of the one dataset on your laptop. Any single sample will miss. Repeat the exercise and the sample means centre exactly on the truth, with no systematic push in either direction.

Reading the second: \((1 - n/N)\) is the finite population correction, and it goes to zero as \(n \to N\). A census has no sampling variance because there is no sampling left to vary. It is negligible when \(n \ll N\) — and it is the term people silently drop after sampling 40% of a small frame, then report standard errors that are far too wide.

Horvitz–Thompson: Unequal, But Known

Horvitz–Thompson weights every observation by the inverse of its own inclusion probability.

Let \(\pi_i = P(R_i = 1) > 0\) be the inclusion probability for unit \(i\). Weight every observation by the inverse of its own probability of being there:

population total

$$\hat{Y}_{HT} = \sum_{i \in S} \frac{y_i}{\pi_i}$$

population mean

$$\hat{\bar{Y}}_{HT} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in S} \frac{y_i}{\pi_i}$$

Reading: a unit sampled with probability 1/10 speaks for ten units. You are not adjusting an estimate — you are rebuilding a synthetic copy of the population, a pseudo-population, in which each observed unit appears \(1/\pi_i\) times, and then computing the ordinary mean of that.

Horvitz & Thompson (1952), JASA 47(260).

The pseudo-population reading is the one that makes every later weighting method obvious.

→ TAKEAWAY — “Known \(\pi\) → HT, exactly unbiased. Estimated \(\pi\) → IPW, unbiased only if the model is right and positivity holds.”

FROM THE PODCAST — The weighing-scale walk-through is worth doing with two named students. “Because of my demographic I only had a one in ten chance of being picked. Horvitz–Thompson says take the inverse, which is 10, so my single answer gets multiplied by 10 — I am suddenly speaking for ten people. You had a one in two chance, so your answer gets multiplied by two.” Then the summary: “you are mathematically cloning the under-represented and suppressing the over-represented, rebuilding a pseudo-population that looks like the real world.”

  • Survey weights, propensity weights, importance weights, off-policy correction — all of them are this one idea with a different story about where pi came from.

What Each Symbol Is

Every term here is about one unit \(i\) in the population. Read the weight last.

\(i\) — one unit — one person, household, transaction.

\(\pi_i\) — the chance unit i had of getting in. In a survey you know it, because you chose it.

\(R_i\) — did unit i actually show up? 1 yes, 0 no.

\(y_i\) — unit i’s actual answer. A fixed number — the randomness is in who shows up, not in the answer.

\(S\) — the units you actually observed — what is in your spreadsheet.

\(N\) — the size of the whole population you are describing.

\(1/\pi_i\) — the weight. How many population units this one observation stands in for.

\(\hat{Y}_{HT}\) — your estimate of the population total. The hat means estimate.

TIME: 1.5 min

Do the weight last and say it as a sentence: one-in-ten odds means this row speaks for ten people.

  • The fixed-\(y_i\) point is the one students miss. The answer is not random; the sampling is. That is why the proof on the next slide can treat \(y_i\) as a constant.
  • Hat notation: if it wears a hat you computed it from data; if it does not, it is the truth you are chasing.

Why It Is Unbiased — Four Lines

Define \(Z_i = R_i\,y_i/\pi_i\) for every unit in the population, observed or not. \(R_i\) is Bernoulli with mean \(\pi_i\), and \(y_i\) is a fixed number, so:

$$E[Z_i] = E[R_i]\cdot\frac{y_i}{\pi_i} = \pi_i \cdot \frac{y_i}{\pi_i} = y_i$$

Sum over the population:

$$E[\hat{Y}_{HT}] = \sum_{i=1}^{N} E[Z_i] = \sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i = Y_{\text{total}}$$

Reading: unbiased regardless of how unequal the \(\pi_i\) are — the proof never used equality — provided every \(\pi_i > 0\). That last proviso is doing enormous work, and we come back to it in three slides.

TIME: 3 min

Walk the proof at the board; it is four lines and it is the only place today where a full proof is cheap enough to do properly.

  • The pedagogical point is where the assumption enters: it is not in the algebra, it is in the division.
  • If pi_i is zero the object Z_i is not defined at all, and no amount of data repairs that.

What Each Symbol Is

The proof invents one new object, \(Z_i\). Everything else you have already met.

\(Z_i\) — a bookkeeping device: unit i’s weighted answer if it showed up, and 0 if it did not. Defined for every unit, observed or not.

\(E[\cdot]\) — the average over all the ways the sample could have come out — not an average over your rows.

\(E[R_i]=\pi_i\) — “Bernoulli with mean \(\pi_i\)” just means: unit i shows up a \(\pi_i\) share of the time.

\(Y_{\text{total}}\) — the population total you are trying to recover. No hat — this is the truth.

the cancellation — \(\pi_i \times y_i/\pi_i = y_i\). The probability you divided by is exactly the probability of showing up, so it cancels.

\(\pi_i > 0\) — every unit needs some chance. A unit with zero chance contributes nothing and can never be weighted back in.

TIME: 1.5 min

  • \(Z_i\) feels like a trick until you say why: it lets you sum over the whole population, so nothing is missing from the sum by construction.
  • The \(\pi_i > 0\) card is the hook for positivity three slides later. Plant it here.

What the Weights Actually Rebuild

High-income households were sampled at \(\pi = 0.20\), low-income at \(\pi = 0.02\). The raw sample is 53% high-income against a population that is 15%.

Note the cost: 100 low-income households now carry the weight of 5,000. The estimator is unbiased and its variance is dominated by a handful of heavily-weighted rows.

From Designed \(\pi\) to Estimated \(\pi\)

Known π gives HT, exactly unbiased. Estimated π gives IPW, unbiased only if the model is right.

Survey sampling

You know \(\pi_i\) because you chose it. HT is exactly unbiased, full stop.

Observational data

You must estimate it. Replace \(\pi_i\) with a fitted propensity score \(e(X_i)\).

$$w_i = \frac{1}{e(X_i)} \;\text{ if treated,} \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{1 - e(X_i)} \;\text{ if not}$$

Reading: identical algebra, strictly weaker guarantee. HT's unbiasedness is a theorem about a known quantity; IPW's is conditional on the propensity model being correctly specified and on every confounder appearing in \(X\).

This one substitution is the foundation of propensity-score methods (Ch 9–10), importance sampling in off-policy evaluation, and covariate-shift correction (Shimodaira 2000).

What Each Symbol Is

One substitution, and one of these is known while the other is guessed.

\(\pi_i\) — the true, known inclusion probability. You have it because you designed the sample.

\(X_i\) — what you measured about unit i that might predict whether it shows up — age, income, device.

\(e(X_i)\) — the propensity score: your model’s guess at the chance unit i shows up, given \(X_i\). A fitted number, not a known one.

\(w_i\) — the weight you actually use: \(1/e(X_i)\) for the treated, \(1/(1-e(X_i))\) for the rest.

“correctly specified” — your model for \(e\) has the right form. If it is wrong, the weights are wrong and so is the answer.

what must be in \(X\) — every confounder. Anything that drives both showing up and the outcome, and is missing from \(X\), breaks it.

Answer: C — Positivity, and It Is Not Fixable Downstream

Positivity is an assumption, not a sample-size problem. A near-zero propensity cannot be fixed downstream.

The HT proof needs \(\pi_i > 0\) for every unit. As \(e(X_i) \to 0\) the weight \(1/e(X_i) \to \infty\), and one observation dominates the estimate. Your estimator is still unbiased in theory and useless in practice, because its variance is being set by a single row.

Why D is tempting but wrong: the model is not misreading that region — the region genuinely contains almost no untreated units. A more flexible model reports a propensity even closer to zero. The data contains no counterfactual there, and no estimator manufactures one.

Trimming and stabilised weights are patches: they buy variance by reintroducing bias, and they change the estimand to "the effect among units with overlap." Say that out loud in the paper.

Two Equations, One Unobserved Link

Heckman (1979) reframed the whole problem. Wages are observed only for people who chose to work:

$$Y_i = X_i'\beta + \epsilon_i \qquad \text{(outcome, observed only if } R_i = 1)$$

$$R_i^{*} = Z_i'\gamma + u_i, \qquad R_i = \mathbf{1}[R_i^{*} > 0] \qquad \text{(selection)}$$

\(R_i^{*}\) is a latent propensity to be observed. You never see it — you see only whether it crossed zero.

Assume \((\epsilon_i, u_i)\) jointly normal. If \(\mathrm{Corr}(\epsilon_i, u_i) \neq 0\), OLS on the observed sample is biased.

Reading: the correlation between the two error terms is the unobserved confounder from Discussion 2. Ambition sits in \(u_i\) (it drives enrolment) and in \(\epsilon_i\) (it drives wages). Heckman does not ask you to measure it. He asks you to model the process that let it in.

What Each Symbol Is

Two equations: one for the answer, one for whether you see it. The link between them is the whole idea.

\(Y_i\) — the outcome — the wage. Observed only for people who worked.

\(X_i,\ \beta\) — what predicts the wage, and how much each piece matters.

\(\epsilon_i\) — everything else that moves the wage and is not in \(X_i\).

\(R_i^{*}\) — a latent pull toward being observed. You never see it.

\(Z_i,\ \gamma\) — what predicts being observed at all — and how much.

\(u_i\) — everything else that moves selection and is not in \(Z_i\).

\(R_i=\mathbf{1}[R_i^{*}>0]\) — you see the outcome only when that hidden pull crosses zero.

\(\mathrm{Corr}(\epsilon_i,u_i)\) — the whole problem. If it is not zero, OLS on the observed sample is biased.

The Omitted Variable Has a Formula

Selection bias is an omitted variable, and the inverse Mills ratio is its formula.

$$E[\epsilon_i \mid R_i = 1] = \rho_{\epsilon u}\,\sigma_\epsilon\,\lambda(Z_i'\gamma)$$

$$\lambda(\cdot) = \frac{\phi(\cdot)}{\Phi(\cdot)}$$

Reading: conditioning on being observed gives the error term a non-zero mean — and that mean is a known function of the selection index. It is not an unknowable contamination. It is a variable you left out.

\(\lambda\) is positive and decreasing: where almost everyone is selected there is almost nothing to correct, and where few are selected the survivors are wildly unrepresentative.

TIME: 3 min

FROM THE PODCAST — The nightclub bouncer — the best explanation of the inverse Mills ratio I have seen anywhere, and it belongs on this slide. You want the average coolness of the people inside a windowless club you cannot enter. The Heckit Two-Step

Heckit: probit the selection, build λ, then include it as a regressor on the selected sample.

1

Probit of \(R_i\) on \(Z_i\) over the whole sample — selected and unselected — to get \(\hat\gamma\).

2

Construct \(\hat\lambda_i = \phi(Z_i'\hat\gamma)/\Phi(Z_i'\hat\gamma)\), then regress \(Y_i\) on \(X_i\) and \(\hat\lambda_i\) over the selected sample only.

3

Read the coefficient on \(\hat\lambda_i\): it estimates \(\rho_{\epsilon u}\sigma_\epsilon\). A direct test of whether selection was operating at all, and its sign tells you which way the naive estimate was pushed.

Standard errors. \(\hat\lambda\) is a generated regressor, so the second-stage OLS standard errors are wrong. Use the Heckman-corrected covariance or bootstrap the whole two-step. This is the same generated-regressor problem you met in 2SLS.

TIME: 3 min

What Identifies It — and Where You Have Seen It Before

If \(Z = X\), the model is identified only off the nonlinearity of \(\lambda\) — which is close to linear over much of its range. In practice that means identified by functional form, which is to say barely identified at all.

Credible Heckit needs a variable in \(Z\) that is not in \(X\): something that shifts the probability of being observed without shifting the outcome.

That is an exclusion restriction — the same object as your instrument from Topic 12.

Same demand, same untestability, same requirement that it be argued rather than tested. If a referee would not accept it as an instrument, do not accept it here either.

Heckman's own labour-supply application used the number and ages of children: they shift participation, and are argued not to shift the offered wage. Whether that argument survives is exactly the debate you had in Topic 12.

Response depends on the outcome itself, so this is MNAR. A and B both reweight on observables, which corrects MAR nonresponse and leaves the within-group distortion exactly where it was: the patients of a given age and ZIP who did respond are still the ones who recovered well.

Leakage Is a Violated Independence Assumption

Leakage breaks the independence of train and test, so the metric is confident and meaningless.

Leakage is information flowing from the test set into training. It is a survey where the pollster already knows the answers.

Feature leakage. Using a variable that is a consequence of the outcome. Predicting loan default from total_payments: defaulted loans have fewer payments by definition.
