Answer: D — But Only One of Them Is Yours to Choose

The error is a product of three terms. Halving either the selection correlation or the outcome's standard deviation halves the product. Quadrupling the panel touches only the third term — and it was never the term doing the damage.

Why A is tempting: "more data helps" is true and load-bearing everywhere else in this course. It is exactly one term of three, and the next fifteen minutes fence it off.

C is a real lever in survey design — you often can choose a less variable outcome or estimand. B is the one you buy with design, and it is the only one that can be driven to exactly zero.

The Data Defect Identity

Three algebraic moves, and an exact accounting of your error

Transition. "Everything so far has been an argument. The next six slides are algebra, and at the end of them the 400 stops being a claim."

The Condition Underneath Everything

Selection bias means the chance of appearing in your data is correlated with the thing you are trying to measure:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$$

\(R_i \in \{0,1\}\) — a switch: 1 if unit \(i\) made it into your data, 0 if not

\(Y_i\) — the outcome you care about for unit \(i\)

In plain English: if being in the dataset and what the answer would have been travel together at all, the sample mean sits off the population mean — and it stays off no matter how many rows arrive.

2 min. Emphasise "at all." This is not a threshold condition where a little correlation is tolerable. It is a switch: zero, or not zero. The hook's minus-0.005 is what "not zero" can look like.

Move One: The Error Is a Covariance

Let \(f = n/N\) be the sampling fraction, so \(\bar{R} = f\). Start from the definition and do nothing clever:

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N} R_i Y_i - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} Y_i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{R_i - f}{f}\,Y_i$$

Split \(Y_i\) into \((Y_i - \bar{Y}_N) + \bar{Y}_N\). The second piece carries \(\sum_i (R_i - \bar{R}) = 0\) and vanishes:

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N = \frac{1}{f}\,\mathrm{Cov}_N(R, Y)$$

Reading: your estimation error is the finite-population covariance between being observed and the outcome, divided by the fraction you sampled. No model, no distribution, no asymptotics have been assumed. It is an identity.

3 min. Write it on the board rather than reading the slide. The sentence that matters: nothing has been assumed. Students trained on asymptotics expect a limit argument somewhere and there is not one, which is why the result feels so much sharper than a bound.

Move Two: Dress the Covariance

Write \(\mathrm{Cov}_N(R,Y) = \rho_{R,Y}\,\sigma_R\,\sigma_Y\). Because \(R\) is Bernoulli with mean \(f\), its standard deviation is known exactly: \(\sigma_R = \sqrt{f(1-f)}\). Substitute:

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N = \frac{\rho_{R,Y}\sqrt{f(1-f)}\,\sigma_Y}{f}$$

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N \;=\; \rho_{R,Y} \;\times\; \sigma_Y \;\times\; \sqrt{\frac{1-f}{f}}$$

Reading: the error is a product of exactly three things — how bent the selection mechanism is, how variable the outcome is, and how much of the population you missed. Random sampling drives the first factor to zero by construction, the whole product collapses, and the third factor does all the work. That is the Chapter 5 story.

3 min. The Bernoulli step is the one students miss on the exam — sigma_R is not a nuisance to be estimated, it is pinned down by f alone, which is why the identity has no free parameters. Ask before revealing the last fragment: "which of these three does more data touch?"

Ten Times More Data, From the Same Pipe

\(\rho_{R,Y}\) — a property of the mechanism. Adding rows does not straighten the pipe.

\(\sigma_Y\) — a property of the world. It does not know how many rows you have.

\(\sqrt{(1-f)/f}\) — the only term that responds. Going from \(f=0.01\) to \(f=0.10\) cuts it by 70%.

A 70% cut in one factor of a product whose first factor is wrong is a 70% cut in a number that was never going to reach the truth.

2 min. The image to leave them with: pouring more water through a bent pipe does not straighten the pipe. Then note the honest caveat — a tenfold increase in f is enormous in practice; most "more data" moves change f by far less than that.

The Big Data Paradox, Formally

Square the identity and take expectations over the selection mechanism:

$$\mathrm{MSE} = \rho_{R,Y}^{2}\,\sigma_Y^{2}\,\frac{1-f}{f} \;\approx\; \rho_{R,Y}^{2}\,\sigma_Y^{2}\,\frac{N}{n}$$

Probability sample. \(\rho_{R,Y}=0\) by design, so this collapses to \(\sigma_Y^2/n\) — the formula you have used since undergrad.

Anything else. The MSE carries \(N/n\). The error grows with the size of the population you are describing.

Reading: a bent mechanism that is harmless when you describe a village is catastrophic when you describe a country. Nothing about the mechanism changed — only \(N\) did. This is the formal statement of the Big Data Paradox, and it is why "we have everyone's clickstream" is not the reassurance it sounds like.

3 min. Anticipate the objection a sharp student will raise: "so is big data useless?" No. Big data is superb for questions where the frame IS the population you care about — your own users, your own transactions. It fails when you generalise from a large convenient frame to a much larger target, and N in that formula is the target's size, not yours.

Move Three: Price It in Random Observations

Ask a question that turns the identity into a decision tool: how many SRS observations would carry the same MSE? Set the two MSEs equal.

$$\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{n_{\text{eff}}} = \rho_{R,Y}^{2}\,\sigma_Y^{2}\,\frac{1-f}{f}$$

$$n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{f}{\rho_{R,Y}^{2}\,(1-f)}$$

\(\sigma_Y\) cancels. The price of a bent mechanism does not depend on how variable the outcome is.

\(n\) is absent except through \(f\). Doubling \(n\) at fixed \(N\) doubles \(f\) — and roughly doubles \(n_{\text{eff}}\), from a base that may be 300.

3 min. This is the slide that converts theory into a tool they will actually use in the replication project. Make the cancellation of sigma_Y explicit — students expect noisier outcomes to be punished harder and they are not, because the SRS benchmark is punished identically.

Now Compute the 400

The 2016 CCES, with the chapter's own figures: \(\rho \approx -0.005\), \(n = 2.3\)M, \(N = 231\)M eligible voters, so \(f = 0.00996\).

$$n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{0.00996}{(0.005)^2(1-0.00996)}$$

= 402

Meng's "roughly 400" is not an assertion you take on trust. It is one line of arithmetic, and you just did it.

3 min. This is the payoff of the derivation and the emotional beat of the first half. Do the arithmetic live on the board if the room is with you. Then generalise from the chart: at f = 0.01, a rho of 0.01 already puts you below a 1,000-person survey, and 0.01 is a mild mechanism.

Debrief: The Ranking Is Not the Point

(c) is the sleeper best. A near-census of a well-defined frame — \(f\) close to 1, so \(\sqrt{(1-f)/f}\) is tiny whatever \(\rho\) does. Its weakness is the frame itself: informal sublets never appear on a filed lease.

(b) beats (a) and (d) at a fraction of their size, because it is the only option where \(\rho_{R,Y}=0\) is bought by construction rather than hoped for.

(a) has \(f = 0.4\) — an enormous sampling fraction. Even so, a mild \(|\rho| = 0.05\) gives \(n_{\text{eff}} = 0.4/(0.0025 \times 0.6) \approx 267\). Four hundred thousand rows carrying 267 observations' worth of information.

And is \(|\rho| = 0.05\) plausible for people who save Zillow searches? They are moving, they are searching in a price band, and their rent burden is exactly what the search reveals. That is not a mild mechanism.

Design: Buying \(\rho_{R,Y} = 0\)

The cure is a property of the procedure — and it is not the only thing a design can get wrong

Transition. "We have diagnosed the disease and priced it. Now the cure. Note the framing: you cannot fix rho after the fact, you can only buy it beforehand."

What Makes a Sample a Probability Sample

Every member of the population has a known, non-zero probability of selection.

Note what the definition does not say:

It says nothing about the sample being large.

It says nothing about the sample feeling random or arbitrary.

It says the inclusion probabilities are quantities you know — because you chose them.

Knowing \(\pi_i\) is what buys \(\rho_{R,Y} = 0\) when the \(\pi_i\) are equal, and what makes the bias correctable when they are not. That second case is Section 3.

2 min. Press on "feeling random." Whoever walks past your table on Huntington Avenue feels arbitrary and is not a probability sample — you cannot write down anyone's chance of being there. Arbitrary is not random. This distinction is the whole difference between a convenience sample and a design.

Simple Random Sampling, With the Term Everyone Drops

\(\pi_i = n/N\) for every unit. Then:

$$E[\bar{y}] = \bar{Y}$$

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{y}) = \frac{s^2}{n}\left(1 - \frac{n}{N}\right)$$

Reading the first: unbiasedness is a property of the procedure, not of the one dataset on your laptop. Any single sample will miss. Repeat the exercise and the sample means centre exactly on the truth, with no systematic push in either direction.

Reading the second: \((1 - n/N)\) is the finite population correction, and it goes to zero as \(n \to N\). A census has no sampling variance because there is no sampling left to vary. It is negligible when \(n \ll N\) — and it is the term people silently drop after sampling 40% of a small frame, then report standard errors that are far too wide.

3 min. The fpc is worth two extra minutes for this audience specifically: administrative datasets in their replication projects routinely cover 20-60% of the relevant frame, and every default standard-error formula in statsmodels assumes an infinite population. Overstated uncertainty is a real cost, not a conservative choice.

Stratification: Unbiased and Tighter

Sort the population into homogeneous strata, then draw an SRS inside each. Both designs are unbiased; stratification removes the between-stratum component of the variance, so it is unbiased and tighter.

SRS represents every subgroup in expectation.

Stratified represents every subgroup in this sample.

When a subgroup is rare, or the sample is small, that difference is the whole ballgame. The chapter's worked case — a 10,000-household city with a true mean income of $64,858:

Simple random sample, \(n = 200\)$66,227  (off by $1,369)
Stratified by income bracket, same \(n\)$64,744  (off by $114)

Two Design Failures, Only One of Them Curable by Data

Bent selection

\(\rho_{R,Y} \neq 0\). The first factor of the product. More data from the same source cannot move it. You must change the mechanism, or model it.

Too little of the population

The third factor is large relative to the effect you are chasing. More data fixes this exactly. It is the only failure in this lecture that money solves.

What the fintech result actually licenses: nothing. At 19.8% power, a null result is roughly four-to-one to appear even when the effect is exactly the size claimed. "Not significant" is not evidence of no effect; here it is barely evidence of anything.

Seeds Ch 8: this is the reason a \(p\)-value must always be read alongside the power the design had against the effect you cared about.

2 min. Give them the sentence to reuse in code review: "what is the minimum detectable effect of this design at 80% power?" It is one line of statsmodels and it kills more bad launches than any downstream analysis. Then set up the poll.

Answer: B — The Precision Is the Symptom

\(s^2/n\) is the SRS variance formula. Loyalty-app users are self-selected, so \(\rho_{R,Y} \neq 0\) and the relevant error is \(\rho^2\sigma_Y^2(1-f)/f\), which does not shrink with \(n\). The 3.4 million pins down the wrong number very tightly — and the narrow interval is produced by the same mechanism that produced the error.

Why A is tempting but wrong: underpowered means the third factor is too big. Here the third factor is tiny. The failure is in the first factor, and power analysis is silent about it.

Seeds Ch 7: a bootstrap resamples your data, so it reproduces this interval faithfully. Reproducibility tests your code. It cannot test your frame.

2 min. This is the precision-versus-accuracy axis, and it is the single most durable idea in the lecture. A number that reproduces across reruns is what people cite as evidence a finding is solid. It is evidence the pipeline is deterministic, nothing more.

Correcting Selection You Can Measure

Horvitz–Thompson, inverse probability weighting, and the assumption that binds

Transition. "Sections 1 and 2 were diagnosis and prevention. Everything from here is treatment — what you do when the sample already exists and you cannot go back and redesign it."

Horvitz–Thompson: Unequal, But Known

Let \(\pi_i = P(R_i = 1) > 0\) be the inclusion probability for unit \(i\). Weight every observation by the inverse of its own probability of being there:

population total

$$\hat{Y}_{HT} = \sum_{i \in S} \frac{y_i}{\pi_i}$$

population mean

$$\hat{\bar{Y}}_{HT} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in S} \frac{y_i}{\pi_i}$$

Reading: a unit sampled with probability 1/10 speaks for ten units. You are not adjusting an estimate — you are rebuilding a synthetic copy of the population, a pseudo-population, in which each observed unit appears \(1/\pi_i\) times, and then computing the ordinary mean of that.

Horvitz & Thompson (1952), JASA 47(260).

2 min. The pseudo-population reading is the one that makes every later weighting method obvious. Survey weights, propensity weights, importance weights, off-policy correction — all of them are this one idea with a different story about where pi came from.

Why It Is Unbiased — Four Lines

Define \(Z_i = R_i\,y_i/\pi_i\) for every unit in the population, observed or not. \(R_i\) is Bernoulli with mean \(\pi_i\), and \(y_i\) is a fixed number, so:

$$E[Z_i] = E[R_i]\cdot\frac{y_i}{\pi_i} = \pi_i \cdot \frac{y_i}{\pi_i} = y_i$$

Sum over the population:

$$E[\hat{Y}_{HT}] = \sum_{i=1}^{N} E[Z_i] = \sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i = Y_{\text{total}}$$

Reading: unbiased regardless of how unequal the \(\pi_i\) are — the proof never used equality — provided every \(\pi_i > 0\). That last proviso is doing enormous work, and we come back to it in three slides.

3 min. Walk the proof at the board; it is four lines and it is the only place today where a full proof is cheap enough to do properly. The pedagogical point is where the assumption enters: it is not in the algebra, it is in the division. If pi_i is zero the object Z_i is not defined at all, and no amount of data repairs that.

What the Weights Actually Rebuild

High-income households were sampled at \(\pi = 0.20\), low-income at \(\pi = 0.02\). The raw sample is 53% high-income against a population that is 15%.

Dividing by \(\pi_i\) restores each stratum to its true count exactly — not approximately, and not because \(n\) is large.

Note the cost: 100 low-income households now carry the weight of 5,000. The estimator is unbiased and its variance is dominated by a handful of heavily-weighted rows.

2 min. The variance caveat is the honest half and it is where students get burned in practice. Unbiasedness is free here; precision is not. Design-based variance estimators for HT are ugly for exactly this reason, and it is why survey statisticians trim weights even though trimming reintroduces bias.

From Designed \(\pi\) to Estimated \(\pi\)

Survey sampling

You know \(\pi_i\) because you chose it. HT is exactly unbiased, full stop.

Observational data

You must estimate it. Replace \(\pi_i\) with a fitted propensity score \(e(X_i)\).

$$w_i = \frac{1}{e(X_i)} \;\text{ if treated,} \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{1 - e(X_i)} \;\text{ if not}$$

Reading: identical algebra, strictly weaker guarantee. HT's unbiasedness is a theorem about a known quantity; IPW's is conditional on the propensity model being correctly specified and on every confounder appearing in \(X\).

This one substitution is the foundation of propensity-score methods (Ch 9–10), importance sampling in off-policy evaluation, and covariate-shift correction (Shimodaira 2000).

3 min. Say the trade plainly: you buy applicability to observational data and you pay in assumptions. Everything that goes wrong with IPW in practice traces to one of the two conditions in the reading — wrong functional form, or a confounder outside X. Neither is testable from the data you have.

What it demonstrates is that the estimator is not broken, not that your study will be fine.

Poll: The Weight That Ate the Estimate

You run IPW on 20,000 observations. One treated unit has an estimated propensity of \(e(X_i) = 0.0008\), and its weight is 1,250. Rerunning with a different seed moves your ATE by 40%.

What has gone wrong?

A) The estimator is biased — IPW needs equal propensities
B) Sample size — 20,000 is too few for weighting
C) Positivity is failing — there is no comparable untreated unit for that region of \(X\)
D) Logistic regression is the wrong propensity model
1 min. Click to vote. D is the seductive distractor for this cohort — "use a flexible model" is the reflex. A more flexible model would fit that region even more confidently and make the problem worse, which is a genuinely counter-intuitive point worth the poll.

Answer: C — Positivity, and It Is Not Fixable Downstream

The HT proof needs \(\pi_i > 0\) for every unit. As \(e(X_i) \to 0\) the weight \(1/e(X_i) \to \infty\), and one observation dominates the estimate. Your estimator is still unbiased in theory and useless in practice, because its variance is being set by a single row.

Why D is tempting but wrong: the model is not misreading that region — the region genuinely contains almost no untreated units. A more flexible model reports a propensity even closer to zero. The data contains no counterfactual there, and no estimator manufactures one.

Trimming and stabilised weights are patches: they buy variance by reintroducing bias, and they change the estimand to "the effect among units with overlap." Say that out loud in the paper.

2 min. The line worth writing down: unbiasedness is a statement about an average over repeated samples, and it says nothing about the sample you actually have. In finite samples with extreme weights, unbiased and unusable are entirely compatible. Then hand to Discussion 2.

Reading Overlap Before You Trust a Weight

Lab 6's propensities

Range 0.145 to 0.936. Comfortably interior, maximum weight about 6.9. This is why IPW worked so cleanly there — and it was designed to.

What real data looks like

A long left tail near zero, often produced by one covariate that nearly determines treatment. Maximum weights in the hundreds.

Three diagnostics, in the order you should run them:

1. Plot the two propensity distributions on the same axis. If they barely overlap, stop.

2. Report the maximum weight and the share of total weight carried by the top 1% of rows.

3. Re-estimate on the trimmed sample and say, explicitly, that the estimand changed.

2 min. This is the checklist for their own replication project and it is worth having them photograph the slide. The third item is the one reviewers catch: trimming without renaming the estimand is the most common quiet error in applied IPW papers.

When You Cannot Measure the Mechanism

Heckman (1979): selection bias is an omitted variable, and the omitted variable has a formula

Transition. "IPW required the confounder to be in X. Now suppose it is not, and cannot be. This is the case your replication projects will actually be in."

Discussion: Would You Believe This IPW Estimate?

A paper estimates the return to a voluntary employer training program. It weights by a propensity score fitted on age, tenure, department, prior salary and last year's performance rating. Overlap is excellent: propensities run 0.09 to 0.91. The IPW estimate is +8% on wages, tightly estimated.

Argue for

What does good overlap actually rule out? Name it precisely.

Argue against

Name one variable that is missing from that list, drives enrolment, and drives wages. Say which direction it biases the +8%.

Then the question that sets up the rest of the lecture: is there any diagnostic run on this dataset that could detect the problem you named?

3 min in pairs → 2 min share

5 min; press 3 for the timer. Target answers: motivation, ambition, an outside job offer, a manager's private assessment. Direction: upward, because the ambitious enrol AND get raises. The final question is the hinge of the lecture and the answer is no — overlap is a statement about X, and the problem is outside X. That "no" is what makes Heckman necessary rather than optional.

Two Equations, One Unobserved Link

Heckman (1979) reframed the whole problem. Wages are observed only for people who chose to work:

$$Y_i = X_i'\beta + \epsilon_i \qquad \text{(outcome, observed only if } R_i = 1)$$

$$R_i^{*} = Z_i'\gamma + u_i, \qquad R_i = \mathbf{1}[R_i^{*} > 0] \qquad \text{(selection)}$$

\(R_i^{*}\) is a latent propensity to be observed. You never see it — you see only whether it crossed zero.

Assume \((\epsilon_i, u_i)\) jointly normal. If \(\mathrm{Corr}(\epsilon_i, u_i) \neq 0\), OLS on the observed sample is biased.

Reading: the correlation between the two error terms is the unobserved confounder from Discussion 2. Ambition sits in \(u_i\) (it drives enrolment) and in \(\epsilon_i\) (it drives wages). Heckman does not ask you to measure it. He asks you to model the process that let it in.

3 min. Draw the link between the two error terms on the board as a curved arrow — that arrow is the entire problem, and it is the same arrow they drew as a backdoor path in Ch 10. The difference is that here the confounding node is unmeasured, which is exactly the case DAG adjustment could not handle.

The Omitted Variable Has a Formula

$$E[\epsilon_i \mid R_i = 1] = \rho_{\epsilon u}\,\sigma_\epsilon\,\lambda(Z_i'\gamma)$$

$$\lambda(\cdot) = \frac{\phi(\cdot)}{\Phi(\cdot)}$$

Reading: conditioning on being observed gives the error term a non-zero mean — and that mean is a known function of the selection index. It is not an unknowable contamination. It is a variable you left out.

\(\lambda\) is positive and decreasing: where almost everyone is selected there is almost nothing to correct, and where few are selected the survivors are wildly unrepresentative.

3 min. The sentence that makes this click for econometrics students: selection bias is omitted variable bias, and for once you can write the omitted variable down. Everything they know about OVB — sign, magnitude, what happens when you include the control — transfers immediately.

The Heckit Two-Step

1

Probit of \(R_i\) on \(Z_i\) over the whole sample — selected and unselected — to get \(\hat\gamma\).

2

Construct \(\hat\lambda_i = \phi(Z_i'\hat\gamma)/\Phi(Z_i'\hat\gamma)\), then regress \(Y_i\) on \(X_i\) and \(\hat\lambda_i\) over the selected sample only.

3

Read the coefficient on \(\hat\lambda_i\): it estimates \(\rho_{\epsilon u}\sigma_\epsilon\). A direct test of whether selection was operating at all, and its sign tells you which way the naive estimate was pushed.

Standard errors. \(\hat\lambda\) is a generated regressor, so the second-stage OLS standard errors are wrong. Use the Heckman-corrected covariance or bootstrap the whole two-step. This is the same generated-regressor problem you met in 2SLS.

3 min. Step 1 running over the whole sample is the detail students get wrong in code — the selection equation needs the units you do not observe outcomes for. If your data has been filtered to complete cases before you start, you cannot estimate the first stage at all, and that filtering has usually already happened upstream.

What Identifies It — and Where You Have Seen It Before

If \(Z = X\), the model is identified only off the nonlinearity of \(\lambda\) — which is close to linear over much of its range. In practice that means identified by functional form, which is to say barely identified at all.

Credible Heckit needs a variable in \(Z\) that is not in \(X\): something that shifts the probability of being observed without shifting the outcome.

That is an exclusion restriction — the same object as your instrument from Topic 12.

Same demand, same untestability, same requirement that it be argued rather than tested. If a referee would not accept it as an instrument, do not accept it here either.

Heckman's own labour-supply application used the number and ages of children: they shift participation, and are argued not to shift the offered wage. Whether that argument survives is exactly the debate you had in Topic 12.

3 min. This is the highest-value cross-reference in the deck for a cohort that has already done DiD, IV and RD. Make it explicit: the whole term has been one long argument that identification is bought with an untestable claim, and the claims keep turning out to be the same claim wearing different clothes.

Poll: Which Tool for Which Failure?

A hospital reports outcomes only for patients who completed a voluntary follow-up survey. Completion depends on how well the patient recovered — and nothing in the administrative record measures recovery.

What is the appropriate correction?

A) Post-stratify on age, sex and ZIP to match the patient population
B) IPW on the observed administrative covariates
C) A selection model, and only if you can defend an exclusion restriction
D) Collect more survey responses through the same channel
1 min. Click to vote. A and B will both draw real support because both are the standard reflex, and both are MAR fixes applied to an MNAR problem. That is the single most common error in applied work on nonresponse.

Answer: C — and Notice the Conditional

Response depends on the outcome itself, so this is MNAR. A and B both reweight on observables, which corrects MAR nonresponse and leaves the within-group distortion exactly where it was: the patients of a given age and ZIP who did respond are still the ones who recovered well.

Why D is the worst answer: more responses through the same channel raises \(f\) and shrinks the third factor. It cannot touch \(\rho_{R,Y}\). You buy precision around the wrong number.

The conditional in C is not a hedge. Without an exclusion restriction the selection model is identified only by the curvature of \(\lambda\), and the honest report is a bounds analysis plus a statement of what you cannot estimate.

2 min. Land the MAR-versus-MNAR distinction one final time — it is the through-line from Topic 1 and it is the most examinable idea today. And credit the students who voted A or B: those are the answers a competent analyst gives, which is exactly why the failure is so widespread.

Your Split Is a Sampling Design

Training set

The sample your model learns from.

Test set

Stands in for the population your model will meet in deployment.

Titanic, 891 passengers, 714 after dropping rows with a missing Age:

SplitTrain survivalTest survival
train_test_split(X, y)0.4100.392
train_test_split(X, y, stratify=y)0.4060.406

stratify=y is stratified sampling — the same design, the same guarantee. It matters most when the positive class is rare: at 2% fraud, an 80/20 split of 1,000 rows leaves roughly four positive cases in the test set, and your recall metric is computed on four observations.

2 min. Make the analogy exact rather than loose: when you report 87% test accuracy you are making an inference about a population you never observed — future users — from a sample you drew yourself. That is a survey, and you chose the frame.

Leakage Is a Violated Independence Assumption

Leakage is information flowing from the test set into training. It is a survey where the pollster already knows the answers.

Preprocessing leakage. Fitting a scaler or imputer on all the data before splitting — the training set now knows the test set's mean and variance.

Feature leakage. Using a variable that is a consequence of the outcome. Predicting loan default from total_payments: defaulted loans have fewer payments by definition.

Temporal leakage. Training on 2020–2024 to predict 2019.

Why it belongs in this lecture: Chapter 5 taught that independence of draws is what makes convergence work. Leakage breaks the independence of train and test, so the test set is no longer a sample from the deployment distribution. The metric is confident and meaningless — exactly like the 2.3 million responses.

2 min. Give them the portable test for feature leakage: for every column, ask whether you would have that value at the moment the prediction must be made. If not, it is not a feature, it is the answer key. That question catches more leakage than any automated check.

Topic 6 in a Nutshell

An identity, not a bound. \(\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N = \rho_{R,Y}\sigma_Y\sqrt{(1-f)/f}\). Three factors; more data shrinks one.

A price, in random observations. \(n_{\text{eff}} = f/(\rho^2(1-f))\) reproduces Meng's 400 exactly.

Known \(\pi\) → HT, exactly unbiased. Estimated \(\pi\) → IPW, unbiased only if the model is right and positivity holds.

Unknown mechanism → model it. Heckman turns selection bias into an omitted variable, and the omitted variable has a formula.

Not every design failure is a bias failure. An underpowered test at 19.8% power is the one failure in this lecture that more data genuinely fixes — and knowing which failure you have is the whole skill.
