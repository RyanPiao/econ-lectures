1  •  What an Instrument Is

Two assumptions. One you can test. One you must argue.

The Problem: Endogeneity

We want $\beta$ in   $Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \varepsilon_i$   — $Y_i$ log earnings, $D_i$ years of schooling, $\varepsilon_i$ everything else, including unobserved ability.

$\text{Cov}(D_i, \varepsilon_i) \neq 0$

Interpretation: high-ability people get more schooling and earn more anyway. OLS is inconsistent.

$\text{plim}\,\hat\beta^{OLS} = \beta + \dfrac{\text{Cov}(D_i,\varepsilon_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)}$

Interpretation: the second term is omitted-variable bias — and it does not shrink with $n$.

More data estimates the wrong number more precisely. Ch 6's data defect identity, again.

3 min. Ch 10 would tell you to measure every confounder and condition on it. Ability alone defeats that. Make sure the plim line is read aloud as a sentence, not as symbols.

The Trick: Find a Lever

Suppose something pushes people into more schooling for a reason that has nothing to do with how much they would earn. Growing up within commuting distance of a college, say. It makes college cheaper, so more people go. It does not, by itself, make you a more productive worker.

Then the variation in schooling caused by proximity is clean — as good as randomly assigned. Throw away all the other variation, the part driven by ability and ambition and family money, and use only that slice.

An instrument does not fix your data. It discards most of it — keeping only the variation you can defend, and paying for that defensibility in precision.

2 min. The word "discards" is the one to land. Everything about IV — the wide standard errors, the LATE, the fragility — follows from the fact that it throws data away on purpose.

Two Assumptions — and They Are Not Symmetric

Relevance

$\text{Cov}(Z_i, D_i) \neq 0$

Interpretation: the instrument actually moves the treatment.

TESTABLE — you observe both $Z_i$ and $D_i$.

Exclusion (validity)

$\text{Cov}(Z_i, \varepsilon_i) = 0$

Interpretation: $Z_i$ reaches $Y_i$ only through $D_i$.

UNTESTABLE IN PRINCIPLE — $\varepsilon_i$ is unobservable by construction.

No sample size, no diagnostic and no robustness table establishes exclusion. An IV paper is an argument about institutions with a regression attached.

3 min. Section 4 will show that even the TESTABLE one has been tested wrong for twenty-five years. Say that now — it stops students filing relevance away as "solved."

The Estimand, in Two Lines

Take the covariance of the structural equation with $Z_i$:

$\text{Cov}(Z_i, Y_i) = \beta\,\text{Cov}(Z_i, D_i) + \underbrace{\text{Cov}(Z_i, \varepsilon_i)}_{=\,0\ \text{by exclusion}}$

Rearrange — which requires $\text{Cov}(Z_i, D_i) \neq 0$, so relevance is exactly the condition that lets you divide:

$\beta = \dfrac{\text{Cov}(Z_i, Y_i)}{\text{Cov}(Z_i, D_i)} = \dfrac{\textbf{reduced form}}{\textbf{first stage}}$

Interpretation: the effect of the instrument on the outcome, divided by its effect on the treatment. That ratio is the entire method.

Plainly. A nudge raises college attendance by 8pp and raises average earnings by 1.6%. If the nudge touches earnings only through college, the effect on those it moved is $0.016 / 0.08 = 20\%$. You scale the nudge's total effect by the fraction who actually complied.

4 min. Derive this on the board, not from the slide. Two lines. The pedagogical payoff is that relevance and exclusion each do visible work — exclusion kills a term, relevance permits a division.

The Exclusion Restriction Is a Missing Arrow

$U$ confounds $D$ and $Y$ — that is the endogeneity. There is no arrow into $Z$, and no arrow from $Z$ to $Y$ except through $D$.

When someone attacks your instrument, they are drawing that arrow and asking you to rule it out.

For college proximity: better local labour markets? Better schools? Different industry mix? Every one is a candidate arrow, and each must be argued away or controlled for.

2 min. Ch 10 said a graph's strongest claims are its absences. This is the sharpest instance of that in the whole book — harvest the recognition.

Common Misconception

“My instrument is randomly assigned, so the exclusion restriction holds.”

Randomisation gives you

Independence. The instrument is unrelated to everything at baseline. Free.

Exclusion is separate

And stronger. That it reaches the outcome through no channel except treatment. Never free.

A randomised email encouraging users to try a feature is perfectly independent — and it violates exclusion the moment it also reminds them their subscription exists. Randomisation buys the first assumption. The second one you still have to argue, and it is the one that usually fails.

2 min. This example returns in Section 5 as the encouragement design. Plant it now so the callback lands.

2  •  From Wald to Two-Stage Least Squares

Four sample means, and then everything else.

The Wald Estimator: Four Sample Means

When the instrument is binary, the ratio has an immediate sample analogue.

$\hat\beta^{Wald} = \dfrac{\bar Y_{Z=1} - \bar Y_{Z=0}}{\bar D_{Z=1} - \bar D_{Z=0}}$

Interpretation: the difference in average outcomes between instrument groups, divided by the difference in average treatment. Four numbers. No matrix algebra.

Reduced form ($Z \to Y$)
0.1559

First stage ($Z \to D$)
0.8290

Wald = RF / FS
0.1881

And IV2SLS with no covariates returns 0.1881, exactly. 2SLS is the Wald estimator generalised.

4 min. Do the arithmetic on the board from the four means BEFORE showing any code — students should watch 0.1881 emerge. Card's proximity raises schooling by 0.83 years and log wages by 0.156.

2SLS Mechanics

FIRST STAGE — project the endogenous variable onto the instrument and controls

$D_i = \gamma_0 + \pi Z_i + \mathbf{X}_i'\boldsymbol\gamma + \nu_i$

Interpretation: $\pi$ is how hard the instrument pushes. Relevance is exactly the claim $\pi \neq 0$.

SECOND STAGE — regress the outcome on the fitted treatment

$Y_i = \alpha + \beta \hat D_i + \mathbf{X}_i'\boldsymbol\delta + \eta_i$

Interpretation: $\hat D_i$ holds only the variation the instrument explains — the part that is, by assumption, unrelated to $\varepsilon_i$. Everything else has been thrown away.

That discarding is why IV estimates are so much less precise than OLS — a cost Card makes vivid in two slides.

3 min. Every symbol gets a name out loud. Do not introduce the SE problem yet; the next slide is better as a surprise.

The Trap: Never Hand-Roll the Second Stage

Running those two regressions yourself gives the right coefficient and the wrong standard error. Worth doing once, deliberately — it is a bug that survives code review.

manual 2SLS   coef=0.2587  SE=0.0284  t=9.11
linearmodels  coef=0.2587  SE=0.0340  t=7.61

Identical coefficients. Standard errors understated by 20%, and $t$ inflated from 7.61 to 9.11.

Mechanism. The hand-rolled stage computes residuals as $Y_i - \hat D_i\hat\beta$, using the fitted treatment. The correct structural residual uses the actual one, $Y_i - D_i\hat\beta$. A projection has strictly less variance than what it projects — so the residuals are too small, the error variance is too small, and every standard error inherits it. The bias is always toward overconfidence.

Here it turns 7.6 into 9.1 and changes nothing. In a marginal case it manufactures a discovery.

4 min. Run this live if you can. The identical coefficients with different standard errors is a genuine surprise, and the projection explanation is the kind of thing that sticks after one hearing.

Answer: B — The Wrong Residual

The projection $\hat D_i$ has less variance than $D_i$, so the manual residuals are too small, the estimated error variance is too small, and every standard error comes out too tight — always in the direction of overconfidence.

Why A is tempting but wrong: the two estimators are algebraically identical for the point estimate — that is why the coefficients match to four decimals. The divergence is entirely in the variance formula.

Honest Stories — and the Tell

Story 1 — LATE. The people proximity moved were liquidity-constrained. Their return to schooling may genuinely be higher than the average person's. (Section 3.)

Story 2 — measurement error. Classical error in self-reported schooling attenuates OLS toward zero; IV corrects for it.

Both are respectable. But Lal, Lockhart, Xu & Zu (2024) replicated 67 IV studies and found 2SLS $\gg$ OLS in the majority — and something worse:

The ratio is negatively correlated with instrument strength wherever the instrument was not experimentally generated. Under robust inference, roughly 19–30% of published 2SLS estimates are underpowered.

If 2SLS were simply correcting bias, the size of the correction would have no reason to track how weak the instrument is.

3 min. This is the pivot into Section 4. The correlation with strength is the decisive piece of evidence — it is what turns "IV is bigger than OLS" from a curiosity into a diagnosis.

Poll: How Should You Read That Pattern?

Across a large replication literature, IV estimates run far above OLS — and the gap widens as instruments get weaker.

The best reading is:

A) IV is correcting a large downward bias in OLS
B) A diagnostic warning: weak first stages amplify small exclusion violations
C) Evidence that OLS should be abandoned for causal work
D) Sampling noise — with enough studies anything correlates
1 min. Click to vote. A is the field's own historical reading, so a large A share is the honest starting point rather than an embarrassment.

Answer: B — Read It as a Warning

A correction that tracks instrument strength is not a correction. It is an artefact of dividing by a small, noisy denominator — any tiny breach of exclusion gets divided by a near-zero first stage and blown up.

Why A is tempting but wrong: it was the discipline's own reading for decades, and it is not refutable from a single study. What refutes it is the correlation with strength — bias correction has no reason to scale with how weak your lever is.

3  •  Whose Effect Did You Estimate?

A number for a group you cannot name, cannot count, and did not choose.

Four Types (Imbens & Angrist 1994)

$D_i(0) = 0$  untreated without the push$D_i(0) = 1$  treated anyway
$D_i(1) = 1$COMPLIER — moves when pushedAlways-taker
$D_i(1) = 0$Never-takerDefier — assumed away

The instrument creates variation only among compliers. Always-takers and never-takers do not move, so they contribute nothing to the numerator and nothing to the denominator.

IV therefore recovers the average effect for compliers only — the Local Average Treatment Effect. “Local” does not mean “in a region.” It means for the subpopulation this particular instrument happens to move.

3 min. Draw the 2×2 on the board as $D_i(0)$ against $D_i(1)$ — the defier cell is the one you assume away, and drawing it is what makes monotonicity concrete two slides from now.

You Cannot Identify Who the Compliers Are

Compliance is defined by a counterfactual — what someone would do under both values of the instrument. You observe each person under one.

What you can do

Estimate the complier share — it is the first stage. With more work, describe their characteristics.

What you can never do

Produce a list. Not with more data, not with a better model. It is not a sample-size problem.

So a different instrument for the same treatment estimates a different parameter. Card's proximity moves people for whom distance was the binding constraint — plausibly poorer, liquidity-constrained, plausibly higher return. Angrist & Krueger's quarter-of-birth moves people who wanted to leave at the minimum legal age — a very different group with plausibly a much lower return.

Two valid instruments, two different numbers, and neither is wrong. They are estimates of different estimands.

3 min. The "cannot identify individually" point reliably produces resistance. Let a student argue it out rather than settling it yourself — the objection dies when they try to state what data would settle it.

Monotonicity: No Defiers

$D_i(1) \geq D_i(0) \quad \text{for all } i$

Interpretation: the instrument pushes everyone in the same direction, or not at all. This is a statement about every individual, not about an average.

Monotonicity is what makes the denominator interpretable. Without it the first stage is a net movement — some people pushed in, others pushed out — and the ratio no longer corresponds to any average effect at all.

Where this bites: judge-leniency designs. Cases are randomly assigned to decision-makers with different approval rates. Frandsen, Lefgren & Leslie (2023) show that if judges are Bayesian decision-makers weighing multidimensional signals, average monotonicity is routinely violated — a lenient judge may deny a case a strict judge would grant, because they weight the evidence differently. When that happens the 2SLS estimate loses its causal interpretation entirely.

2 min. Point back at the 2×2: monotonicity is exactly "the bottom-right cell is empty." Judge designs return in Section 5 as one of three modern families.

The Saturation Problem — Chapter 11, Second Sighting

Standard practice reads 2SLS-with-covariates as a positively weighted average of covariate-specific LATEs. Blandhol, Bonney, Mogstad & Torgovitsky (2022) proved this is false unless the specification is non-parametrically saturated — the instrument interacted with every combination of covariate values.

Include covariates linearly, as essentially every applied paper does, and 2SLS assigns negative weights to the treatment effects of always-takers and never-takers. The estimate can come back negative when the treatment strictly helped every single person in the population.

You have seen this before. TWFE under staggered adoption; 2SLS with linear covariates. Same pathology, different estimator: a linear specification imposing a weighting scheme the researcher never chose and cannot see in the output. Blandhol et al. is to IV what Goodman-Bacon is to DiD.

Remedy: saturate — estimate a LATE within each covariate cell and aggregate with weights you choose and report. Expensive in degrees of freedom. The alternative is an estimate whose sign you cannot vouch for.

4 min. Draw the Ch 11 parallel on the board explicitly. For a graduate cohort, seeing the same pathology twice in two weeks is the single most valuable thing in this module — the 2020s reckoning is one discovery repeated across designs.

Think Like an Economist

A product manager sees your significant IV estimate and asks whether rolling the treatment out to everyone will deliver that lift.

The answer is no — and the useful version explains why.

Compliers
The slice a nudge could tip. Your estimate is about them.

Always-takers
Already had the treatment. The rollout does nothing for them.

Never-takers
Will not adopt whatever you do.

The professional move: state the estimate, describe the compliers concretely, and say what would have to be true about everyone else for the number to generalise.

2 min. This is the interview answer. Students who can say it in this order — estimate, compliers, extrapolation condition — sound like they have done the work, because they have.

4  •  The Weak Instrument Crisis

Where the field spent twenty-five years being wrong about the assumption it could test.

The Mechanism Is the Ratio

The problem is not that a weak instrument gives an imprecise estimate. It does — but that would be honest: wide intervals telling you that you have learned little.

The problem is that it gives an estimate biased in a specific direction — toward OLS — while reporting standard errors that suggest more confidence than you have.

IV divides by the first stage. As the first stage approaches zero you are dividing by something small and noisy. Any tiny correlation between instrument and error — any small breach of exclusion — gets divided by that small number and blown up.

In the limit of a useless instrument, 2SLS converges on the OLS estimate you were trying to escape.

2 min. Point back at the estimand slide: $\beta = $ RF / FS. Everything here is a consequence of what happens to a ratio when the denominator goes to zero.

Instruments Made of Pure Noise

Bound, Jaeger & Baker (1995) took Angrist & Krueger's celebrated quarter-of-birth study — hundreds of thousands of observations, a beautifully clean natural experiment — and replaced the real instruments with randomly generated ones.

They reproduced results resembling the published estimates.

The instruments contained essentially no information. The procedure still produced a plausible-looking number, with a standard error, in a table.

This is why the field started caring. Not a theoretical worry — a demonstration that the output is indistinguishable from the output of nothing.

2 min. Tell this one in full. It is short, it is shocking, and it is the historical hinge — it converted weak instruments from a footnote into a literature.

A Weak Instrument Is Worse Than No Instrument

OLS was biased by +1.0. The IV estimate hired to fix it is off by +2.9.

A weak instrument is not a weaker correction. It is a different, larger and less legible error — dressed in the language of causal identification.

No instrument leaves you with an OLS bias you can at least reason about. A weak one gives you a different bias you cannot.

2 min. This is the payoff of the hook. Say it plainly: the method that was supposed to rescue you from bias handed you a bigger one and called it identification.

The Confident Wrong Answer

One draw is not evidence. Across 500 draws of the same DGP:

$\pi = 0.05$

31.4% of studies reject $\beta = 0$ at the 5% level.

Of those significant results, 75.2% are off the truth by more than 0.5.

$\pi = 0.01$

21.0% reject $\beta = 0$.

Of those, 98.1% are wrong by more than 0.5.

You do not run 500 studies. You run one. “My second stage was significant” is therefore not a defence — under a weak instrument, significance is evidence of a lucky draw from a distribution with no finite moments, not evidence of identification.

3 min. Stop hard here. Ask the room whether an estimator that is right on the median is behaving; let them say yes; then reveal the conditional-on-significance column. That is the moment the lecture's thesis lands.

Poll: What Does That Tell You?

The right conclusion is:

A) Weak instruments produce obviously noisy, easily spotted results
B) Significance under a weak instrument is close to worthless as evidence
C) Increase the sample size and the problem resolves
D) Use a one-sided test to recover the lost power
1 min. Click to vote. C is the reflex answer of anyone trained to treat every inference problem as a sample-size problem — worth surfacing.

Answer: B — Confident, Not Noisy

A weak instrument produces a confident wrong answer, not a visibly noisy one. That is exactly why these results survive peer review — nothing in the output looks broken.

Why C is tempting but wrong: weak-instrument bias does not vanish with $n$. The bias is governed by the concentration parameter, and more data on a lever that does not move anything buys you nothing.

Stop Using $F > 10$

Lee, McCrary, Moreira & Porter (2022): a genuine 5% test in the just-identified model needs $F > 104.7$.

Their $tF$ procedure gives an adjusted standard error as a smooth function of the first-stage $F$. Keep $F$ near 10 and you must inflate the critical value from 1.96 to about 3.43 to preserve valid inference.

And use an effective $F$. The conventional first-stage $F$ assumes homoskedastic, unclustered errors. Real data have neither. Montiel Olea–Pflueger (Andrews, Stock & Sun 2019).

3 min. Lal et al. identify ignoring clustered errors as a leading source of overstated instrument strength — researchers are not just using the wrong threshold, they are using it on the wrong statistic.

Common Misconception

“linearmodels gives me the effective $F$ in first_stage.”

It does not. That property reports R², partial R², Shea's R², and a partial $F$ — and under a robust covariance that partial $F$ is reported on a chi-squared distribution. There is no Montiel Olea–Pflueger effective $F$ in that table.

Compute it separately, or use a package that implements it (ivDiag in R).

Believing you have checked something you have not is a worse position than knowing you have not checked it.

2 min. Worth being blunt: this is a real bug in real papers, and it comes from reading a table heading rather than the documentation.

Report a Weak-Instrument-Robust Confidence Set

Anderson–Rubin sidesteps the whole problem by never dividing by the first stage. Under $H_0: \beta = \beta_0$, the restricted residual $Y_i - \beta_0 D_i$ must be uncorrelated with $Z_i$. Test that directly, sweep a grid of candidate $\beta_0$, and keep every value you cannot reject.

Anderson-Rubin 95% CI: [0.2020, 0.3390]
Conventional Wald CI:  [0.1926, 0.3248]

At Card's $F = 59$ the two are close — the AR set is shifted up and about 4% wider. That is the honest picture for a moderately strong instrument, and it is reassuring here.

The value of AR is what it does when the instrument is genuinely weak: the set becomes unbounded, or empty — the method correctly reporting that the data do not identify $\beta$.

A Wald interval never tells you that. An AR set does.

3 min. The unbounded-set property is the single most useful thing about the method and students find it strange — spend the time. An empty set is not a bug; it is the estimator refusing to answer.

5  •  When Exclusion Is Only Nearly True

The third instance of one idea.

Plausibly Exogenous

Every serious objection to an IV paper is an argument that exclusion fails a little. Not catastrophically — a little. The conventional response is to insist it holds exactly: a binary claim about an untestable assumption.

Conley, Hansen & Rossi (2012) formalise the mature alternative. Allow the instrument a direct effect:

$Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \gamma Z_i + \varepsilon_i$

Interpretation: $\gamma$ is exactly the arrow the DAG said was absent. Standard IV asserts $\gamma = 0$.

Instead of asserting it, place $\gamma$ in a plausible range and trace $\hat\beta$ across it — the union of confidence intervals, or the local-to-zero approximation. The output is a set, and the reported claim becomes conditional and defensible:

“Our conclusion survives any direct effect of the instrument on the outcome smaller than $\bar\gamma$.”

2 min. This is exactly the γ-sweep you will build in Diagnosis 3 of Lab 12 — say so, so the lab reads as a continuation rather than a new task.

Key Takeaways

An instrument discards data. Identification without measuring a confounder, bought by keeping only the slice a lever explains — which is why IV costs so much precision.

One assumption is testable, one is not. Randomisation delivers independence, never exclusion. Exclusion is an argument about institutions with a regression attached.

IV estimates the LATE. Compliers only — a group you cannot enumerate. Different valid instruments recover genuinely different parameters, and neither is wrong.

$F > 10$ is wrong by an order of magnitude. Valid 5% inference needs $F > 104.7$; at $\pi = 0.01$ our simulation returned 3.949 against a truth of 1.0 and an OLS bias of 2.018.

Unless you saturate, 2SLS with covariates carries negative weights — Chapter 11's pathology in a second estimator. And: when an assumption cannot be tested, bound it and report the breakdown value.
