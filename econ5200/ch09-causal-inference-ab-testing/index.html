1  •  What a Coin Flip Buys

One term in one decomposition. Not more, and not less.

The Outcome You Cannot Observe

Priya sees the new checkout button and buys. In the world where she saw the old one, she does not. The causal effect on Priya is the gap between those two worlds — and she only ever lives in one.

Notation for the rest of the term

\(Y_i(1)\) — outcome if treated
\(Y_i(0)\) — outcome if not
\(D_i \in \{0,1\}\) — treatment indicator

\[\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\]

In words: the effect on one person is the gap between two versions of the same person at the same moment. One of them is never recorded, so \(\tau_i\) is not hard to estimate — it is missing by construction.

Holland (1986), the Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference — Chapter 9 §4.1.
2.5 min. Write \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\) on the board and leave them there all lecture; every method after today is a different answer to "how do I get at the one I cannot see." Insist that a potential outcome is defined for everyone, treated or not — that is what makes the notation do work.

The Retreat to the Average

\[\tau = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] = E[Y_i(1)] - E[Y_i(0)]\]

In words: we give up on any individual's effect and ask for the population average instead. Averaging is what converts an impossible quantity into one that a difference between two group means can reach.

Note what the retreat costs. \(\tau\) says nothing about who benefits. A treatment that helps half the users and harms the other half by the same amount has \(\tau = 0\) — and a scorecard that reports only \(\tau\) will call it a non-event.

Heterogeneity is Chapter 24's problem — Double ML estimates conditional effects, not just the average.
2 min. The last box is the graduate addition. Ask: "what experiment would you run to detect that cancellation?" — answer is pre-registered subgroups or a CATE model, and that is where Ch 24 goes.

What a Naive Comparison Actually Measures

\[E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] = \underbrace{E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]}_{\text{what you want}} + \underbrace{E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i=1] - E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}\]

In words: the number you compute is the number you want plus a term asking how the two groups would have differed had nobody been treated.

That second term is not measurement error. It has no \(1/\sqrt{n}\) in it. Ten million observations estimate a biased quantity very precisely — which is exactly Meng's data-defect result from Chapter 6.

Chapter 9 §4.2. The bias term is the reason Chapters 10–13 exist.
3 min. Derive it on the board rather than reading it: add and subtract \(E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1]\). Students who have seen this in econometrics still get value from watching the potential-outcomes version, because here the added-and-subtracted term is a counterfactual rather than an algebraic convenience.

The Same Programme, Two Answers

Same programme. Same men. Same outcome variable. Only the comparison group changes — and the conclusion changes sign.

Note the direction. Students expect selection bias to flatter a programme. NSW enrollees were far more disadvantaged than PSID men, so the bias runs downward and makes an effective programme look actively harmful.

Lalonde (1986); Dehejia & Wahba (1999) subsample, 185 treated / 260 control. Computed in figures/_prep_data.py from the NBER files.

What Randomisation Actually Assumes

\[D_i \perp\!\!\!\perp \big(Y_i(1),\, Y_i(0)\big)\]

In words: assignment carries no information about what would have happened under either condition. Not "the groups look similar on the covariates you plotted" — independence from both potential outcomes, measured and unmeasured alike.

The consequence is one line: \(E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i=1] = E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i=0]\), so the bracketed selection term is exactly zero, and \(\hat{\tau} = \bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C\) is unbiased for the ATE.

Chapter 9 §4.3. Chapter 10 restates this as "randomisation deletes every arrow into \(D\)", which closes all backdoor paths at once.
2.5 min. Push on the difference between independence and balance: a balance table is evidence about a finite sample, the independence statement is about the assignment mechanism. A failed balance test on one covariate out of twenty is expected; a broken randomiser is not.

The Payoff, and Why It Feels Like a Trick

Chapter 8 — the t-test

\(\bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C\)

A statement about two samples.

Chapter 9 — the ATE

\(\bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C\)

A statement about a cause.

Identical arithmetic. The causal content is not in the estimator — it is in the data-generating process that produced \(D_i\). You cannot read it off the output, and no software will warn you when it is absent.

2 min. This is the sentence to repeat all term. Every method in Chapters 10–13 is an attempt to manufacture that data-generating property when you were not allowed to randomise.

2  •  Measuring It Honestly

Inference with no distributional assumptions, and the sample size you should have committed to.

Why Last Week's Tool Is Uncomfortable Here

The t-test leans on the CLT. Revenue per user, session duration, transaction value: right-skewed and zero-inflated. Most users spend nothing; a few spend a great deal.

Chapter 7 already gave you the warning: CLT convergence is slow for heavy-tailed data. "Asymptotically valid" is not a property of your dataset; it is a property of a limit your dataset may be nowhere near.

In the Lalonde sample roughly a third of 1978 earnings are exactly zero. The sampling distribution of the difference in means is not a bell curve, and we do not need it to be.

1.5 min. If someone objects that n = 445 is "big enough", agree that it usually is and then point at the zero mass — the issue is not n, it is the shape the CLT has to smooth over.

The Permutation Test: One Idea

Under Fisher's sharp null — \(Y_i(1) = Y_i(0)\) for every \(i\) — the labels "treatment" and "control" are arbitrary. Every outcome is fixed; only the label varies.

Stronger than "the ATE is zero." The sharp null says no individual is affected at all — and that is exactly what licenses treating the labels as exchangeable, which is what makes the test exact in finite samples rather than asymptotically valid.

The algorithm

1. Compute \(\hat{\tau}_{\text{obs}} = \bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C\)
2. Pool every observation
3. Reassign labels at random, keeping group sizes
4. Recompute the difference
5. Repeat 20,000 times
6. \(p\) = share of shuffles at least as extreme

Chapter 9 §5.1. This is the third resampling flavour from Chapter 7 — Monte Carlo, bootstrap, permutation — and the only one that belongs to a randomised design.
2.5 min. The exchangeability point is the graduate half of this slide. Ask why we cannot permute in an observational study: because there the labels are not exchangeable even under a null of no effect, since assignment depended on the units.

What 20,000 Shuffles Look Like

Observed +$1,794. Fewer than four shuffles in a thousand reach it.

\(p = 0.0037\)

What we did not assume: normality, equal variances, a t-distribution, or a large-sample limit. The null distribution was built out of the data we were handed.

Power Is a Design Tool, Not a Report

\[n = \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_{\beta})^2 \,(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_0^2)}{\delta^2}\]

\(z_{\alpha/2}\) — 1.96 at \(\alpha = 0.05\)
\(z_{\beta}\) — 0.84 at 80% power
\(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_0^2\) — how noisy the metric is
\(\delta\) — the minimum detectable effect

In words: three of the four inputs are conventions. Only \(\delta\) is a business decision — the smallest effect you would actually act on — and it is the one people skip.

\(\delta\) sits in the denominator, squared. That single fact governs every experiment you will ever size.

2 min. Make them say what \(\delta\) is for a product they know. "Any improvement" is not an answer — press until someone commits to a number, then compute what it costs on the next slide.

1 min. Expect a heavy B. It is the right rule applied to the wrong ratio, which is the most instructive kind of wrong answer.

Answer: C — About Sixteen

171 → 2,719, a factor of 15.9. Because \(n \propto 1/\delta^2\), the multiple is the square of the ratio of effects: \((2000/500)^2 = 16\).

Why B is the trap: quadrupling is what you pay for halving the effect. Applying the halving rule to a quartering is the single most common sizing error, and it under-provisions an experiment by a factor of four.

A Number to Distrust: 80.8%

Plug the NSW study's own observed effect (\(d = 0.273\)) back into the power formula with its own sample sizes and you get an achieved power of 80.8%. It looks like a study designed exactly right.

It is the p-value in a costume. Post-hoc power evaluated at the observed effect is a strictly monotone function of the p-value: a significant result always returns high achieved power, a null one always returns low. It contains no information the p-value did not already carry.

Labs in this course — and papers you will referee — print ADEQUATELY powered from exactly this calculation. That verdict is not evidence the design was adequate. Do not quote it as if it were.

The informative version is the previous slide: power on the effect you would care about, before any data exists.

Achieved power computed in figures/_prep_data.py at the observed \(d = 0.273\), \(n_1 = 185\), \(n_0 = 260\).
2 min. Keep this slide even under time pressure. It is the single most-repeated error in student write-ups and in submitted manuscripts. If someone asks for the proof of monotonicity: achieved power is \(1-\Phi(z_{\alpha/2} - |d|\sqrt{n/2}\,)\) up to constants, and \(|d|\) is a monotone transform of the test statistic, hence of the p-value.

3  •  Advanced Treatment: CUPED

You cannot always buy sixteen times the users. You can sometimes remove the variance you already understand.

The Estimator

\[\theta = \frac{\text{Cov}(Y, X)}{\text{Var}(X)}, \qquad \tilde{Y}_i = Y_i - \theta\,(X_i - \bar{X})\]

\(Y_i\) — the in-experiment outcome
\(X_i\) — a pre-experiment covariate for the same unit
\(\theta\) — the slope from regressing \(Y\) on \(X\)

In words: strip out the part of each user's outcome that was already predictable before the experiment started. Heavy spenders keep spending heavily — that variation is noise with respect to the treatment.

Last month's purchases predicting this week's. Prior watch time predicting this week's. Baseline session count predicting today's.

Deng, Xu, Kohavi & Walker (2013), WSDM — Advanced Treatment, Chapter 9 §7.
2 min. Note that \(\theta\) is estimated from the pooled data, both arms together. Estimating it separately per arm reintroduces exactly the kind of arm-specific adjustment that can bias the contrast.

Why This Is Not Cheating

1. \(X\) is measured before assignment. It cannot have been affected by treatment, so you are not conditioning on a collider or on a post-treatment variable. The usual objection to "controlling for things" does not apply.

2. The adjustment is mean-zero in each arm. Under randomisation \(E[X_i - \bar{X}] = 0\) in treatment and in control alike, so \(\hat{\tau}\) stays unbiased. Any finite-sample imbalance in \(X\) is itself corrected rather than exploited.

So this is not a bias–variance trade. You get variance reduction for nothing, which is unusual enough that it is worth being suspicious of — and the two conditions above are exactly what buys it.

2 min. Push back if someone says "isn't this just regression adjustment?" — yes, essentially, and that is the point: it is Lin's (2013) regression-adjusted estimator arrived at from an engineering direction. What matters is that the covariate is pre-treatment.

The Mechanism, in One Picture

The tilt is removed; the scatter about the line is not. The treatment effect lives in that residual scatter and survives untouched — the predictable variation does not.

700-point random subsample of the lab's DGP, drawn for legibility. The full-sample figures are on the next slides.
1.5 min. Say explicitly that the vertical axis is unchanged between panels — the collapse is real, not a rescaling. The dashed line is the arm-pooled mean in both panels.

The Ceiling Is \(\rho^2\), and Nothing Beats It

\[\frac{\text{Var}(\tilde{Y})}{\text{Var}(Y)} = 1 - \rho^2(Y, X)\]

In words: CUPED cannot remove more variance than the covariate explains. At \(\rho = 0.9\) — routine for stable metrics like daily active usage — you remove 81%, the sensitivity of an experiment five times larger.

Which is why the engineering effort goes into finding a high-\(\rho\) covariate, not into the adjustment code.

2 min. The convexity is the teaching point: going from \(\rho=0.3\) to \(\rho=0.5\) buys you 16 points of variance; going from 0.7 to 0.9 buys you 32. Effort spent on the covariate pays increasingly, not decreasingly.

The State of the Art, and Its Price

CUPAC — Staponaitė et al., KDD 2025

An ML model predicts \(Y\) from many pre-period features.
38.5% average reduction in CI width — and 21.2× slower than baseline.

The graduate point. Variance reduction is a compute-versus-sensitivity trade-off, and at 100,000 experiments a year the compute term is not a rounding error. CUPED survives not because it is the most powerful method but because it is closed-form.

One caution on in-experiment covariates: anything measured after assignment can be affected by treatment, and adjusting on it reintroduces bias. Lin & Crespo are careful about which in-experiment quantities are admissible; a naive implementation would not be.

2 min. The last line is the one to press on in office hours — it is the seam where a variance-reduction technique becomes a post-treatment-conditioning bug, and it is the sort of thing that gets shipped.

Poll: Where CUPED Fails

You apply CUPED to the engagement metric for a brand-new feature and get a 2% variance reduction.

Most likely explanation?

A) The code is broken — CUPED always delivers 50%+
B) The pre-period covariate barely correlates with the outcome
C) The treatment effect is too small for CUPED to detect
D) The sample is too small for the adjustment to apply

Answer: B — There Is No Pre-Period

Reduction is exactly \(\rho^2\), so 2% means \(\rho \approx 0.14\). For a brand-new feature there often is no informative pre-period behaviour — nobody has used it before.

4  •  How a Correct Experiment Still Lies

Randomisation was necessary. Four reasons it was not sufficient.

Failure 1: Peeking

The experiment is planned for seven days. On day three the dashboard shows p = 0.03. The PM asks whether you can ship now.

The fixed-\(n\) test is valid only at the \(n\) you committed to. Every interim look is another chance for a random walk to wander below 0.05 and back out again, and the test statistic has no memory of the looks you already took.

Your lab simulates exactly this. Look at what it finds before you answer the PM.

1 min. Ask the room for a show of hands on whether they would ship. Most say yes, or say "check again tomorrow" — which is the same thing. Then run the next slide.

Fifty Looks Turn 5% Into a Third

Both arms convert at exactly 10%. Every "discovery" on this slide is fictitious. Bonferroni fixes the error rate and over-corrects — 1.9% against a 5% budget is power thrown away.

Left: lab Part 5, seed 123. Right: 1,000 simulations, seed 42; Monte Carlo SE on 32.4% is 1.5 pp — figures/_prep_data.py.
2.5 min. Trace the left panel with a finger: the walk crosses at n = 700, an experimenter ships, and the p-value ends at 0.276. They never see the walk back because they stopped looking. The right panel is the lab's Extension task — students who complete it should get 32.4% to within about 3 pp.

Formal Sketch: Always-Valid Inference

Bonferroni is a blunt instrument. The principled fix is a test that is valid at every stopping time — the mixture Sequential Probability Ratio Test (mSPRT), built on confidence sequences: intervals valid simultaneously at all \(t\), not just at a pre-specified \(n\).

\[p_t = \inf_{s \leq t} \tilde{p}_s\]

In words: your reported p-value is the running minimum of the adjusted p-values so far. It can only go down, so it cannot be gamed by choosing when to look — the number already accounts for every look you could have taken.

The guarantee: \(P(p_t < \alpha \text{ for any } t) \leq \alpha\) under the null, regardless of how often you check.

Johari, Pekelis & Walsh (2022), Operations Research 70(3) — Formal Sketch, Chapter 9 §7.
2.5 min. Offer the budget metaphor from the chapter: each peek spends some of your error allowance, and the sequential framework tracks the balance so you never overspend. Do not derive the likelihood-ratio process; the infimum and the guarantee are the two things they need.

The Right to Peek Has a Price

Always-valid methods need roughly 20–40% more sample than a fixed-horizon test for the same power.

That is the exchange rate, and it is worth stating as one: you buy the right to stop whenever you like, and you pay for it in users. Whether that is a good trade depends on how much organisational velocity the fixed horizon was costing you.

Microsoft's ExP implements mSPRT across all experiments — which is what makes a continuously-refreshing dashboard statistically honest rather than a trap. Spotify's open-source Confidence library is a public implementation.

Notice how this interacts with Section 3: CUPED's variance reduction can more than pay for always-valid inference's sample premium. The two techniques are complements, and platforms ship them together.

1.5 min. The last line is the synthesis worth landing: 82.7% variance reduction against a 20–40% sample premium is not a close call. This is why a modern platform can offer both a live dashboard and an honest p-value.

Failure 2: Sample Ratio Mismatch

You randomised 50/50. You observe 50.5 / 49.5. Something in the pipeline is broken.

Usual causes

Logging bugs that drop treatment rows · bot traffic clustering in one arm · a slower redirect on one variant · trigger conditions that interact with treatment

The correct response

Halt. Diagnose. Rerun. No adjustment repairs a broken randomiser — Microsoft's platform blocks the display of p-values entirely when SRM fires.

Fabijan et al. (2019) find SRM in roughly 6% of experiments at Microsoft and Booking.com. Industry alarms at p < 0.001, not 0.05 — the check runs on every experiment, and a 5% false-alarm rate would drown the channel. The \(\chi^2\) critical value at df = 1 is 10.83.

1.5 min. Emphasise that SRM invalidates everything, not just the affected metric — if assignment is broken you no longer have independence, so the identification argument from Section 1 is gone.

2 min. Do not give away the resolution yet — make them sit with the paradox for a beat. Someone will say "so raise the threshold," which is the answer you want to reject two slides from now.

Significance Is Not Severity

A 0.60 pp deviation trips the alarm at N ≈ 75,000. A 0.05 pp deviation trips it at N ≈ 10.8 million. A platform running 20-million-user experiments will alarm on deviations far too small to move any metric.

Do not raise \(\alpha\). That reintroduces the false negatives the strict threshold exists to prevent. Pair the test with an effect-size gate: what magnitude of misassignment could plausibly bias this metric, given its variance? Alarm on the conjunction.

The same statistical-vs-practical-significance move you make about the treatment effect, applied to the diagnostic itself.
2 min. This is the slide the undergraduate deck does not have. The transferable idea is that a diagnostic which is run on every unit of a very large population needs a magnitude criterion, not just a probability one — the same reason genome-wide association studies do not use \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Failure 3: You Optimised the Wrong Thing

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." — Chapter 2, via Meta's DAU/MAU gaming and Wells Fargo's unauthorised accounts.

A recommendation test lifts click-through by 40%. Ship it?

Not if the algorithm earned those clicks with clickbait. Long-run satisfaction and retention cratered. CTR became a target and stopped being a measure of value — and the experiment was correctly analysed the entire time.

This failure is invisible to every diagnostic on the previous four slides. Randomisation was fine. The estimator was fine. The question was wrong.

1.5 min. Ask who remembers Goodhart from Chapter 2 before you put the definition up. The pivot that matters: this is the one failure mode that no amount of statistical rigour detects.

The Defence: A Metric Hierarchy

Primary (success) metric — the one quantity the experiment is designed to move. Exactly one.

Guardrail metrics — system health that must not degrade: page load time, error rate, support ticket volume.

Counter-metrics — the cost of the primary's improvement. Conversion up, average order value down, revenue flat.

The decision rule: ship only if the primary metric improves and the guardrails show non-inferiority — no statistically significant degradation.

Note the asymmetry: the primary needs a significant improvement; guardrails need only the absence of significant harm. Those are different tests with different power requirements.
2 min. The footnote is the graduate detail — a non-inferiority test is not "we failed to reject", it is a test against a margin. An underpowered guardrail check reliably passes, which is precisely how a harmful feature ships with a clean scorecard.

Economist at Work: Wayfair's Demeter

The problem. The true LTV impact of a marketing change takes 60–90 days to materialise. Waiting two months per test destroys velocity — but optimising short-run clicks runs straight into Goodhart.

Surrogate indices

3–7 day leading indicators — add-to-cart velocity, initial session duration — forecast the 60-day revenue impact. Internal validation had accurate 60-day forecasts by day 14, when realised 14-day revenue was under half the cumulative impact.

Geo-experimentation

For direct mail and vendor networks, where user-level randomisation is impossible: 210 mutually exclusive geographic units, assigned by integer optimisation, lift estimated with synthetic difference-in-differences.

The surrogate is itself a Goodhart risk — you are now optimising a prediction of the thing you care about. It works only because the surrogate is audited on a rolling basis against realised 60-day outcomes.

Wayfair Tech Blog (2024–2025), Boston. Their 210 units return on the SUTVA slides in a moment.
2 min. The synthetic DiD reference forward-links to Chapter 11 on the 5200 track. Flag it: the geo design is a DiD design, and they will build one in three weeks.

Failure 4: The Assumption Nobody States

\[Y_i(D_1, D_2, \ldots, D_n) = Y_i(D_i) \quad \text{for all } i\]

In words: your outcome depends on your assignment and nobody else's. Stated once in a footnote, never checked — and on a platform it is routinely false.

Two-sided marketplaces

Discounted users book the listings control users would have booked. Holtz et al. (2025): at least 19.76% of Airbnb's individually-randomised estimate was interference bias.

Social networks

A treated user's new sharing feature puts more content in front of their control-group friends. The control arm is indirectly treated.

Resource constraints

Faster treated Uber drivers consume dispatches from a fixed rider pool, suppressing control drivers.

Holtz et al. (2025), Management Science 71(1):390–406 — a meta-experiment, i.e. an experiment on experimental designs.
2 min. Note what a meta-experiment is, because it is a lovely piece of design: they randomised the randomisation unit, running cluster-randomised and individually-randomised versions of the same test, and differenced the estimates.

Your Lab's Three Worlds

The mechanism, precisely. Both arms are hurt by scarcity; control is hurt more, because a control buyer hesitates and loses the item while a discounted buyer converts on the spot. That asymmetry is the bias.

10,000 buyers, 4,000 items, availability elasticity 1.0 for control against 0.3 for treated — Lab 9 Part 3, seed 42.
2.5 min. Stress that interference does not have to inflate: if scarcity multiplied both arms' probability by the same factor, an additive effect times a common factor below one would shrink, and you would understate. "Interference overstates" is a consequence of who loses the contested unit, not a law.

Two Biases. Only One Is the Decision.

Against the individual effect

0.1861 vs 0.1500 → bias +0.0361
19.4% of the naive estimate

Against the global rollout

0.1861 vs 0.1428 → bias +0.0433
23.3% of the naive estimate

The second one. Nobody ships a discount to a random half of the market forever. The business question is "what if we turn this on for everyone" — and that counterfactual has the same inventory serving twice as many boosted buyers.

In the A/B test, the control arm was quietly subsidising the treatment arm's supply. Both estimates sit inside the 15–25% band Holtz et al. report.

2 min. This distinction is the deepest idea in the section and it is 5200-only. The A/B test measures a direct effect at a fixed level of market saturation; the rollout changes the saturation. Formally it is the difference between a direct effect and a total (equilibrium) effect — and it is exactly why marketplace teams run switchbacks.

The Fix Is the Unit, Not the Sample Size

More users make a biased estimate more precise. You will get a tighter confidence interval around the wrong number.

Cluster randomisation — randomise geographies, not people. Wayfair's 210 units. Interference is contained within a cluster.

Switchback designs — alternate treatment and control across time periods. Uber and Lyft (Bojinov & Simchi-Levi 2020). The whole market is treated at once, so nobody is competing across arms.

Graph cluster randomisation — partition the social graph into loosely connected components and randomise those.

All three trade precision for validity: fewer effective units means wider intervals. That is the correct trade, and it is why a cluster design needs a power calculation on clusters, not on users.

1.5 min. The closing line matters for their labs and their final projects — the effective sample size in a cluster design is closer to the number of clusters than to the number of users, and students routinely size these wrong by two orders of magnitude.

The Base Rate You Cannot Design Away

Miller & Hosanagar (2025) analysed 2,270 experiments across 242 firms and found essentially no systematic p-hacking — shipping a false positive earns no revenue, so the market corrects fast. And yet Berman & Van den Bulte (2022) find 18–25% of "significant" A/B results are false positives.

Test 100 features; 70 are truly null. At \(\alpha = 0.05\) you expect 3.5 false positives — fixed by \(\alpha\) and the base rate alone. How many true positives you get is entirely a question of power:

Power on the 30 real effectsTrue +FDR
80%2413%
20%637%

Underpowered testing does not merely miss effects — it poisons the discoveries you do make. The false positives keep arriving at a rate \(\alpha\) does not care about while the true ones dry up.

The observed 18–25% sits between these rows, which tells you something about the power of a typical industry experiment.

Benjamini–Hochberg (Ch 8) controls FDR across metrics; most platforms now report adjusted p-values or test hierarchically — primary first, secondaries only if the primary holds.
2.5 min. Callback to the hook: this is what "100,000 tests, two-thirds null" implies about the shipped set. And it closes the loop with Section 2 — power is not just about your experiment, it is about the credibility of your whole portfolio.

Poll: The +12% Driver

A ride-hailing platform randomises drivers: treated drivers get a new dispatch algorithm. Treated drivers complete 12% more rides. Rider demand is unchanged over the test window.

Most likely problem?

A) Sample Ratio Mismatch — the split is broken
B) SUTVA fails — treated drivers took rides from control drivers
C) Peeking — they stopped the test early
D) Nothing — 12% is real and should ship
1 min. The load-bearing clause is "rider demand unchanged." Do not paraphrase it away when you read the stem aloud.

Answer: B — The Rides Came From Somewhere

Fixed demand plus a per-driver gain means the extra rides were taken from the control arm. The measured gap is inflated by exactly the amount control was depressed — this is World 2 against World 3 with drivers in place of buyers.

What a full rollout would deliver: possibly close to nothing, because then every driver competes on the same fixed demand. The fix is a switchback or a city-level cluster design — not a bigger sample.

5  •  Going Deeper: The Decision

A p-value ranks the surprise. Expected loss prices the mistake.

A Different Question

Frequentist

If the treatment did nothing, how surprising is this data?

Bayesian

Given this data, what is the probability B is better than A?

The second is the question the person deciding whether to ship actually asked.

Beta–Binomial conjugacy, uniform \(\text{Beta}(1,1)\) prior, \(s\) successes in \(n\) trials

\[\theta \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Beta}(1 + s,\; 1 + n - s)\]

In words: the prior contributes one imaginary success and one imaginary failure; the data supplies the rest. At \(n = 3{,}000\) the prior is invisible — which is exactly what a uniform prior should do.

Going Deeper, Chapter 9 §7. Connects to Chapter 5: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.
2 min. Anticipate "isn't the prior arbitrary?" — yes, and the honest response is that at this sample size it does not matter, while at \(n = 30\) it would, and then you would have to defend it. That is a feature: the Bayesian framework makes you say what you believed.

Expected Loss Is the Decision Rule

\[\mathbb{E}[\text{loss of shipping B}] = \mathbb{E}\big[\max(\theta_A - \theta_B,\, 0)\big]\]

In words: average, over everything you still believe could be true, the conversion rate you would give up by shipping B when A was actually better. Measured in units of the metric, not in units of surprise.

Expected loss, ship B

0.047 pp

Expected loss, ship A

1.077 pp

Permutation test

p = 0.230

Under the lab's rule — ship if \(P(B>A) > 0.95\) and expected loss < 0.001 — the verdict is do not ship. The loss criterion passes comfortably; the probability criterion does not. And the frequentist test agrees: fail to reject.

2 min. Say plainly that the two frameworks agreeing is the normal case at this sample size. The Bayesian machinery is not a device for reaching a different verdict — it is a device for stating the verdict in units a decision-maker can price.

Discussion: Would You Ship It?

Same scorecard: \(P(B>A) = 0.887\) · expected loss of shipping B = 0.047 pp · expected loss of shipping A = 1.077 pp · permutation \(p = 0.230\).

1. Ship, hold, or extend? Commit before you argue.

2. Your answer should change with the cost of the change. Does it? Compare a one-line copy edit against a re-architecture of checkout.

3. What would move you from "hold" to "ship" without collecting more data?

Note the asymmetry before you answer: shipping A when B is better costs 23 times what shipping B when A is better costs. The 0.95 threshold is not a statistical fact — it is a risk preference.

Key Takeaways

1

Randomisation kills one specific term. On Lalonde that term is −$16,999, and it flips the sign of the finding.

2

The permutation test is exact under the sharp null. The interval, not the p-value, is what goes to a decision.

3

\(n \propto 1/\delta^2\): quarter the effect, sixteen times the users. Post-hoc power adds nothing the p-value did not.

4

CUPED buys sensitivity for free up to a hard ceiling of \(\rho^2\) — 82.7% in your lab, exactly its bound.

5

A correct estimator is not a valid experiment: 50 peeks turn 5% into 32.4%; SRM significance is not SRM severity; interference overstates the global effect by 23%.

6

Decide in units of loss. A p-value ranks the surprise; expected loss prices the mistake.
