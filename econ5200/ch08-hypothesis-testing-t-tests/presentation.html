1  •  Two Philosophies, One Ritual

The p-value and the 0.05 threshold were invented by people who disagreed with each other

Two Hypotheses — and Which One You Assume

$$H_0: \mu_{\text{treat}} - \mu_{\text{ctrl}} = 0 \qquad\qquad H_1: \mu_{\text{treat}} - \mu_{\text{ctrl}} \neq 0$$

Reading it. The null is not what you believe. It is what you assume, so that you can compute how surprising your data would be if it held.

The courtroom. “Innocent” is the default. The data is evidence. A small p-value is “guilty beyond reasonable doubt.”

And its limit. An acquittal is not a proof of innocence. You never accept \(H_0\) — you fail to reject it.

2 min. The courtroom analogy is the one that sticks. Push on the second box: ask what a jury is actually asserting when it acquits. "The prosecution did not meet its burden" — which is a statement about the evidence, not about the defendant.

The p-Value

$$p = P\big(|T| \geq |t_{\text{obs}}| \;\big|\; H_0 \text{ true}\big)$$

Reading it. If the null were true and you repeated this study many times, you would see a result this extreme only \(100p\%\) of the time. It measures the compatibility of the data with a hypothesis. That is all it measures.

The prosecutor's fallacy

\(p = P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\), not \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\). Flipping the conditional needs Bayes and a prior — which the frequentist framework does not supply. The ASA published a formal statement in 2016 because this error is endemic.

3 min. The red-shirt example lands every time: 5% of people wear red shirts; the suspect wore a red shirt; is there a 5% chance he is innocent? Everyone sees it instantly in that framing and nobody sees it in the p-value framing. Say that out loud — the difficulty is the notation, not the logic.

The Same Number, Two Incompatible Jobs

Left: no alternative exists, and the p-value is the output. Right: the alternative is required, and the output is a decision.

1 min setup for the table that follows. Point at the left panel and ask "where is H1?" — there isn't one. Fisher never draws it. That single omission is the whole disagreement.

Fisher vs Neyman–Pearson

Fisher Neyman–Pearson
What is \(p\)?a continuous measure of evidencean input to a decision rule
Alternativenever specifiedmust be specified
Repetitionone experiment, judged after the factlong-run error rates over repeated sampling
Fixed in advancenothing\(\alpha\), \(n\), the rejection region

Applied practice reports a Fisherian continuous p-value and then uses it as a lever to trigger a Neyman–Pearson binary decision at 0.05. That is not a compromise — it is a category error, and it is why “\(p=0.049\)” versus “\(p=0.051\)” feels meaningful when it is not.

3 min. Neyman's own phrase was "inductive behaviour" — you are not learning whether a proposition is true, you are adopting a rule whose long-run error frequencies you can state. Fisher hated it. The two of them feuded in print for thirty years and the profession quietly adopted a hybrid neither of them would have signed. Wasserstein, Schirm and Lazar (2019) is the reference to give anyone who wants the full story.

2  •  The Most Powerful Test

Why the t-test is the optimal test and not merely the customary one

Every Decision Rule Fails in Two Directions

\(H_0\) is true \(H_0\) is false
Reject \(H_0\) Type I error — rate \(\alpha\) correct — probability \(1-\beta\) = power
Fail to reject correct Type II error — rate \(\beta\)

Drug approval. A false positive harms patients. Trials routinely run \(\alpha = 0.01\) or stricter.

A UI A/B test. A false positive ships a harmless button. \(\alpha = 0.05\) or even 0.10 is defensible.

\(\alpha\) is chosen, not discovered. It should price the asymmetry between the two errors.

2 min. The sentence to leave on the board: alpha is a price, not a constant. Anyone who defaults to 0.05 without an argument has skipped the only decision in the framework that is genuinely theirs to make.

The Neyman–Pearson Lemma (1933)

For simple \(H_0: \theta = \theta_0\) against simple \(H_1: \theta = \theta_1\), the most powerful test at level \(\alpha\) rejects when:

$$\frac{L(\theta_1 \mid \mathbf{x})}{L(\theta_0 \mid \mathbf{x})} > c, \qquad c \text{ chosen so that } P(\text{reject} \mid H_0) = \alpha$$

Reading it. Ask how much more likely your data is under \(H_1\) than under \(H_0\). If that ratio is large enough, reject. The lemma proves no other level-\(\alpha\) test has higher power — you cannot do better without either tolerating more false positives or assuming more about the data.

For two normal populations, the likelihood ratio test reduces algebraically to the t-test. It is not a convention. It is the optimal test — under its assumptions.

3 min. This is the slide that separates this room from the 3916 section. Emphasise the last four words: "under its assumptions." Everything in Sections 3 and 6 is an inventory of what happens when one of them fails, and now they know exactly what is being lost — not correctness, optimality.

Scope Conditions, Stated Honestly

The lemma covers simple vs simple — one specific alternative value. Real alternatives are composite: \(H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2\) is an infinity of them.

A test that is best against every alternative at once is uniformly most powerful. Getting one requires extra structure — typically a monotone likelihood ratio, via the Karlin–Rubin theorem.

Most two-sided problems in economics have no UMP test at all. The two-sided t-test is justified by unbiasedness and invariance arguments — not by the lemma alone. Say so when you write it up.

2 min. Worth being blunt: a lot of textbooks assert "the t-test is UMP" and it is false for the two-sided case. Karlin-Rubin gives you UMP one-sided tests in monotone-likelihood-ratio families; two-sided needs UMP-unbiased. Students who go on to a PhD sequence will meet this again in their first term and will remember that they heard it here first.

Look at the Distributions Before the Test Statistic

2 min. The top panel is the honest picture and the bottom panel is what the test sees. A randomly chosen trainee is not reliably richer than a randomly chosen control — the overlap is nearly total. What the test detects is a shift in the mean that is large relative to the standard error OF THE MEAN, which is a far weaker claim than "training worked for these people." Students conflate the two constantly.

Welch's t-Statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

Reading it. The numerator is what you saw. The denominator is how much what you saw would bounce around across repeated samples. A large \(|t|\) means the gap sits many standard errors from zero — unlikely if the null holds.

The df. Satterthwaite's approximation reduces the degrees of freedom when variances are unequal — a more conservative p-value. In practice: equal_var=False.

The rule. Always Welch. When variances are equal it agrees with Student's to three decimals. When they are not, Student's misleads. There is no real dataset where Student's is the right call.

2 min. Worth naming the historical oddity: Student's t is the default in most textbooks and the wrong default in every applied setting. The cost of Welch's when you did not need it is a fraction of a degree of freedom.

The Three Assumptions

1. Independence. Hardest to verify, most damaging to violate — you cannot check it from the data, only from knowing how the data was made. Clustered outcomes shrink the effective \(n\), so the standard error is too small.

2. Normality of the sampling distribution of the mean. The CLT covers large \(n\) — but “large” depends on skew, and the mean carries Chapter 4's breakdown point of \(1/n\). One observation moves \(\bar{x}\), inflates \(s\), and moves \(t\).

3. Equal variance. Almost never true for wages, prices or returns. Chapter 4's quick diagnostic: if one group's IQR is more than twice the other's, do not assume it.

2 min. Ask which is hardest to verify. Most say normality; the answer is independence, and the reason is that it is not a property of the numbers at all — it is a property of the sampling mechanism, which is Chapter 6's territory. Set up the poll by asking which of the three Welch's actually repairs.

Poll: What Does Welch's Actually Fix?

1 min. C is the tempting wrong answer because "Welch's is the robust one" is how people talk about it.

Answer: B — Variance, and Nothing Else

Welch's adjusts the standard error and the degrees of freedom for unequal variance. It does nothing about dependence, and nothing about heavy tails. A single $200,000 earner still moves the numerator and the denominator together, and Welch's has no defence against it.

Fixing the tails is Section 6's job — and it is not the fix most people expect.

1 min. Plant the Section 6 hook explicitly here. The four-method result later is much more surprising if they have already been told Welch's leaves the tail problem untouched.

4  •  How Big?

The p-value answers “is it noise”. It never answers “does it matter”.

A p-Value That Answered the Wrong Half

Is it real?

Leqembi (lecanemab), Clarity AD. n = 1,795. Effect on the CDR-SB cognitive scale with p < 0.001. FDA traditional approval, 2023.

Does it matter?

0.45 points on an 18-point scale — a difference most clinicians struggle to detect — against a documented risk of brain swelling and bleeding (ARIA).

The tiny p-value established that the difference was not noise. It said nothing whatsoever about whether the difference was worth the ARIA risk.

2 min. This is the cleanest case in the chapter because both numbers are unambiguous. p < 0.001 and clinically invisible are not in tension — they answer different questions. Say that sentence twice.

Cohen's d

$$d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_{\text{pooled}}}, \qquad s_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}}$$

Reading it. The gap, measured in standard deviations instead of dollars. Benchmarks \(d = 0.2\) / \(0.5\) / \(0.8\) are rough guides, not thresholds — a “small” effect on earnings can still be thousands of dollars.

The property that matters: unlike the p-value, \(d\) does not move when you add data. Ten million users make a 0.001% CTR difference significant at \(p<0.01\); \(d\) stays microscopic, because it was always microscopic.

2 min. The question is never "is the effect exactly zero" — it never is. The question is whether it is large enough to act on. Any test with enough n rejects; that is not a discovery about the world, it is a fact about the denominator.

5  •  Power, Written Out

The design decision you cannot make after the fact

Statistical Power

$$\text{Power} = 1 - \beta = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ true})$$

Reading it. The probability that a real effect actually trips your decision rule. Conventional target: 0.80. Four quantities determine it — fix any three and the fourth is pinned down.

Effect size \(d\) — bigger effects are easier to see

Sample size \(n\) — more data, less noise

Significance \(\alpha\) — a stricter bar costs power

Variability \(\sigma\) — noisier outcomes need more \(n\)

1 min. The third box is the one that matters for Discussion 1's callback: the group that demanded alpha = 0.001 has just bought themselves a much larger trial and did not say so.

The Power Function

$$\pi(\delta) = 1 - F_{t,\nu}\big(t_{\alpha/2,\nu} - \delta\big) + F_{t,\nu}\big(-t_{\alpha/2,\nu} - \delta\big), \qquad \delta = \frac{\mu_1-\mu_2}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$$

Reading it. \(\delta\) is the true effect measured in standard errors — the noncentrality parameter. At \(\delta = 0\) the two terms give exactly \(\alpha\): with no effect you reject only by chance, at the rate you chose. As \(\delta\) grows the distribution of \(T\) slides right, more of it clears the critical value, and power climbs toward 1. This is the curve statsmodels plots.

The approximation hiding in it. This expression shifts a central \(t\) by \(\delta\). The exact power uses the noncentral \(t\), \(F_{t,\nu,\delta}\), whose shape — not only its location — changes with \(\delta\). They agree in large samples and diverge exactly where you care.

3 min. This is the derivation the 3916 section never sees; they get the power curve as a picture with no formula behind it. Make sure they read pi(0) = alpha off the equation themselves — it is the cleanest sanity check in the chapter and it also explains why alpha is called the size of the test.

Where the Approximation Breaks

At \(\nu = 58\) the curves coincide. At \(\nu = 14\) — an \(n = 8\) per group pilot — they do not.

2 min. The practical rule: use TTestIndPower, which uses the noncentral t, and never hand-roll a normal approximation for a small-sample power calculation. The irony is that small samples are precisely where people improvise the formula, because "it's only a pilot."

What Precision Costs

$$n \approx \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \,(s_1^2 + s_2^2)}{\delta^2}$$

Reading it. \(\delta\) — the minimum effect you want to be able to detect — is squared in the denominator. Halve the MDE and you quadruple the sample. That single exponent is the whole economics of experimentation.

Poll: The Colleague at Your Desk

“My test came back p = 0.08. I'll just run it another week and check again.” What is the problem?

A) Nothing — more data is always better
B) Optional stopping — repeated checking inflates the true Type I rate
C) The p-value always falls as \(n\) rises

1 min. C is the interesting distractor — it feels right and it is false.

Answer: B — Optional Stopping

Check the p-value after every 100 observations and stop the first time it dips under 0.05, and the true false-positive rate can exceed 30%. The sample size has to be fixed before the experiment starts.

Why C is false: under a true null the p-value is uniform on (0,1) at every sample size. It does not drift downward with \(n\) — it just gets more chances to wander below 0.05 if you keep looking.

Legitimate early stopping does exist — sequential designs, group sequential boundaries. Chapter 9.

2 min. The uniformity of the p-value under the null is worth a moment; it is the single most useful fact in this chapter and most students have never been told it explicitly. It is also what makes the multiple-comparisons arithmetic in Section 7 exact rather than approximate.

6  •  Robust Alternatives — and What They Actually Relax

“Non-parametric” is not a synonym for “robust”

Yuen's Trimmed t-Test (1974)

Earnings, revenue and returns are not normal, and the t-test inherits its fragility from the mean. Yuen replaces sample means with trimmed means and sample variances with Winsorised variances:

1. Order each sample; drop the bottom and top \(\gamma\) fraction  •  2. Compute the trimmed means  •  3. Winsorise — replace each trimmed value with the nearest retained one — then take the variance  •  4. Apply the Welch formula to those quantities

Reading it. At \(\gamma = 0\) Yuen's reduces exactly to Welch's. At \(\gamma = 0.20\) it asks whether the central 60% of the two distributions differ, and the tails cannot vote. This is Chapter 4's trimmed mean, promoted from a description to a test.

What changes with \(\gamma\) is the estimand, not just the estimator. You are no longer testing a claim about mean earnings. Say which one you meant.

2 min. In SciPy: stats.ttest_ind(..., equal_var=False, trim=0.2). The estimand warning is the graduate-level point and the one people skip — a trimmed-mean test that rejects is not evidence about total earnings, which is often the quantity a policy actually cares about.

Randomization Inference

Under the sharp null \(H_0: Y_i(1) = Y_i(0)\) for every unit \(i\) — treatment changes no one's outcome — the treatment labels are exchangeable. So:

1. Compute the statistic on the observed data  •  2. Permute the treatment labels \(R = 9{,}999\) times  •  3. Recompute the statistic each time  •  4. \(p\) = the fraction of permuted statistics at least as extreme as the observed one

Reading it. You are not assuming a formula for the null distribution — you are building it out of relabellings of your own data. Note that the sharp null is stronger than “the average effect is zero,” which is what the t-test tests.

Young (2019, QJE) applied this to 53 experimental papers in the AER: classical standard errors frequently over-reject, and several conventionally significant findings lost significance under exact permutation. Report both.

2 min. The sharp-null point is subtle and worth thirty seconds: rejecting it says "some unit was affected", not "the mean moved". Fisher's null and Neyman's null are different nulls — which is the Section 1 quarrel resurfacing in a completely different place.

7  •  Multiplicity, Publication Bias, and What a p-Value Is Worth

From one test to a literature

Test Twenty Things and Something Will Light Up

$$P(\text{at least one false positive}) = 1 - (1-\alpha)^m$$

Reading it. Each test gets its own independent chance to fail. Twenty tests at \(\alpha = 0.05\): 64%. One hundred: 99.4%.

At the scale of thousands of concurrent experiments, dozens of false positives per cycle are not a risk. They are a certainty.

2 min. This is exact, not approximate, and the reason is the fact from Poll 3: under a true null the p-value is uniform, so P(p < 0.05) is exactly 0.05 every time. Independence across tests is what makes the product form work — and the correction methods on the next slides differ precisely in what they assume about that.

Two Corrections, Two Different Promises

Bonferroni — FWER

Test each hypothesis at \(\alpha/m\). Controls the probability of even one false positive. With \(m=20\): every test must clear \(p < 0.0025\).

Holds under arbitrary dependence. It assumes nothing about independence — which is exactly why it is so conservative.

Benjamini–Hochberg — FDR

Sort \(p_{(1)} \leq \cdots \leq p_{(m)}\); find the largest \(k\) with \(p_{(k)} \leq \frac{k}{m}\alpha\); reject \(1,\ldots,k\). Controls the expected proportion of your rejections that are false.

BH is the one with a dependence condition — independence or positive regression dependence. Otherwise use Benjamini–Yekutieli.

Bonferroni says “no false positives at all, and I accept that I will miss real effects.” BH says “some of my discoveries will be false; I control the rate.”

3 min. Correct the folk belief out loud: people routinely say Bonferroni "assumes independent tests". It does not — it follows from Boole's inequality and holds under any dependence structure whatsoever. The dependence condition belongs to BH. Getting this backwards is common enough in applied papers that they should be able to catch it as referees.

Bonferroni's bar is \(0.05/20 = 0.0025\). The stronger effect clears it; the weaker one, at 0.004, does not — and a genuine finding is discarded to guarantee that no false one survives. BH judges the second-smallest against \(\frac{2}{20}\times 0.05 = 0.005\), and it survives.

That single discarded finding is the entire practical difference between FWER and FDR.

2 min. Ask which they would use if they were Spotify running thousands of experiments. BH, obviously — and then ask which they would use for a drug safety panel. The answer flips, and the reason is the same cost asymmetry from Discussion 1.

The Winner's Curse

Netflix, KDD 2025 Best Paper (Applied Data Science). 300m+ memberships, thousands of concurrent experiments. The variants showing the strongest significance often do so because they caught favourable noise. Ship the smallest-p thumbnail out of 100 and the realised lift is smaller than the experimental lift — part of that lift was luck.

Netflix estimated that blindly shipping all “significant winners” produced aggregate business impact 20–40% below expectation.

Their fix — return-aware experimentation. Use 90% of units to select the winning variant, and the held-out 10% to estimate its effect. This is Chapter 6's train–test logic, applied to business decisions instead of model fitting.

Never evaluate a decision rule on the data you used to optimise it.

2 min. Worth naming the generalisation: selection on a noisy criterion biases the selected estimate upward, always, in every field. It is the same mathematics as regression to the mean and the same mathematics as post-selection inference in Chapter 16. Netflix just put a dollar figure on it.

Why Published Findings Fail

Ioannidis (2005). “Why Most Published Research Findings Are False” — a mathematical argument, not an empirical one: low base rates plus modest power plus publication bias.

Open Science Collaboration (2015). 100 psychology experiments from top journals; 36–39% replicated at the original significance level.

Camerer et al. (2016). 18 experiments from the AER and QJE; 61% replicated, at roughly 66% of the original effect size.

Brodeur, Cook & Heyes (2020). 21,000 hypothesis tests across 25 economics journals — and a two-humped camel in the distribution of \(z\).

2 min. The order matters: Ioannidis predicted it from arithmetic in 2005, and the measurements arrived afterwards and agreed with him. That is a rare thing in social science and worth saying.

Correcting for the Filter

Andrews & Kasy (2019, AER). If significant results are published at rate \(p_1\) and insignificant ones at \(p_0 < p_1\), the published record is a truncated version of the truth — and the truncation is invertible:

$$f(\hat{\theta}) \;\propto\; \frac{g(\hat{\theta})}{p(\hat{\theta})}$$

Reading it. \(g\) is what you can see — the distribution of published estimates. \(p\) is the probability that a study with that estimate gets published at all. \(f\) is what you want: every estimate, published or not. Divide the visible distribution by the chance of being visible.

Applied to experimental economics and psychology replications, this shrinks median effect sizes by roughly 30% — independently consistent with the I4R's 1.80 → 1.54 drop in mean \(t\).

2 min. The intuition is a survey with non-random non-response: you reweight by the inverse probability of responding. Here the "response" is publication. Note that estimating p(theta) is the hard part and Andrews and Kasy do it off replication samples and off systematic reviews — the formula is easy, the identification is not.

What Is a Published \(p < 0.05\) Worth?

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{(1-\beta)\,\pi}{(1-\beta)\,\pi + \alpha\,(1-\pi)}$$

Reading it. Of everything declared significant, what fraction is real? The numerator is true positives — power times the base rate \(\pi\) of hypotheses that are actually true. The denominator adds false positives, \(\alpha\) times the rate of false hypotheses. This is Bayes' theorem, and \(\pi\) is the prior the p-value never had.

With \(\pi = 0.10\) and power \(= 0.50\) — ordinary numbers for social science — the PPV of a \(p < 0.05\) result is 53%. Barely better than a coin flip.

2 min. This is the slide that closes the loop opened by Moderna. Nothing here says the p-value was computed wrongly. Every number in that literature is arithmetically correct and the posterior is still 53%. Note that the chapter's Section 8.2 warned you cannot invert the conditional without a prior — this is what happens when you supply one.

The Cheapest Fix Is Not a Stricter \(\alpha\)

2 min. Read across the figure with them. Raising power from 0.5 to 0.9 at pi = 0.10 takes the PPV from 53% to about 67% — real but not dramatic. Moving from pi = 0.10 to pi = 0.25 at the SAME power takes you to about 77%. The base rate does more work than the power does, which means the highest-return methodological reform is testing fewer, better-motivated hypotheses — theory, not statistics.

Six things to carry into Chapter 9

Chapter 8 in a Nutshell

1. The p-value is \(P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\) and belongs to Fisher. The 0.05 threshold belongs to Neyman and Pearson. Using one to trigger the other is the ritual, not the theory.

2. The t-test is the likelihood ratio test for normal populations — optimal, under its assumptions. Welch's is the default because equal variance is the assumption most likely to fail and the cheapest to drop.

3. Effect size answers “how big”; the p-value answers “is it noise”. Report both, always.

4. Power is a design decision made before data collection. At \(n=15\) the lab's analyst got the sign wrong and called the programme a failure.

5. “Non-parametric” is not “robust”. Choose the estimand and the statistic first; choose how to get its null distribution second.

6. Bonferroni controls FWER under arbitrary dependence; BH controls FDR under independence or positive dependence. And a published \(p<0.05\) at typical power and base rate is about 53% likely to be real.

2 min. If you are short on time, cut 3 and 5 in the room — they are the two the lab reinforces most directly, so students meet them again this week regardless.

Exit Ticket

A colleague shows you a result: p = 0.03, from a study of 40 people, testing twelve outcomes, with no pre-registration.

In three sentences: (1) what the p-value does and does not tell you; (2) the one correction you would apply, and what it controls; (3) what you would need to see before you believed the result.
