Summary Statistics Are a Compression

15 min for this section. Say the subtitle out loud — it is the graduate-level version of "always plot your data."

A Summary Statistic Is Lossy Compression

Anscombe’s eleven (x, y) points go in — 22 numbers. One comes out.

Like saving a photo as a tiny JPEG. The file is smaller and still looks like something — but you cannot get the original back, and what gets thrown away was chosen by the compression, not by you.

The danger: a decision-maker treats the one number as if it were the data. The shape of the cloud is exactly what the correlation discards.

Next: four different datasets that all compress to r = 0.816.

SAY: A summary statistic is lossy compression: twenty-two numbers in, one number out. You cannot get the data back from it. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY:

  • Summary statistics are wrong. (They are exact; the problem is what they leave out.)
  • The correlation tells you the relationship is linear. (It assumes linearity; it does not test it.)

Anscombe, 1973 — What Does Agreement Buy You?

Francis Anscombe built four datasets of eleven \((x, y)\) pairs. Run your usual summary on each file and you get the same answer four times:

What may you conclude about the four datasets’ shapes?

A) They are the same data up to row order
B) Same shape, possibly different scale
C) Nothing at all — shape is not in those statistics
D) Both must be at least approximately linear
  • 2 min.
  • Read the equation once: the OLS fit says every dataset starts at 3 when \(x=0\) and rises half a unit per unit of \(x\) — a true sentence about all four.
  • Then the poll: students click to vote on their devices; results appear on the next slide (down-arrow).
  • Expect a real D share — a high \(r\) feels like evidence of linearity. Anscombe II, on the next slide, is the counterexample.

Answer: C — Nothing at All

Agreeing on mean, variance, correlation and fitted line tells you nothing about shape. Plot first.

Those four statistics are a map from a dataset to \(\mathbb{R}^4\). The map is massively many-to-one, and in four slides annealing shows the preimage is rich enough to contain a dinosaur. Agreement in the image tells you nothing about the preimage.

Why D is the interesting wrong answer: \(r = 0.816\) is high, and high \(r\) feels like it certifies linearity. It does not. Anscombe II has \(r = 0.816\) and is a parabola. \(r\) measures how well a line fits, not whether a line is the right object.

SAY: Agreeing on mean, variance, correlation and fitted line tells you nothing about shape. Plot first. ⏸ 3 s. Then D: r measures how well a line fits, not whether a line is the right object.

DON'T SAY: High r means the relationship is linear.  |  Correlation is useless.

OLS Is Blind to Shape

OLS minimizes vertical distance; it never checks shape. The residuals do.

Anscombe dataset II · 11 points

What OLS does: picks the line that minimizes \(\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2\) — the squared vertical gaps. Nothing in that objective asks whether a line is the right shape.

Here it returns \(\hat y = 3.00 + 0.50x\), \(r = 0.816\) — the same answer as the other three datasets. Nothing failed.

The tell: residual signs in \(x\) order run − − + + + + + + + − −. Noise scatters signs; a curve arches them.

Add \(x^2\) and \(R^2 = 0.999999\). The data were a parabola all along.

SAY: OLS minimizes vertical distance; it never checks shape. The residuals do. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: OLS failed here. (It did exactly its job: the least-squares line for this data. The failure is choosing a line.)  |  A high r means the relationship is linear. (r = 0.816 on a perfect parabola.)

BACKGROUND: 2 min. Click 1: the fit matches the other three datasets. Click 2: the residuals and their signs appear — ask the room what pattern noise would give. Click 3: the quadratic. Numbers computed on Anscombe II: OLS 3.001 + 0.500x, r = 0.8162, r² = 0.666; quadratic −0.1267x² + 2.7808x − 5.9957, R² = 0.999999, largest residual 0.0024. The next slide shows all four datasets.

IF ASKED: “So should we always add x²?” → No: plot the residuals against x and let their pattern tell you the shape. That is the Ch 14 diagnostic.  |  “Why squared distances?” → Squaring makes the problem solvable in closed form and punishes big misses; it says nothing about shape either way.

The Same Four Numbers, Plotted

I — the textbook case

A linear cloud. The fitted line is the right model and the correlation means what you think it means.

II — a parabola

Deterministic curvature. \(r = 0.816\) and the linear model is simply the wrong functional form.

III — one outlier

Ten points on a perfect line, one off it. That single point tilts the slope away from the pattern the other ten agree on.

IV — one point does all the work

Every x is 8 except one. Delete that observation and the slope is undefined. The fit rests on n = 1.

Three of the four need a different model — and the numbers on the last slide could not tell you which.

  • Click four times: each click outlines one panel and adds its reading.
  • II is a parabola: the linear fit is simply the wrong model — it wants a nonlinear term.
  • III is a perfect line that one outlier has tilted — robust estimation (Ch 4).
  • IV is the pathological one: ten points share an x value, so the eleventh point alone determines the slope; delete it and the slope is undefined — leverage diagnostics (Ch 14).

Proof by construction: summary statistics can hide any shape you like.

  • The upgrade over Anscombe is not the dinosaur, it is the method.
  • Anscombe hand-built four counterexamples; annealing manufactures them on demand.
  • So the defence "well, Anscombe is a contrived special case" is dead — the special case is constructible for any target statistics you name.

How the Datasaurus Was Made: Simulated Annealing

The statistics are the constraint, not the goal: every accepted move leaves them unchanged, so any shape you name is reachable.

Step 3 is the fence. A move survives only if the mean, SD and correlation still match to two decimals. The shape changes; the summary never does.

Why “sometimes keep a worse move”? So the search does not get stuck. Early on (“hot”) it accepts detours often; as it “cools” it accepts them rarely — the name comes from annealing metal.

If a computer can generate counterexamples on demand, a summary statistic alone certifies nothing.

Matejka & Fitzmaurice, “Same Stats, Different Graphs,” CHI 2017: points are perturbed at random; a perturbation is kept only if the summary statistics are unchanged to two decimal places, and it is accepted when it moves toward the target shape or, with a probability that falls as the temperature decreases, when it does not.

SAY: The statistics are the constraint, not the goal. Every accepted move leaves them unchanged, so any shape you name is reachable. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: The algorithm searches for datasets with the same statistics. (It starts from one and never leaves the set; it searches for a shape.)  |  It runs until the statistics match. (They match at every step.)

BACKGROUND: 2 min. Six clicks: steps 2, 3 (with the fence box), 4 (with the cooling box), the loop closing on “repeat”, then the last line. Walk it clockwise, pointing at each box. Step 3 is the whole trick — say it twice. Then the cooling box: annealing is borrowed from metallurgy, slow cooling lets the structure settle. Close on the last line; it is why the previous slide matters for a graduate course: counterexamples are cheap.

IF ASKED: “Could you do it for regression coefficients too?” → Yes — any statistic you can compute can be held fixed in step 3.

Common Misconception

Mean, SD and correlation describe centre, spread and linear association — nothing else.

“I computed the mean, the standard deviation, and the correlation. I understand my data.”

Those three capture centre, spread, and linear association. That is the entire list. They are silent on multimodality, non-linearity, clusters, outliers, gaps, and shape.

The related trap: “correlation near zero means no relationship.” A perfect parabola \(y = x^2\) has correlation near zero — the halves cancel. That is Anscombe II wearing a different hat. Low \(r\) is a reason to plot, never a reason to stop.

SAY: Mean, SD and correlation describe centre, spread and linear association — nothing else. ⏸ 3 s. Low r is a reason to plot, never a reason to stop.

DON'T SAY: A correlation near zero means no relationship.

TIME: Mon ~3:42

BACKGROUND: 2 min. Say plainly: "plot first, summarise second" is the rule, and it inverts what most of you were trained to do, which is summarise first and plot only if a referee asks.

Section Two

Putting a Number on Distortion

Tufte's two ratios, and the six mechanisms they do and do not catch

20 min. This is the longest section and the one with the most transferable craft in it.

The Data-Ink Ratio

Data-Ink Ratio = ink that shows data ÷ all the ink. Erase ink that encodes no number.

\[\text{Data-Ink Ratio} = \frac{\text{ink that encodes data}}{\text{total ink in the graphic}}\]

Reading it: of everything the printer puts on the page, what fraction is carrying information? Points, bars and lines count in the numerator. Grid lines, borders, drop shadows, 3-D extrusions, background images and decorative fills go only in the denominator.

Ratio near 0 — a chart that is mostly decoration. Tufte's word for the decoration is chartjunk.

Ratio near 1 — erase anything you can erase without losing information. What survives is the data.

Tufte · data-ink · chartjunk

SAY: Data-Ink Ratio equals ink that shows data divided by all the ink. Erase ink that encodes no number. ⏸ 3 s. It measures efficiency, not honesty.

DON'T SAY: A high data-ink ratio means the chart is honest.

TIME: Mon ~3:50

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • This one measures efficiency, not honesty — a chart can be beautifully spare and still lie.
  • Say that explicitly, because students routinely conflate the two ratios.
  • The Lie Factor is the honesty instrument and it is next.
  • The eraser test makes it usable: point at a mark and ask “if I erase it, is a number lost?” If not, it goes.

The Lie Factor

Lie Factor = size of the effect shown ÷ size of the effect in the data. Honest is about 1.

\[\text{Lie Factor} = \frac{\text{size of effect shown in the graphic}}{\text{size of effect in the data}}\]

Reading it: a ratio of two proportional changes — how much bigger the picture got, over how much bigger the number got. Both are unitless, so the ratio is unitless. A value of 1.0 means the eye and the arithmetic agree.

1.0

honest

> 1.5

meaningful exaggeration

> 5.0

serious distortion

Below 1.0 is understatement — also a distortion, and the one nobody complains about.

SAY: Lie Factor equals the size of the effect shown divided by the size of the effect in the data — both as proportional changes. Honest is about 1. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: Tufte says 1.5 is the limit. (Tufte: 0.95–1.05. The 1.5 / 5.0 bands are ours, for teaching.)

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • Stress that the two inputs must be measured the same way — both as proportional change — or the ratio is meaningless.
  • And flag that these thresholds are conventions, not theorems.
  • We come back to "whose threshold is this?" hard in Section 4.

IF ASKED: “Where do 1.5 and 5.0 come from?” → This course's rules of thumb. Tufte's own tolerance is 0.95–1.05.

One Dataset, Two Baselines

Q1 $98M • Q2 $99M • Q3 $100M • Q4 $102M — identical numbers in both panels

1 min. Ask which panel goes in the investor deck and which one the regulator wants. Then ask which one is wrong. Neither is wrong. That is the uncomfortable answer and it is the correct one.

Do the Arithmetic

What the eye is given

Above the $96M cut, the Q1 bar is 2 units tall and the Q4 bar is 6. The drawn quantity grows by

\(\dfrac{6-2}{2} = 2.00 = 200\%\)

What the data says

Revenue goes from $98M to $102M, so the underlying quantity grows by

\(\dfrac{102-98}{98} = 0.0408 = 4.08\%\)

\(\text{Lie Factor} = \dfrac{2.00}{0.0408} \approx \mathbf{49}\)

The picture overstates the change by roughly fifty times. A trivial fluctuation, drawn as a revolution.

  • 3 min.
  • Walk the arithmetic on the board, slowly.
  • The move students miss is that the numerator is computed on the drawn heights above the truncation point, not on the revenue values — truncation is exactly the act of changing what "height" means.
  • Then note the second cost of truncation, which the wage figure later makes visible: a truncated axis does not merely exaggerate, it silently deletes every observation below the cut.

Six Mechanisms — The Geometry Three

Three lies bend the geometry: a truncated axis, dual y-axes, and area encoding.

1

Truncated axis. Move the baseline and every drawn height changes meaning. The commonest source of a high Lie Factor.

2

Dual y-axes. Two independent scales are two free parameters. Tune them and any two series can be made to move together — or apart. The reader cannot audit a scaling they cannot see.

3

Area encoding. Double a radius and you quadruple the area; a sphere gets eight times the volume. Bubble charts turn a linear difference into a quadratic impression.

What Follows for Your Charts

Use position — scatter, dot plot, line — whenever the reader must compare precisely.

Use colour for categorical grouping, not for quantity. It sits last on the list for a measured reason.

Use area only when approximate magnitude is genuinely all you need.

Avoid pie charts for exact comparison, and stacked bars whenever a non-baseline segment must be compared.

Think Like an Economist

Every chart makes three decisions. Interrogate all three before you trust one — including your own.

What to show

Which variables? Which window? Which units?

How to encode

Position, length, area, angle, colour?

What to omit

Missing data? Uncertainty? Context variables?

A chart is not a window onto the data. It is an argument made in visual rhetoric.

When Truncation Is the Honest Choice

Truncation misleads only when it makes a small change look large. Lie Factor above 2: annotate.

Truncation is not a sin. Zero is not sacred. Some quantities simply do not live near zero.

Body temperature in Fahrenheit lives in 97–104. A zero baseline spends 93% of the panel on a region no observation will ever occupy.

Policy rates live in 0–10%. Here the natural baseline is zero — and it has been binding, which is itself the interesting fact.

The test is not "does it start at zero." It is: does the truncation make a small change appear large? If the change is genuinely large within the relevant range, truncating is informative. If not, it is distortion.

Practical rule: Lie Factor above 2.0 → annotate the chart. "Note: y-axis does not start at zero." Disclosure is the floor, not the ceiling.

Common Misconception

On a log scale equal distances are equal ratios. Use it when data span orders of magnitude.

“Log scales are dishonest — they hide how big the change really was.”

Log scales answer a different question. On a linear axis, equal distances are equal differences — a $10 rise looks the same from $20 as from $100. On a log axis, equal distances are equal ratios, so a 50% rise looks the same wherever it starts.

Log is the honest choice for GDP, population, stock indices — anything spanning orders of magnitude, where constant growth should draw as a straight line.

Linear is the honest choice for our wage series, varying by 1.29×, where the structural breaks are the whole economic story and log compresses them away.

The choice depends on the question. There is no blanket rule, and anyone offering you one is selling something.

SAY: On a log scale equal distances are equal ratios. Use it when the data span orders of magnitude. ⏸ 3 s. Our wage series spans 1.29×, so linear.

DON'T SAY: Log scales are dishonest.

EDA as Forensics

Four Passes, In Order

EDA runs in order: structure, distributions, relationships, anomalies — before every model.

Tukey coined EDA in 1977 and described it as detective work — clues, leads, hypotheses that formal analysis will later test. The order matters: each pass tells you what to look for in the next.

1  Structure

Dimensions, types, tidiness. shape, dtypes, head

2  Distributions

Normal, skewed, bimodal? hist, kdeplot

3  Relationships

What moves with what, and how? pairplot, corr

4  Anomalies

Missing, duplicated, impossible. describe, duplicated

EDA = exploratory data analysis · Tukey

SAY: EDA runs in order: structure, distributions, relationships, anomalies — before every model. ⏸ 3 s.

Both Tools Have a Dial You Chose

Histogram

Bins the range, counts per bin. Concrete and honest about \(n\). Its shape depends on bin width — too few and detail vanishes, too many and noise wins.

Kernel density estimate

A kernel at every point, summed into a smooth curve. No bins — but a bandwidth instead. Narrow is noisy; wide can smooth a genuine second mode out of existence.

There is no free choice here. Bin width and bandwidth are design decisions in exactly the sense the axis range was — and a bandwidth that hides bimodality is a Lie Factor you cannot compute. Plot both, and vary the dial before you believe the shape.

  • KDE bandwidth is the sneakiest because it comes with a plausible default and no tick labels to audit it against.
