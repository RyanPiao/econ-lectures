And a Version You Cannot Catch by Reading the Axis

Reordering: the categories are sorted to tell a story instead of shown in their real order.

The dates on the x-axis were not in chronological order. They had been sorted so that bar heights descended. Put the dates back in time order and the decline disappears entirely.

Summary Statistics Are a Compression

And every compression is lossy in a direction someone chose

15 min for this section. Say the subtitle out loud — it is the graduate-level version of "always plot your data."

Anscombe, 1973

Francis Anscombe constructed four datasets of eleven \((x, y)\) pairs each. They agree on every statistic an analyst would normally report.

9.00

mean of \(x\)

7.50

mean of \(y\)

0.816

correlation

\(\hat y = 3 + \tfrac{1}{2}x\)

fitted line

Reading the equation: the OLS fit says every dataset starts at 3 when \(x=0\) and rises half a unit for each unit of \(x\). One sentence, four datasets, and it is a true sentence about all four.

  • 2 min.
  • Write the four numbers on the board before you show the plots.
  • Ask: "on this evidence, would you model these four the same way?" Nearly everyone says yes, and they are being reasonable — that is what makes the next slide land.

The Same Four Numbers, Plotted

  • 2 min.
  • Show it and say nothing for five seconds.
  • Then: dataset II is a parabola and the linear fit is simply the wrong model.
  • III is a perfect line that one outlier has tilted.
  • IV is the pathological one — ten points share an x value, so the eleventh point alone determines the slope; delete it and the slope is undefined.
  • Every one of those four stories is invisible in the numbers on the previous slide.

Then It Got Worse

Matejka & Fitzmaurice (2017) generalised Anscombe with simulated annealing: perturb points iteratively while constraining the summary statistics to stay fixed. Thirteen datasets came out.

54.26 / 47.83

means of \(x\), \(y\) — all thirteen

16.77 / 26.93

standard deviations

−0.06

correlation

Plotted, they are circles, stars, parallel lines, a cross — and a Tyrannosaurus rex. This is proof by construction: summary statistics can hide any shape you like.

We made one too. A husky: 142 points drawn for this course, then rescaled so its means, SDs and correlation equal the cards above to two decimals. It took seconds, not a search.

  • 2 min.
  • The upgrade over Anscombe is not the dinosaur, it is the method.
  • Anscombe hand-built four counterexamples; annealing manufactures them on demand.
  • So the defence "well, Anscombe is a contrived special case" is dead — the special case is constructible for any target statistics you name.
  • Click: the husky is ours. We did not anneal — we drew a shape and rescaled it to the five numbers, which is even cheaper. Matching the statistics is easy, so the statistics cannot tell you the shape.

Why an Economist Should Care

1,000 households. Income against consumption. You report \(r = 0.72\) and a slope.

Low-income cluster. Steep slope — nearly every extra dollar is spent. Marginal propensity to consume close to one.

High-income cluster. Flat slope — most of the extra dollar is saved. A different MPC entirely.

One OLS line through both returns an average of two slopes that describes neither group — and every policy simulation you run off that coefficient inherits the error. The scatter plot is what tells you to split the sample.

  • 3 min.
  • Make the stakes concrete: a stimulus-multiplier estimate built on the pooled MPC will misprice a transfer that is targeted at one of the two groups.
  • This is not an aesthetic complaint about charts — it is a specification error that a two-second scatter would have caught.

Common Misconception

Mean, SD and correlation describe centre, spread and linear association — nothing else.

“I computed the mean, the standard deviation, and the correlation. I understand my data.”

Those three capture centre, spread, and linear association. That is the entire list. They are silent on multimodality, non-linearity, clusters, outliers, gaps, and shape.

The related trap: “correlation near zero means no relationship.” A perfect parabola \(y = x^2\) has correlation near zero — the halves cancel. That is Anscombe II wearing a different hat. Low \(r\) is a reason to plot, never a reason to stop.

SAY: Mean, SD and correlation describe centre, spread and linear association — nothing else. ⏸ 3 s. Low r is a reason to plot, never a reason to stop.

DON'T SAY: A correlation near zero means no relationship.

TIME: Mon ~3:42

BACKGROUND: 2 min. Say plainly: "plot first, summarise second" is the rule, and it inverts what most of you were trained to do, which is summarise first and plot only if a referee asks.

Answer: C — Nothing at All

Agreeing on mean, variance, correlation and fitted line tells you nothing about shape. Plot first.

Those four statistics are a map from a dataset to \(\mathbb{R}^4\). The map is massively many-to-one, and annealing shows the preimage is rich enough to contain a dinosaur. Agreement in the image tells you nothing about the preimage.

Why D is the interesting wrong answer: \(r = 0.816\) is high, and high \(r\) feels like it certifies linearity. It does not. Anscombe II has \(r = 0.816\) and is a parabola. \(r\) measures how well a line fits, not whether a line is the right object.

SAY: Agreeing on mean, variance, correlation and fitted line tells you nothing about shape. Plot first. ⏸ 3 s. Then D: r measures how well a line fits, not whether a line is the right object.

DON'T SAY: High r means the relationship is linear.  |  Correlation is useless.

Section Two

Putting a Number on Distortion

Tufte's two ratios, and the six mechanisms they do and do not catch

20 min. This is the longest section and the one with the most transferable craft in it.

The Data-Ink Ratio

Data-Ink Ratio = ink that shows data ÷ all the ink. Erase ink that encodes no number.

\[\text{Data-Ink Ratio} = \frac{\text{ink that encodes data}}{\text{total ink in the graphic}}\]

Reading it: of everything the printer puts on the page, what fraction is carrying information? Points, bars and lines count in the numerator. Grid lines, borders, drop shadows, 3-D extrusions, background images and decorative fills go only in the denominator.

Ratio near 0 — a chart that is mostly decoration. Tufte's word for the decoration is chartjunk.

Ratio near 1 — erase anything you can erase without losing information. What survives is the data.

Tufte · data-ink · chartjunk

SAY: Data-Ink Ratio equals ink that shows data divided by all the ink. Erase ink that encodes no number. ⏸ 3 s. It measures efficiency, not honesty.

DON'T SAY: A high data-ink ratio means the chart is honest.

TIME: Mon ~3:50

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • This one measures efficiency, not honesty — a chart can be beautifully spare and still lie.
  • Say that explicitly, because students routinely conflate the two ratios.
  • The Lie Factor is the honesty instrument and it is next.
  • The eraser test makes it usable: point at a mark and ask “if I erase it, is a number lost?” If not, it goes.

The Lie Factor

Lie Factor = size of the effect shown ÷ size of the effect in the data. Honest is about 1.

\[\text{Lie Factor} = \frac{\text{size of effect shown in the graphic}}{\text{size of effect in the data}}\]

Reading it: a ratio of two proportional changes — how much bigger the picture got, over how much bigger the number got. Both are unitless, so the ratio is unitless. A value of 1.0 means the eye and the arithmetic agree.

1.0

honest

> 1.5

meaningful exaggeration

> 5.0

serious distortion

Below 1.0 is understatement — also a distortion, and the one nobody complains about.

SAY: Lie Factor equals the size of the effect shown divided by the size of the effect in the data — both as proportional changes. Honest is about 1. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: Tufte says 1.5 is the limit. (Tufte: 0.95–1.05. The 1.5 / 5.0 bands are ours, for teaching.)

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • Stress that the two inputs must be measured the same way — both as proportional change — or the ratio is meaningless.
  • And flag that these thresholds are conventions, not theorems.
  • We come back to "whose threshold is this?" hard in Section 4.

IF ASKED: “Where do 1.5 and 5.0 come from?” → This course's rules of thumb. Tufte's own tolerance is 0.95–1.05.

One Dataset, Two Baselines

Q1 $98M • Q2 $99M • Q3 $100M • Q4 $102M — identical numbers in both panels

1 min. Ask which panel goes in the investor deck and which one the regulator wants. Then ask which one is wrong. Neither is wrong. That is the uncomfortable answer and it is the correct one.

Do the Arithmetic

What the eye is given

Above the $96M cut, the Q1 bar is 2 units tall and the Q4 bar is 6. The drawn quantity grows by

\(\dfrac{6-2}{2} = 2.00 = 200\%\)

What the data says

Revenue goes from $98M to $102M, so the underlying quantity grows by

\(\dfrac{102-98}{98} = 0.0408 = 4.08\%\)

\(\text{Lie Factor} = \dfrac{2.00}{0.0408} \approx \mathbf{49}\)

The picture overstates the change by roughly fifty times. A trivial fluctuation, drawn as a revolution.

  • 3 min.
  • Walk the arithmetic on the board, slowly.
  • The move students miss is that the numerator is computed on the drawn heights above the truncation point, not on the revenue values — truncation is exactly the act of changing what "height" means.
  • Then note the second cost of truncation, which the wage figure later makes visible: a truncated axis does not merely exaggerate, it silently deletes every observation below the cut.

Six Mechanisms — The Geometry Three

Three lies bend the geometry: a truncated axis, dual y-axes, and area encoding.

1

Truncated axis. Move the baseline and every drawn height changes meaning. The commonest source of a high Lie Factor.

2

Dual y-axes. Two independent scales are two free parameters. Tune them and any two series can be made to move together — or apart. The reader cannot audit a scaling they cannot see.

3

Area encoding. Double a radius and you quadruple the area; a sphere gets eight times the volume. Bubble charts turn a linear difference into a quadratic impression.

SAY: Three lies bend the geometry: a truncated axis, dual y-axes, and area encoding. ⏸ 3 s. A ruler catches all three.

DON'T SAY: An axis that goes below zero is truncated.

TIME: Mon ~4:01

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • These three are geometric — the picture's proportions no longer match the numbers' proportions, and in principle a careful reader with a ruler could catch all three.
  • The next three are worse, because a ruler will not help.

IF ASKED: “So how big should the second circle be?” → Twice the area, so the radius grows by √2 ≈ 1.41, not 2. Doubling the radius gives four times the area.

Six Mechanisms — The Three a Ruler Misses

Three lies a ruler misses: a misleading map, a mislabelled metric, missing context.

4

Misleading maps. A choropleth weights by acreage. Our hook. The fix is a cartogram — distort the geography so area encodes the variable you actually mean.

5

Metric mislabelling. A widely shared Financial Times chart on AI hiring, labelled “hires”, dipped below zero — impossible for a count. The series was net employment change. The geometry was fine; the label lied.

6

Missing context. No uncertainty band implies a precision you have not got. No mention of last month's revision implies a change that may not have happened.

SAY: Three lies a ruler misses: a misleading map, a mislabelled metric, missing context. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: The FT chart was from May 2025 / about the top 15 AI companies. (Unverified — removed from the textbook 19 Sep. The mechanism stands; the provenance does not.)

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • Mechanism 5 is the one to dwell on for this cohort: a gross-flow label on a net-flow series turns a structural contraction into a hiring slowdown, and no amount of chart-craft catches it.
  • Only reading the variable definition does.
  • That is a data-provenance skill, not a design skill.
  • Mechanism 6 seeds Ch 7 — from bootstrap intervals onward, every estimate you show gets a band.

IF ASKED: “How do you catch mislabelling?” → Read the variable's definition — FRED gives one on every series page.

Poll: Compute It

Lie Factor ≈ 49: the bars grow 200%, revenue grows 4.08%. 2.00 ÷ 0.0408.

Encoding Is Measured, Not Debated

Readers judge position most accurately, then length, then angle and area; colour last. Measured, not taste.

Cleveland & McGill (1984), controlled experiments; replicated by Heer & Bostock (2010) on Mechanical Turk, 186 Turkers

SAY: Readers judge position most accurately, then length, then angle and area; colour last. Measured, not taste. ⏸ 3 s.

TIME: Mon ~4:07

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • The move that matters for a graduate audience: this is not taste.
  • Cleveland and McGill ran experiments and measured error rates, so "I prefer bar charts to pie charts" is not an aesthetic preference, it is a claim with an effect size behind it.
  • Heer and Bostock replicated it on Mechanical Turk with 186 workers and reproduced the ranking: area performed worse than angle, and both significantly worse than position.
  • They did not find angle worse than length.

What Follows for Your Charts

Use position — scatter, dot plot, line — whenever the reader must compare precisely.

Use colour for categorical grouping, not for quantity. It sits last on the list for a measured reason.

Use area only when approximate magnitude is genuinely all you need.

Avoid pie charts for exact comparison, and stacked bars whenever a non-baseline segment must be compared.

Das & Mueller (2025) now automate part of this: MisVisFix flags truncated axes (F1 0.98), 3-D effects (0.97) and dual axes (0.96), and highlights the offending regions.

  • 2 min.
  • Close the section on the automation note but do not oversell it — the tool catches geometry, which is mechanisms 1 to 3.
  • Nothing in it catches a gross-flow label on a net-flow series.
  • The judgement does not automate.

Think Like an Economist

Every chart makes three decisions. Interrogate all three before you trust one — including your own.

What to show

Which variables? Which window? Which units?

How to encode

Position, length, area, angle, colour?

What to omit

Missing data? Uncertainty? Context variables?

A chart is not a window onto the data. It is an argument made in visual rhetoric.

2 min. The third column is the one people skip, and it is where the serious failures live — omission leaves no trace on the page for anyone to audit.

When Truncation Is the Honest Choice

Truncation misleads only when it makes a small change look large. Lie Factor above 2: annotate.

Truncation is not a sin. Zero is not sacred. Some quantities simply do not live near zero.

Body temperature in Fahrenheit lives in 97–104. A zero baseline spends 93% of the panel on a region no observation will ever occupy.

Policy rates live in 0–10%. Here the natural baseline is zero — and it has been binding, which is itself the interesting fact.

The test is not "does it start at zero." It is: does the truncation make a small change appear large? If the change is genuinely large within the relevant range, truncating is informative. If not, it is distortion.

Practical rule: Lie Factor above 2.0 → annotate the chart. "Note: y-axis does not start at zero." Disclosure is the floor, not the ceiling.

SAY: Truncation misleads only when it makes a small change look large. If the Lie Factor is above 2, annotate. ⏸ 3 s. Disclosure is the floor, not the ceiling.

DON'T SAY: A truncated axis is always a lie.  |  A footnote makes it honest.

Common Misconception

On a log scale equal distances are equal ratios. Use it when data span orders of magnitude.

“Log scales are dishonest — they hide how big the change really was.”

Log scales answer a different question. On a linear axis, equal distances are equal differences — a $10 rise looks the same from $20 as from $100. On a log axis, equal distances are equal ratios, so a 50% rise looks the same wherever it starts.

Log is the honest choice for GDP, population, stock indices — anything spanning orders of magnitude, where constant growth should draw as a straight line.

Linear is the honest choice for our wage series, varying by 1.29×, where the structural breaks are the whole economic story and log compresses them away.

The choice depends on the question. There is no blanket rule, and anyone offering you one is selling something.

SAY: On a log scale equal distances are equal ratios. Use it when the data span orders of magnitude. ⏸ 3 s. Our wage series spans 1.29×, so linear.

DON'T SAY: Log scales are dishonest.

Section Four

EDA as Forensics

Tukey called it detective work. In production it never stops running.

12 min. Shift of register here: sections 1 to 3 were about charts you show other people. This one is about charts you show yourself, before anyone else sees anything.

Four Passes, In Order

EDA runs in order: structure, distributions, relationships, anomalies — before every model.

Tukey coined EDA in 1977 and described it as detective work — clues, leads, hypotheses that formal analysis will later test. The order matters: each pass tells you what to look for in the next.

1  Structure

Dimensions, types, tidiness. shape, dtypes, head

2  Distributions

Normal, skewed, bimodal? hist, kdeplot

3  Relationships

What moves with what, and how? pairplot, corr

4  Anomalies

Missing, duplicated, impossible. describe, duplicated

EDA = exploratory data analysis · Tukey

SAY: EDA runs in order: structure, distributions, relationships, anomalies — before every model. ⏸ 3 s.

TIME: Wed 23 — Monday stops after slide 36 (or 35 if behind)

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • Emphasise that this is not a warm-up you do before the real work.
  • Pass 3 is what tells you whether OLS in Ch 12 is even the right object; pass 4 is what stops you fitting a model to an encoding error.

Both Tools Have a Dial You Chose

Histogram

Bins the range, counts per bin. Concrete and honest about \(n\). Its shape depends on bin width — too few and detail vanishes, too many and noise wins.

Kernel density estimate

A kernel at every point, summed into a smooth curve. No bins — but a bandwidth instead. Narrow is noisy; wide can smooth a genuine second mode out of existence.

There is no free choice here. Bin width and bandwidth are design decisions in exactly the sense the axis range was — and a bandwidth that hides bimodality is a Lie Factor you cannot compute. Plot both, and vary the dial before you believe the shape.

  • 3 min.
  • This is the slide that ties Section 4 back to Sections 2 and 3, so do not skip it for time.
  • The through-line of the whole lecture: every visual tool has a parameter someone chose, and the professional obligation is to know which parameter you chose and why.
  • KDE bandwidth is the sneakiest because it comes with a plausible default and no tick labels to audit it against.

Why 10 Planted Becomes 12 Found

The generator negates 10 GDP values, then converts 15 life expectancies to months, and then duplicates 20 randomly chosen rows. Some of those duplicated rows were already corrupt. The defect is copied along with the row.

Negative GDP: 10 planted, 12 present

Life expectancy in months: 15 planted, 16 present

The transferable lesson: in a real pipeline the count of corrupted rows is not the count of corrupting events. Defects propagate through joins, appends and backfills, and the multiplier is whatever the merge did. Never reconcile "we know about 10 bad rows" against a scan that returns 12 by assuming the scan is wrong.

  • 3 min.

  • Ask: if your upstream team tells you they injected exactly N bad records, and your audit finds N + k, what are the candidate explanations?
  • Answers: propagation through a join, a retry that double-wrote, or your detector's threshold is catching something else.
  • All three are worth ruling out separately, and none of them is "the audit is buggy."

Answer: B — Whose Threshold Is 0.10?

Those three columns were never touched. With 138 training and 92 inference rows spread over 10 bins, the sampling variation in the bin proportions alone produces PSI in the low tenths. The statistic is behaving correctly; the cut-off was imported without its sample size.

0.10 and 0.25 are credit-scoring folklore, calibrated on populations of tens of thousands. Carried onto \(n \approx 100\) without recalibration, they are Chapter 2's base-year problem again: a default you did not choose is still a choice you own.

SAY: The threshold was imported without its sample size. At this n, noise alone clears 0.10.

What a Usable Monitor Looks Like

The gdp shift is unmistakable at PSI 2.46 — nearly twenty-five times the “moderate” line. That detection is not in doubt. The problem is everything else the same rule fires on.

Bootstrap the null. Resample train against train, no shift, and read off the PSI you get from noise at your \(n\) and bin count. That distribution is your threshold. (Ch 7.)

Report the value, not the verdict. “PSI = 0.164” survives review. “SIGNIFICANT SHIFT” hides the number that would have let a reader disagree.

Plot the overlay anyway. A KDE of train against inference shows you where the mass moved. A scalar never will — which is Anscombe, one last time, wearing production clothes.

  • 3 min.
  • Land the closing callback deliberately: PSI is a summary statistic, so everything Section 1 said about summary statistics applies to it.
  • Two very different drifts — a uniform level shift and a new mode appearing in one tail — can return the same PSI.
  • The overlay plot is the Anscombe diagnostic for distribution monitoring, and it costs one line.
