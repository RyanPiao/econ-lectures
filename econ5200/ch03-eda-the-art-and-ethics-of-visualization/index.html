Every County Was Coloured Correctly

November 2024. A county-level election map floods every timeline. Red from Appalachia to the Rockies; blue reduced to specks. Commentators read it as a mandate.

And a Version You Cannot Catch by Reading the Axis

May 2020. Georgia's Department of Public Health published confirmed COVID-19 cases for the five worst-affected counties. The bars descended left to right. The state looked to be recovering.

Section One

Summary Statistics Are a Compression

And every compression is lossy in a direction someone chose

15 min for this section. Say the subtitle out loud — it is the graduate-level version of "always plot your data."

Anscombe, 1973

Francis Anscombe constructed four datasets of eleven \((x, y)\) pairs each. They agree on every statistic an analyst would normally report.

9.00

mean of \(x\)

7.50

mean of \(y\)

0.816

correlation

\(\hat y = 3 + \tfrac{1}{2}x\)

fitted line

Reading the equation: the OLS fit says every dataset starts at 3 when \(x=0\) and rises half a unit for each unit of \(x\). One sentence, four datasets, and it is a true sentence about all four.

2 min. Write the four numbers on the board before you show the plots. Ask: "on this evidence, would you model these four the same way?" Nearly everyone says yes, and they are being reasonable — that is what makes the next slide land.

The Same Four Numbers, Plotted

2 min. Show it and say nothing for five seconds. Then: dataset II is a parabola and the linear fit is simply the wrong model. III is a perfect line that one outlier has tilted. IV is the pathological one — ten points share an x value, so the eleventh point alone determines the slope; delete it and the slope is undefined. Every one of those four stories is invisible in the numbers on the previous slide.

Anscombe rounded his construction, which is why the variances differ in the third decimal — that residue is real, and quoting "identical" without the caveat would be a small lie of exactly the kind this chapter is about.

Then It Got Worse

Matejka & Fitzmaurice (2017) generalised Anscombe with simulated annealing: perturb points iteratively while constraining the summary statistics to stay fixed. Thirteen datasets came out.

54.26 / 47.83

means of \(x\), \(y\) — all thirteen

16.77 / 26.93

standard deviations

−0.06

correlation

Plotted, they are circles, stars, parallel lines, a cross — and a Tyrannosaurus rex. This is proof by construction: summary statistics can hide any shape you like.

2 min. The upgrade over Anscombe is not the dinosaur, it is the method. Anscombe hand-built four counterexamples; annealing manufactures them on demand. So the defence "well, Anscombe is a contrived special case" is dead — the special case is constructible for any target statistics you name.

Why an Economist Should Care

1,000 households. Income against consumption. You report \(r = 0.72\) and a slope.

Low-income cluster. Steep slope — nearly every extra dollar is spent. Marginal propensity to consume close to one.

High-income cluster. Flat slope — most of the extra dollar is saved. A different MPC entirely.

One OLS line through both returns an average of two slopes that describes neither group — and every policy simulation you run off that coefficient inherits the error. The scatter plot is what tells you to split the sample.

3 min. Make the stakes concrete: a stimulus-multiplier estimate built on the pooled MPC will misprice a transfer that is targeted at one of the two groups. This is not an aesthetic complaint about charts — it is a specification error that a two-second scatter would have caught.

The Rate Is Itself a Summary Statistic

2010 to 2019. A dashboard showing one series declares victory.

Unemployment rate

9.6% → 3.5%

share of the labour force seeking work

Labour force participation

64.7% → 63.1%

share of working-age population in the labour force

People who stop looking leave the denominator of the first series entirely. Workforce exit and genuine recovery push the unemployment rate the same direction. Only plotting both separates them.

3 min. This is the bridge from Ch 2. The unemployment rate is a constructed index with a chosen denominator, exactly like CPI has a chosen basket. Anscombe applies to it: the rate compresses two different labour-market worlds into one number, and the compression is lossy in a direction that flatters whoever is in office.

Common Misconception

“I computed the mean, the standard deviation, and the correlation. I understand my data.”

Those three capture centre, spread, and linear association. That is the entire list. They are silent on multimodality, non-linearity, clusters, outliers, gaps, and shape.

The related trap: “correlation near zero means no relationship.” A perfect parabola \(y = x^2\) has correlation near zero — the halves cancel. That is Anscombe II wearing a different hat. Low \(r\) is a reason to plot, never a reason to stop.

2 min. Say plainly: "plot first, summarise second" is the rule, and it inverts what most of you were trained to do, which is summarise first and plot only if a referee asks.

Poll: What Does Agreement Buy You?

Two datasets agree on mean, variance, correlation, and the fitted regression line, to three decimals.

What may you conclude about their shapes?

A) They are the same data up to row order
B) Same shape, possibly different scale
C) Nothing at all — shape is not in those statistics
D) Both must be at least approximately linear
1 min. Students click to vote on their devices; results appear on the next slide. Expect a real D share — a high \(r\) feels like evidence of linearity, and Anscombe II is precisely the counterexample they have just seen.

Answer: C — Nothing at All

Those four statistics are a map from a dataset to \(\mathbb{R}^4\). The map is massively many-to-one, and annealing shows the preimage is rich enough to contain a dinosaur. Agreement in the image tells you nothing about the preimage.

Why D is the interesting wrong answer: \(r = 0.816\) is high, and high \(r\) feels like it certifies linearity. It does not. Anscombe II has \(r = 0.816\) and is a parabola. \(r\) measures how well a line fits, not whether a line is the right object.

Section Two

Putting a Number on Distortion

Tufte's two ratios, and the six mechanisms they do and do not catch

20 min. This is the longest section and the one with the most transferable craft in it.

The Data-Ink Ratio

\[\text{Data-Ink Ratio} = \frac{\text{ink that encodes data}}{\text{total ink in the graphic}}\]

Reading it: of everything the printer puts on the page, what fraction is carrying information? Points, bars and lines count in the numerator. Grid lines, borders, drop shadows, 3-D extrusions, background images and decorative fills go only in the denominator.

Ratio near 0 — a chart that is mostly decoration. Tufte's word for the decoration is chartjunk.

Ratio near 1 — erase anything you can erase without losing information. What survives is the data.

3 min. This one measures efficiency, not honesty — a chart can be beautifully spare and still lie. Say that explicitly, because students routinely conflate the two ratios. The Lie Factor is the honesty instrument and it is next.

The Lie Factor

\[\text{Lie Factor} = \frac{\text{size of effect shown in the graphic}}{\text{size of effect in the data}}\]

Reading it: a ratio of two proportional changes — how much bigger the picture got, over how much bigger the number got. Both are unitless, so the ratio is unitless. A value of 1.0 means the eye and the arithmetic agree.

1.0

honest

> 1.5

meaningful exaggeration

> 5.0

serious distortion

Below 1.0 is understatement — also a distortion, and the one nobody complains about.

3 min. Stress that the two inputs must be measured the same way — both as proportional change — or the ratio is meaningless. And flag that these thresholds are conventions, not theorems. We come back to "whose threshold is this?" hard in Section 4.

One Dataset, Two Baselines

Q1 $98M • Q2 $99M • Q3 $100M • Q4 $102M — identical numbers in both panels

1 min. Ask which panel goes in the investor deck and which one the regulator wants. Then ask which one is wrong. Neither is wrong. That is the uncomfortable answer and it is the correct one.

Do the Arithmetic

What the eye is given

Above the $96M cut, the Q1 bar is 2 units tall and the Q4 bar is 6. The drawn quantity grows by

\(\dfrac{6-2}{2} = 2.00 = 200\%\)

What the data says

Revenue goes from $98M to $102M, so the underlying quantity grows by

\(\dfrac{102-98}{98} = 0.0408 = 4.08\%\)

\(\text{Lie Factor} = \dfrac{2.00}{0.0408} \approx \mathbf{49}\)

The picture overstates the change by roughly fifty times. A trivial fluctuation, drawn as a revolution.

3 min. Walk the arithmetic on the board, slowly. The move students miss is that the numerator is computed on the drawn heights above the truncation point, not on the revenue values — truncation is exactly the act of changing what "height" means. Then note the second cost of truncation, which the wage figure later makes visible: a truncated axis does not merely exaggerate, it silently deletes every observation below the cut.

This Is the Norm, Not the Edge Case

86%

of audited annual reports

An audit of 50 publicly traded companies found that 86% contained at least one broken chart. The single most common defect: a truncated y-axis on revenue and earnings.

Read the statistic precisely. The audit counted broken charts. It did not count charts above some Lie Factor. The 1.5 and 5.0 cut-offs are ours, for teaching — not the source's.

CPA Journal, "The Danger of Bad Charts," October 2025.

2 min. The grey box is not pedantry, it is the habit the whole chapter is trying to build: when you put someone else's number on a slide, state what it actually counted. A student who quotes "86% of corporate charts exceed a Lie Factor of 1.5" has invented a finding. Do not let that pass in the midterm.

Six Mechanisms — The Geometry Three

1

Truncated axis. Move the baseline and every drawn height changes meaning. The commonest source of a high Lie Factor.

2

Dual y-axes. Two independent scales are two free parameters. Tune them and any two series can be made to move together — or apart. The reader cannot audit a scaling they cannot see.

3

Area encoding. Double a radius and you quadruple the area; a sphere gets eight times the volume. Bubble charts turn a linear difference into a quadratic impression.

3 min. These three are geometric — the picture's proportions no longer match the numbers' proportions, and in principle a careful reader with a ruler could catch all three. The next three are worse, because a ruler will not help.

Six Mechanisms — The Three a Ruler Misses

4

Misleading maps. A choropleth weights by acreage. Our hook. The fix is a cartogram — distort the geography so area encodes the variable you actually mean.

5

Metric mislabelling. May 2025: an FT chart titled "Hires per month" for the top 15 US AI companies dipped below zero — impossible for a count. The series was net employment change. The geometry was fine; the label lied.

6

Missing context. No uncertainty band implies a precision you have not got. No mention of last month's revision implies a change that may not have happened.

3 min. Mechanism 5 is the one to dwell on for this cohort: a gross-flow label on a net-flow series turns a structural contraction into a hiring slowdown, and no amount of chart-craft catches it. Only reading the variable definition does. That is a data-provenance skill, not a design skill. Mechanism 6 seeds Ch 7 — from bootstrap intervals onward, every estimate you show gets a band.

Poll: Compute It

Revenue runs $98M → $102M across the year. The y-axis is cut at $96M, so the Q4 bar draws three times the height of the Q1 bar.

What is the Lie Factor?

A) About 3 — the bar tripled
B) About 4 — revenue rose about 4%
C) About 49
D) About 200 — the drawn height rose 200%
1 min. Click to vote. A and D are each half the calculation — A reports only the visual ratio, D only the visual percentage. Both are the error of forgetting to divide by the data effect.

Answer: C — About 49

Visual effect \(= 200\%\). Data effect \(= 4.08\%\). The ratio is \(200/4.08 \approx 49\). Both inputs must be proportional changes; mixing a ratio with a percentage is what produces A and D.

The habit worth keeping: a Lie Factor near 50 is not a rhetorical flourish — it is a computed quantity you can put in a referee report, an audit memo, or a footnote. That is why Tufte bothered to define it.

Encoding Is Measured, Not Debated

Cleveland & McGill (1984), controlled experiments; replicated by Heer & Bostock (2010), n = 688

3 min. The move that matters for a graduate audience: this is not taste. Cleveland and McGill ran experiments and measured error rates. Angle judgements carry roughly 1.8 times the error of position judgements. So "I prefer bar charts to pie charts" is not an aesthetic preference, it is a claim with an effect size behind it. Heer and Bostock replicated it on Mechanical Turk and also found stacked bars add error, because comparing non-baseline segments requires mental subtraction.

What Follows for Your Charts

Use position — scatter, dot plot, line — whenever the reader must compare precisely.

Use colour for categorical grouping, not for quantity. It sits last on the list for a measured reason.

Use area only when approximate magnitude is genuinely all you need.

Avoid pie charts for exact comparison, and stacked bars whenever a non-baseline segment must be compared.

Mueller et al. (2025) now automate part of this: MisVisFix flags truncated axes, 3-D distortion and dual-axis manipulation at 0.96–0.98 F1 and localises the offending pixels.

2 min. Close the section on the automation note but do not oversell it — the tool catches geometry, which is mechanisms 1 to 3. Nothing in it catches a gross-flow label on a net-flow series. The judgement does not automate.

Think Like an Economist

Every chart makes three decisions. Interrogate all three before you trust one — including your own.

What to show

Which variables? Which window? Which units?

How to encode

Position, length, area, angle, colour?

What to omit

Missing data? Uncertainty? Context variables?

A chart is not a window onto the data. It is an argument made in visual rhetoric.

2 min. The third column is the one people skip, and it is where the serious failures live — omission leaves no trace on the page for anyone to audit.

The chapter says "within a factor of 1.5"; we checked, and it is 1.29.

When Truncation Is the Honest Choice

Truncation is not a sin. Zero is not sacred. Some quantities simply do not live near zero.

Body temperature in Fahrenheit lives in 97–104. A zero baseline spends 93% of the panel on a region no observation will ever occupy.

Policy rates live in 0–10%. Here the natural baseline is zero — and it has been binding, which is itself the interesting fact.

The test is not "does it start at zero." It is: does the truncation make a small change appear large? If the change is genuinely large within the relevant range, truncating is informative. If not, it is distortion.

Practical rule: Lie Factor above 2.0 → annotate the chart. "Note: y-axis does not start at zero." Disclosure is the floor, not the ceiling.

3 min. This is the slide that stops the chapter collapsing into a rule of thumb students then apply badly. Someone always leaves Ch 3 believing "truncated axis equals lie," and then truncates nothing, and produces unreadable interest-rate charts. The criterion is the ratio, not the baseline.

Common Misconception

“Log scales are dishonest — they hide how big the change really was.”

Log scales answer a different question. On a linear axis, equal distances are equal differences — a $10 rise looks the same from $20 as from $100. On a log axis, equal distances are equal ratios, so a 50% rise looks the same wherever it starts.

Log is the honest choice for GDP, population, stock indices — anything spanning orders of magnitude, where constant growth should draw as a straight line.

Linear is the honest choice for our wage series, varying by 1.29×, where the structural breaks are the whole economic story and log compresses them away.

The choice depends on the question. There is no blanket rule, and anyone offering you one is selling something.

2 min. Seeds Ch 20: this same choice reappears as a modelling decision when you decide between levels and log-returns. It is the same decision wearing different clothes — here it is a presentation choice, there it is a specification choice, and in both places the justification is about the question, not the data.

Poll: Where Is the Line?

Economist at Work: The Dashboard Optimises Itself

The failure is not that anyone drew a misleading chart. It is that choosing what to visualise is choosing what gets managed. Mechanism 6 — omission — operating at organisational scale.

Section Four

EDA as Forensics

Tukey called it detective work. In production it never stops running.

12 min. Shift of register here: sections 1 to 3 were about charts you show other people. This one is about charts you show yourself, before anyone else sees anything.

Four Passes, In Order

Tukey coined EDA in 1977 and described it as detective work — clues, leads, hypotheses that formal analysis will later test. The order matters: each pass tells you what to look for in the next.

1  Structure

Dimensions, types, tidiness. shape, dtypes, head

2  Distributions

Normal, skewed, bimodal? hist, kdeplot

3  Relationships

What moves with what, and how? pairplot, corr

4  Anomalies

Missing, duplicated, impossible. describe, duplicated

3 min. Emphasise that this is not a warm-up you do before the real work. Pass 3 is what tells you whether OLS in Ch 12 is even the right object; pass 4 is what stops you fitting a model to an encoding error. Skipping EDA is how you end up fitting a line to Anscombe II.

Both Tools Have a Dial You Chose

Histogram

Bins the range, counts per bin. Concrete and honest about \(n\). Its shape depends on bin width — too few and detail vanishes, too many and noise wins.

Kernel density estimate

A kernel at every point, summed into a smooth curve. No bins — but a bandwidth instead. Narrow is noisy; wide can smooth a genuine second mode out of existence.

There is no free choice here. Bin width and bandwidth are design decisions in exactly the sense the axis range was — and a bandwidth that hides bimodality is a Lie Factor you cannot compute. Plot both, and vary the dial before you believe the shape.

3 min. This is the slide that ties Section 4 back to Sections 2 and 3, so do not skip it for time. The through-line of the whole lecture: every visual tool has a parameter someone chose, and the professional obligation is to know which parameter you chose and why. KDE bandwidth is the sneakiest because it comes with a plausible default and no tick labels to audit it against.

Count the Defects. Then Count Again.

2 min. Run this live if the room is awake — it is twelve lines. The two red numbers are the whole point of the slide and students will not spot why until you tell them.

Why 10 Planted Becomes 12 Found

The generator negates 10 GDP values, then converts 15 life expectancies to months, and then duplicates 20 randomly chosen rows. Some of those duplicated rows were already corrupt. The defect is copied along with the row.

Negative GDP: 10 planted, 12 present

Life expectancy in months: 15 planted, 16 present

The transferable lesson: in a real pipeline the count of corrupted rows is not the count of corrupting events. Defects propagate through joins, appends and backfills, and the multiplier is whatever the merge did. Never reconcile "we know about 10 bad rows" against a scan that returns 12 by assuming the scan is wrong.

3 min. This is the graduate payoff of the lab and it is worth the time. Ask: if your upstream team tells you they injected exactly N bad records, and your audit finds N + k, what are the candidate explanations? Answers: propagation through a join, a retry that double-wrote, or your detector's threshold is catching something else. All three are worth ruling out separately, and none of them is "the audit is buggy."

Poll: The Detector Fires on Untouched Columns

In Part 2 only gdp is shifted, by a factor of 1.3. Run PSI on all four columns against the usual 0.10 “moderate shift” threshold, and three untouched columns return 0.092, 0.164 and 0.234.

What is the right reading?

B) The threshold has no null distribution — at this \(n\), noise alone clears 0.10

Answer: B — Whose Threshold Is 0.10?

Those three columns were never touched. With 138 training and 92 inference rows spread over 10 bins, the sampling variation in the bin proportions alone produces PSI in the low tenths. The statistic is behaving correctly; the cut-off was imported without its sample size.

0.10 and 0.25 are credit-scoring folklore, calibrated on populations of tens of thousands. Carried onto \(n \approx 100\) without recalibration, they are Chapter 2's base-year problem again: a default you did not choose is still a choice you own.

The Signal Is Real. So Is the Noise Floor.

2 min. Note the log axis, and say why you chose it: gdp is more than ten times the next bar, so on a linear scale the three columns that carry the lesson would be unreadable slivers. That is a defensible use of a log scale by the criterion from Section 3 — the data spans an order of magnitude — and you should narrate the choice rather than let it pass. Practise what the lecture preaches.

What a Usable Monitor Looks Like

The gdp shift is unmistakable at PSI 2.46 — twenty-six times the “significant” line. That detection is not in doubt. The problem is everything else the same rule fires on.

Bootstrap the null. Resample train against train, no shift, and read off the PSI you get from noise at your \(n\) and bin count. That distribution is your threshold. (Ch 7.)

Report the value, not the verdict. “PSI = 0.164” survives review. “SIGNIFICANT SHIFT” hides the number that would have let a reader disagree.

Plot the overlay anyway. A KDE of train against inference shows you where the mass moved. A scalar never will — which is Anscombe, one last time, wearing production clothes.

3 min. Land the closing callback deliberately: PSI is a summary statistic, so everything Section 1 said about summary statistics applies to it. Two very different drifts — a uniform level shift and a new mode appearing in one tail — can return the same PSI. The overlay plot is the Anscombe diagnostic for distribution monitoring, and it costs one line.

Six mechanisms. Three are geometric and a ruler catches them; mislabelling and omission it does not.
