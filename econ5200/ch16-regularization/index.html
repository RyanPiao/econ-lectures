Section 16.1

Why You Cannot Simply
Try Every Model

Stop searching. Change the objective.

4.5 min of content in this section plus the poll (2 min).

Enumeration Is Not Slow. It Is Impossible.

Chapter 15's procedure: enumerate candidate models, cross-validate each, take the winner. With seven polynomial degrees that is seven fits. With \(p\) candidate variables it is \(2^p\) subsets.

\(p = 20\)

a million models

tedious, but possible

\(p = 30\)

best subset's ceiling

beyond here, nothing enumerates

\(p = 500\)

a 151-digit number

no faster computer changes this

The classical repairs — forward stepwise, backward elimination — are fast, and they fail three ways:

Unstable — two analysts with 99% overlapping data report different models

Greedy — blind to a variable that only matters alongside another

Tests hypotheses the data suggested — Ch 8, industrialised

Regularization fixes the first two. It does not fix the third — that is Section 16.6, and it is where we finish.

2 min. 2 min. Write \(2^{500}\) on the board and let it sit — students accept "we can't try everything" abstractly, and the 151 digits make it visceral. Flag the third stepwise failure and promise to return to it: it makes the last block land as a payoff rather than an appendix.

The Reframing

Rather than searching over subsets, change the objective so the optimiser is charged for complexity — then solve one convex problem.

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \Big\{ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2}_{\text{fit — as in Ch 12}} + \underbrace{\lambda \, P(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{charge for complexity}} \Big\}$$

\(\lambda \geq 0\) — penalty strength. At \(\lambda = 0\) the penalty vanishes and you recover OLS. As \(\lambda \to \infty\) every coefficient is driven to zero and the model predicts the mean.

\(P(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) — the penalty's shape, which is what defines the method. Everything today turns on this one choice.

Choosing \(\lambda\) is choosing a point on Chapter 15's bias-variance curve — continuously, instead of by trying a handful of discrete models.

1.5 min. Present this as a reframing, not an algorithm: you are not searching, you are changing what "best fit" means. That sentence does more work than the equation.

Standardisation Is Mandatory, Not Stylistic

OLS is scale-invariant: measure income in dollars or in thousands of dollars and the fitted values are identical. Penalised regression is not.

The penalty charges by coefficient magnitude. A variable measured in dollars has a coefficient a thousand times smaller than the same variable in thousands of dollars — so it is charged a thousandth as much. Without standardisation the penalty silently prefers whichever variables happen to be measured in large units. That is a preference nobody chose.

make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoCV(cv=5))

Always standardise, and always inside a pipeline, so the scaler is refitted on each training fold — the leakage argument of Chapter 15, unchanged.

1 min. 1 min, and repeat it in the lab. Forgetting this produces results that look plausible and are wrong, which is the worst failure mode there is. Note that scikit-learn does NOT scale inside Lasso or LassoCV — that surprises people who have used glmnet, which does standardise by default and back-transforms.

Poll: Which One Gets Dropped?

You fit LASSO on unstandardised data. Household income is in dollars; the unemployment rate is in percentage points. Both genuinely matter.

A) Income — LASSO zeroes small coefficients, and in dollars income's is tiny
B) The unemployment rate — its coefficient is large, so the penalty charges it heavily
C) Neither — scikit-learn standardises internally
D) Impossible to say — it depends only on which one predicts better
1 min. 1 min. Students vote on their devices; results appear on the next slide. Expect A to lead — it is the soft-thresholding picture applied where it does not hold.

Answer: B — the Large-Coefficient Variable

Income in dollars carries a coefficient roughly \(10^{-4}\) the size of the same variable in units of ten thousand dollars, so the \(L_1\) charge on it is negligible and it survives almost free. The penalty is expressing a preference about units.

Why A is the productive error: it imports soft thresholding — zero out anything below \(\lambda\) — which holds only in an orthonormal design. In general the condition for a zero is \(|\mathbf{x}_j'\mathbf{r}|/n \leq \lambda\), and the left side scales with the column's magnitude. A large-unit column is the hardest one to zero out.

1 min. If A led the vote, say so out loud — it means they have internalised soft thresholding, which is genuinely good, and now they learn its precondition. C is worth one sentence: sklearn does not standardise; glmnet does. Same method, different defaults.

Section 16.2

Ridge — Shrink Everything

The \(L_2\) penalty, and the one term that dissolves \(p > n\)

7.5 min of content plus Discussion 1 (4 min).

Ridge: the \(L_2\) Penalty, and the One Term That Matters

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2 \right\}$$

\(\sum\beta_j^2\) is the squared length of the coefficient vector, so a coefficient of 4 costs sixteen times what a coefficient of 1 costs. The intercept is never penalised — that would make the model depend on where you put the origin of \(y\), which is arbitrary.

OLS

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

Ridge

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

The only difference is \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\), and that addition is the entire point.

Chapter 14 required \((\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})\) to be invertible — which fails under perfect collinearity and always fails when \(p > n\). Adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) shifts every eigenvalue up by \(\lambda\), so none can be zero, and the matrix is invertible for any \(\lambda > 0\), always.

Ridge does not merely help with multicollinearity. It dissolves the condition that made it fatal. A problem OLS cannot express an answer to, Ridge answers.

2 min. Circle the \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) on the board. The eigenvalue justification takes thirty seconds and we prove it properly in the Advanced Treatment block later — tell them it is coming so they do not chase it now.

Why Shrinking Toward a Number You Know Is Wrong Helps

Suppose the true coefficient is 2.0 and your unbiased estimator has a standard error of 3.0.

Unbiased

Right on average. Also: a typical draw is 5.1, or −0.4, or 4.3. Each produces a badly wrong prediction, in a different direction every time you resample.

Shrunk by half

Now biased — it averages 1.0 against a truth of 2.0. But the standard error is 1.5, so a typical draw lies between −0.5 and 2.5.

Squared error punishes the occasional wild estimate far more than a systematic small one. Halving the variance more than pays for the bias: bias\(^2\) rose a little, variance fell a lot, and their sum fell. That is Chapter 15's decomposition doing the work.

Shrinkage is worthwhile exactly when your estimates are noisy — which in high dimensions they always are.

1.5 min. This is the slide that converts "biased estimator" from heresy into engineering. If one student is still uncomfortable, ask them which they would rather bet on: an estimate that is right on average but ranges over seven units, or one that is off by one and ranges over three.

A Circle Has No Corners. A Diamond Does.

Equivalent constrained form: minimise the ordinary sum of squared residuals subject to a budget on total coefficient size. The estimate sits where the expanding ellipses first touch the region.

2 min. 2 min, and it is the most valuable two minutes in the lecture. Draw the circle first, get the tangency, then rub it out and draw the diamond in the same place. The chapter itself registers this picture as a production gap in the interactive hub — carrying it in prose loses the argument. Do NOT explain the diamond yet in detail; you return to it in Section 3.

Where Ridge Fails — and a Diagnostic Habit

Right tool when many predictors each contribute a little and they are correlated: Ridge shrinks correlated groups together rather than picking between them.

Wrong tool when most predictors are genuinely irrelevant — and the reason is structural, not about tuning. Uniform shrinkage cannot manufacture sparsity. No \(\lambda\) makes 490 useless coefficients disappear while leaving 10 useful ones intact.

OLS test MSE

43.28

Ridge test MSE — a 2% improvement on a catastrophe

Offered \(\lambda\) from \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^{6}\), cross-validation selected the smallest: 0.01. Facing a problem Ridge cannot solve, it correctly reported that more shrinkage does not help. When CV selects a value at the edge of your grid, the grid is too narrow or the method is wrong. Widening to \(10^{8}\) changed nothing — which rules out the first.

1.5 min. The boundary-selection habit generalises far past this chapter and is the reason for Discussion 1. Say plainly: a hyperparameter sitting at a boundary is information, not a result to report and move past.

Section 16.3

LASSO — Shrink and Select

One exponent changes. The consequence is qualitative.

3.5 min of content plus the poll (2 min).

The Corner of the Diamond

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \right\}$$

One exponent changed, \(\beta_j^2 \to |\beta_j|\). The constraint region \(\sum|\beta_j| \leq s\) is now a diamond — a square rotated 45°, with corners sitting exactly on the axes.

Expanding elliptical contours meeting a diamond are very likely to touch at a corner, because corners stick out. And a corner of the diamond is a point where one coordinate is exactly zero.

That is the whole mechanism. LASSO produces sparsity not by testing coefficients and dropping the insignificant ones, but because the geometry of its constraint region has points on the axes and the solution tends to land on them. In \(p\) dimensions the region has faces of every dimension, so solutions with any number of exact zeros are available.

1.5 min. Go back to the geometry figure if the room needs it. The sentence to land: LASSO does not test anything. Students who arrive from econometrics assume there is a significance test hiding inside; there is not.

Watching It Work — and Then Reading the Last Line

   OLS  train 0.0000   test   43.99
 Ridge  train 0.0000   test   43.28
 LASSO  train 0.5984   test    1.54

LASSO kept 44 of 500 features
  true features recovered: 10/10
  false positives:         34

Every true feature found. But look at the last line before celebrating: 34 false positives. LASSO kept 44 to catch 10 — roughly three-quarters of what it selected is noise that happened to correlate with the outcome in this sample.

Not a tuning failure. A penalty aggressive enough to exclude every lucky noise variable would also exclude the weaker true ones. LASSO is a screening device, not an oracle — and these 34 occur on independent Gaussian columns, the friendliest design there is. A lower bound on the difficulty.

Three Things Are Happening at Once

1. It is the U-curve. Training error falls monotonically — 32.191 down to 0.005 — while test error bottoms at \(\lambda = 0.138\) and rises again. That is precisely what cross-validation selected, without being shown the test set. The procedure works.

2. The top two rows are perfect. At \(\lambda = 2.0\) and \(\lambda = 1.0\), LASSO keeps exactly 10 features and every one is true. No misses, no false positives.

3. Those perfect rows are terrible predictors. Test MSE at \(\lambda = 1.0\) is 6.92 against 1.54 at the optimum. The model that identifies exactly the right variables predicts 4.5× worse than the model that keeps 44.

Not a paradox. At \(\lambda = 1.0\) the ten survivors have been shrunk hard toward zero, so the variables are right and the magnitudes are badly attenuated. Loosening the penalty restores the magnitudes and admits noise along with them — and for squared-error prediction that trade is worth making.

2 min. This is the most important slide in the first half. If they take one thing: selecting the right variables and predicting well are different objectives, optimised at different \(\lambda\), and here they are in direct conflict.

Section 16.5

The Penalty Is a Belief,
Not an Upgrade

The centre of the chapter. Change nothing but the truth.

7.5 min of content plus Discussion 2 (3 min). This is the block that changes minds.

Same Estimators. Same Grid. One Line of the Truth Changed.

Textbooks present LASSO as Ridge with a bonus feature — same shrinkage, plus selection, therefore better. Let us test that. Now all 500 features matter, each a little:

dense_beta = rng.normal(0, 0.25, p)   # every feature matters, slightly
DENSE truth (all 500 features matter a little):
   OLS    train 0.0000   test   27.84
   Ridge  train 0.0000   test   28.42
   LASSO  train 0.0003   test   37.36   (kept 114 of 500)

LASSO is now the worst of the three — a third worse than OLS, on data where OLS is already poor. Change nothing but the truth, and the method that was 28× better becomes the loser.

A penalty is not a neutral technical device — it encodes a belief. \(L_1\) believes most coefficients are exactly zero; \(L_2\) believes many are small but nonzero. Neither is a safe default, and the choice is a claim about the economics of your problem.

Eight Variables of Identical True Importance

Five blocks of eight near-duplicates. The textbook claim is that Ridge handles correlation better — but prediction is essentially identical across all four methods (corner of the plot), and LASSO is fractionally best. The difference is in the coefficients.

LASSO drops two entirely, gives one 1.127 — more than double the truth — and scatters the rest. Refit on a slightly different sample and a different two would be dropped.

Elastic Net — Let the Data Choose the Mix

$$\lambda \left( \alpha_{EN} \sum_j |\beta_j| + \frac{1 - \alpha_{EN}}{2}\sum_j \beta_j^2 \right)$$

Reading it: \(\alpha_{EN} \in [0,1]\) is the mixing parameter — 1 gives pure LASSO, 0 pure Ridge. The \(L_1\) part preserves sparsity; the \(L_2\) part supplies a grouping effect, pulling correlated predictors toward each other rather than letting one win arbitrarily.

ElasticNetCV(cv=5, l1_ratio=[0.1, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0], random_state=42)

Passing a list makes the mix a tuned hyperparameter rather than an assumption. On the sparse design of Section 16.3 it selects 1.0 — pure LASSO, the correct answer there.

The chosen mixing parameter is itself a result worth reporting. \(\alpha_{EN}\) near 1 says the data looks sparse; near 0, that effects are diffuse. It converts a modelling judgement into something the data can speak to — the honest position when you genuinely do not know. Elastic Net also escapes a hard limit: pure LASSO saturates at \(n\) selected variables.

1.5 min. The reportable-\(\alpha_{EN}\) point is a genuinely useful habit and almost nobody teaches it. Tell them it belongs in a results table, not a footnote.

Economist at Work — the IMF, 2024

Post-pandemic structural breaks had made the reliable data short, while high-frequency indicators had grown past a hundred. Dynamic factor models were struggling and OLS was not merely inaccurate but incomputable — with \(p\) approaching \(n\), the design matrix was rank-deficient.

The team deployed LASSO, Ridge and Elastic Net. The first result was disappointing. Left to itself on highly collinear macro series, LASSO selected variables that made no economic sense — the irrepresentable condition failing exactly as theory predicts.

What fixed it was not a better algorithm. The economists imposed sign restrictions — consumer sentiment positive, unemployment claims negative, and so on wherever theory dictates a direction. With those constraints the model outperformed both MIDAS and dynamic factor benchmarks. On Japanese core inflation, LASSO's selections were themselves informative: household inflation expectations, exchange rates and inbound tourism as post-2022 drivers.

An algorithm turned loose on collinear data finds patterns. An economist decides which patterns are permitted.

1.5 min. The chapter's thesis in operational form: the penalty encodes a belief about sparsity, sign restrictions encode a belief about direction, and both are economics entering the estimator. IMF WP/24/206 and WP/25/217 if anyone asks.

Advanced Treatment • ECON 5200

Closed Forms, Soft Thresholding,
and Why Only One Penalty Selects

The geometry was the intuition. This is the algebra underneath it.

6 min. Graduate-only — the undergraduate rendering of this chapter stops after the workflow checklist. Say that out loud; it tells them why the second half of this lecture is different in kind.

Ridge, From the Normal Equations

Differentiate \(\tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 + \lambda\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2\) and set the gradient to zero:

$$-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}'(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) + 2\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda' \mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

absorbing constants into \(\lambda'\).

The invertibility argument, properly. \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) is positive semi-definite with eigenvalues \(\gamma_j \geq 0\). So \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda'\mathbf{I}\) has eigenvalues \(\gamma_j + \lambda' > 0\) for any \(\lambda' > 0\). A matrix with no zero eigenvalue is invertible. Therefore Ridge is always defined — including when \(p > n\) and \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) is singular.

That is the formal content of the claim on the closed-form slide, and it takes thirty seconds. Note what it does not require: no full rank, no \(n > p\), no condition on the data at all.

1.5 min. Graduate students appreciate that this is a one-line proof and not hand-waving. If someone asks about the ill-conditioning of \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) rather than singularity: \(\lambda\) also bounds the condition number, which is why Ridge is numerically stable where OLS is not.

LASSO, From the Subgradient

The \(L_1\) objective is not differentiable at \(\beta_j = 0\), so the first-order condition uses subgradients:

$$-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_j'(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) + \lambda s_j = 0, \qquad s_j \in \begin{cases} \{\text{sign}(\hat{\beta}_j)\} & \hat{\beta}_j \neq 0 \\ [-1, 1] & \hat{\beta}_j = 0\end{cases}$$

That set-valued subgradient at zero is the whole story. At \(\hat\beta_j = 0\), \(s_j\) has an entire interval to choose from — so the optimality condition can be satisfied with \(\hat\beta_j\) exactly zero across a whole range of data configurations, not just on a knife edge.

Ridge's derivative \(2\lambda\beta_j\) is single-valued and simply vanishes at zero, so zero is never specially favoured. Sparsity is a consequence of the penalty's non-smoothness at the origin — the corner of the diamond, in calculus.

1.5 min. The payoff sentence: the kink IS the selection mechanism. Any penalty with a kink at the origin selects; any smooth one does not. That generalises to SCAD, MCP and group LASSO, and it is worth saying so — several of them will meet group LASSO in their capstones.

Soft Thresholding — a Translation With a Floor

$$\hat{\beta}_j^{\text{lasso}} = \text{sign}(\hat{\beta}_j^{\text{OLS}}) \max\left(|\hat{\beta}_j^{\text{OLS}}| - \lambda, 0\right)$$

Every coefficient is moved \(\lambda\) toward zero and held there if it would cross — a translation with a floor, rather than Ridge's proportional scaling.

Coefficients smaller than \(\lambda\) become exactly zero. This is the algebraic form of the diamond's corner.

Ridge, same design: \(\hat\beta_j^{\text{ridge}} = \hat\beta_j^{\text{OLS}}/(1+\lambda)\) — one uniform discount on the whole vector. Proportional shrinkage never reaches zero for finite \(\lambda\). A circle has no corners, in algebra.

2 min. The two lines on this plot are the entire difference between the methods, in one picture. Note the LASSO line is parallel to the identity outside the dead zone — that parallel offset is the bias on the large coefficients, which is the next slide.

A Consequence Worth Sitting With

Look again at what soft thresholding does to a large true coefficient. It is shrunk by the full \(\lambda\) too — the operator does not spare it.

That biases every surviving coefficient toward zero, and it destroys asymptotic normality. The estimator's sampling distribution has a point mass at zero and a shifted continuous part — there is no \(\sqrt{n}\)-normal limit to build a \(t\)-statistic on.

Two things follow, and we take both up shortly:

The adaptive LASSO is built precisely to remove this bias — Formal Sketch, next.

And it is the deep reason Section 16.6's trap is a trap: there is no valid \(t\)-statistic to report, before you even get to the selection problem.

1 min. 1 min. Worth being explicit: students often think post-LASSO inference fails only because of the selection step. It fails twice — shrinkage bias AND selection. The refit-on-survivors procedure of Section 16.6 removes the first and leaves the second entirely.

Formal Sketch • ECON 5200

When LASSO Recovers the Truth,
and When It Cannot

Zhao & Yu (2006) — and why economics fails the condition

4 min. Graduate-only.

The Irrepresentable Condition

Partition the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) into the active set \(\mathbf{X}_1\) (truly nonzero) and the inactive set \(\mathbf{X}_2\), and write \(\Sigma = \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) in matching blocks. LASSO recovers the true support with probability approaching one only if:

$$\left\|\Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\,\text{sign}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_1)\right\|_{\infty} \leq 1 - \eta \quad \text{for some } \eta > 0$$

Interpretation. \(\Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\) is the matrix of coefficients from regressing each irrelevant variable on the relevant ones. The condition says: no irrelevant variable may be too well explained by the relevant ones. If a noise variable is nearly a linear combination of the true predictors, LASSO cannot tell them apart — it will include it while dropping a genuine one.

And no increase in sample size repairs this. The condition is about the design, not about \(n\) — not a small-sample problem you can outgrow. Macroeconomic indicators, asset-pricing factors and anything with a factor structure have exactly the property it forbids: everything is well explained by everything else. Section 16.3's 34 false positives are the mild case; the IMF's nonsensical selections are what real macro data does.

2.5 min. 2 min. The fragment is the sentence that matters and graduate students routinely get it wrong — they assume asymptotics will save them. Zhao & Yu (2006), JMLR 7, 2541-2563.

Two Responses

Adaptive LASSO — Zou (2006)

Weight each penalty by the inverse of an initial consistent estimate: \(\lambda\sum_j w_j|\beta_j|\) with \(w_j = 1/|\hat\beta_j^{\text{init}}|^\gamma\). Large coefficients are penalised lightly and small ones heavily — which removes the shrinkage bias of the previous block and delivers the oracle property: asymptotically the estimator performs as if the true support had been known in advance.

Factor-adjusted selection

Project out the common factors first, restoring an approximately orthogonal design before applying \(L_1\). This is the response that matches the economics: if everything correlates because of a few common factors, remove the factors and the condition stops failing.

Zhao & Yu (2006) JMLR 7 • Zou (2006) JASA 101(476) • Fan & Li (2001) JASA 96(456) for the SCAD alternative.

1 min. 1 min. The oracle property is a strong claim and worth one caution: it is asymptotic, and Leeb & Pötscher have shown oracle estimators have badly non-uniform finite-sample behaviour. Mention it if you have a sharp room; it is exactly the kind of caveat a thesis committee will raise.

For economists, the most consequential thing in the chapter

9.5 min including Poll 4. For economists this is the most consequential block in the chapter.

A Plausible Workflow. What Is Wrong With It?

You have 500 candidate controls.

You run LASSO. It keeps 12.

You fit ordinary OLS on those 12, report the coefficients with their standard errors and \(p\)-values, and describe the result as a regression with well-chosen controls.

This is invalid, and the invalidity is not subtle.

1.5 min. Ask the room before revealing the fragment. Genuinely wait — the failure is not obvious and the discovery is worth the silence. Some of them have done exactly this in a previous course.

Because the Specification Is Now Random

Classical inference assumes the specification was fixed before seeing the data. Chapter 8 built every \(p\)-value on that assumption.

But these 12 variables were chosen because they correlated strongly with the outcome in this sample — that is what LASSO does. Reporting classical standard errors treats a data-driven choice as if theory had made it, and the intervals are consequently too narrow. Their true coverage is well below nominal.

It is the same error as running 500 hypothesis tests and reporting only the significant ones — Chapter 8 named it. LASSO simply automates the selection and makes it feel principled.

And it is worse than the manual version in one respect. A researcher running 500 tests by hand knows they did it. LASSO performs the same search silently, returns a tidy list, and produces no artefact recording how many alternatives were considered. A folder of 500 test outputs can at least be audited. An optimisation cannot.

1.5 min. The fragment is the graduate-level point and it is not in the undergraduate rendering. The absence of an audit trail is what makes this failure structurally different from p-hacking, and it is worth naming as such.

How Bad? You Measure It in Lab 16

\(n = 200\), 100 candidate controls, true treatment effect 1.0, five covariates driving both treatment and outcome.

The naive procedure keeps 21 of 100 controls. Point estimate 1.0495, interval [0.9272, 1.1717] — which does cover, on that one draw.

Repeat 200 times: coverage 82.5% against a nominal 95%. Mean estimate 1.0400 against a truth of 1.0.

Selection biases the point estimate, not merely the interval — and biases it away from zero, because the controls that survive are the ones whose sample correlation happened to be large.

1.5 min. These are the lab's own executed numbers, not a textbook illustration — run on 5 Sep 2026. Emphasise that the single-draw interval covers: one replication tells you nothing, which is itself a Ch 15 lesson.

Poll: The Obvious Fix

Coverage came out at 82.5% against a nominal 95%. A colleague proposes fixing it by raising \(\lambda\) so that fewer controls are selected. What happens to coverage?

A) It improves — fewer selected variables means less selection to account for
B) It gets worse — a bigger penalty drops confounders and reintroduces omitted-variable bias
C) Unchanged — \(\lambda\) moves the point estimate but not the interval width
D) It reaches nominal — with enough shrinkage the selection step becomes irrelevant
1 min. 1 min. A should lead comfortably. It is the intuitive answer and it is wrong.

Answer: B — It Gets Worse

Raising \(\lambda\) does reduce the search — but it drops genuine confounders along the way. The resulting omitted-variable bias moves the estimate further from the truth while the interval stays roughly the same width, so coverage falls further. You trade a variance problem for a bias problem, and lose.

This is Diagnosis 1, question 3 of your lab — you will demonstrate it yourself. The repair is not a different \(\lambda\). It is a different procedure.

1 min. Land the closing line hard: no setting of the tuning parameter repairs a procedure that is structurally invalid. That is the bridge to post-double selection.

The Repair — Post-Double Selection

Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen (2014), Review of Economic Studies 81(2). Run LASSO twice.

1

LASSO predicting the outcome from the controls \(\rightarrow\) support \(A\)

2

LASSO predicting the treatment from the controls \(\rightarrow\) support \(B\)

3

Unpenalised OLS of \(y\) on \(d\) and \(\mathbf{Z}_{A \cup B}\) — the union

The second LASSO is what saves you. It retains confounders that predict treatment strongly but the outcome weakly — exactly the variables a single outcome-focused LASSO discards, and exactly the ones whose omission reintroduces omitted-variable bias.

In the lab's design that lifts coverage from 82.5% to about 90% and raises the retained control count from 21 to about 26. Better — and still not 95%, which is worth saying out loud: these are asymptotic guarantees meeting \(n = 200\).

1.5 min. The honesty about 90% matters. A reference implementation over 400 replications lands at 89.8% with a Monte Carlo SE of 1.5 points. If a student's lab result comes out near 90 rather than 95, they have done it right — say so now so nobody spends an evening hunting a bug that is not there.

How Much Does the Repair Buy? Less Than You Expect.

A 2024 World Bank audit (Cilliers, Elashmawy & McKenzie) re-estimated 780 treatment effects from published randomised trials using post-double selection.

< 1%

average improvement in standard errors, against plain ANCOVA with pre-specified controls

> half

of cases where the treatment equation selected no controls at all

The second finding explains the first. In a randomised trial, assignment is random by construction — so nothing should predict it. The second LASSO has nothing to find, and the procedure collapses back to careful outcome modelling.

PDS is worth least exactly where you can already trust your design — and worth most exactly where you cannot check whether it worked.

1.5 min. This slide keeps the room honest. It is easy to teach PDS as a triumph; the audit says it is a repair whose value depends entirely on the design you brought. World Bank Policy Research WP 10931.

Going Deeper • ECON 5200

Valid Inference After Selection

Three frameworks, three different promises

4 min of content plus Discussion 3 (3 min). Undergraduates never see this block.

Three Ways to Pay for the Selection

Framework What it promises Mechanism
Post-double selection
BCH 2014		 valid inference on one target parameter select on both equations, take the union, unpenalised OLS. \(\sqrt{n}\)-consistent and asymptotically normal under approximate sparsity.
Exact post-selection inference
Lee, Sun, Sun & Taylor 2016		 exact, honest intervals conditional on what you selected LASSO's chosen model corresponds to the data falling in a polyhedron \(\{y : \mathbf{A}y \leq b\}\); conditioning on that event yields a truncated Gaussian.
Debiased (desparsified) LASSO
Zhang & Zhang 2014; van de Geer et al. 2014		 intervals for every coefficient, even when \(p \gg n\) add a correction derived from inverting the KKT conditions; removes the shrinkage bias and restores asymptotic normality.

Lee et al.'s intervals can be very wide — and that is not a defect. It is an accurate reflection of how much the selection step cost you.

1.5 min. Do not derive any of them. The job here is a map: which tool answers which question. Point out that PDS answers "one parameter I care about" and debiased LASSO answers "all of them", and those are genuinely different research designs.

The Idea Connecting All Three

Selection consumes information.

Pay by conditioning on what you did  —  Lee et al.

Pay by correcting the bias it introduced  —  debiased LASSO

Pay by selecting so conservatively that nothing important is lost  —  post-double selection

What you cannot do is ignore it. That is the failure the 780-effect audit documents at scale.

PDS is the direct ancestor of Double Machine Learning: swap the two LASSOs for arbitrary learners, add cross-fitting, and you have Chapter 24. The residualise-on-both-sides logic is Frisch–Waugh–Lovell from Chapter 14.

1 min. Close the graduate arc here. They should leave knowing this is the bridge to Ch 24 and that BCH (2014) is the paper both chapters share.

What Regularization Does Not Fix

It does not fix a confounded design. Shrinkage reduces variance; it says nothing about bias from an omitted confounder — and it makes that bias harder to see, since a shrunk coefficient looks modest and well-behaved whether it is correct or badly confounded.

It does not identify causal variables. LASSO selects variables that predict. A variable predicts because it causes, because it shares a cause, or because it is a consequence — Chapter 10's three structures, indistinguishable to any penalty. Put a collider among the candidates and LASSO keeps it happily. And note the limit of everything in the last block: debiased LASSO will hand you a valid confidence interval for that collider's coefficient. Valid inference on a meaningless parameter is still meaningless.

It does not rescue a badly measured target. Chapter 15's noise floor is unmoved by any penalty. Past that limit the answer is better data, not a better estimator.

And one property nobody teaches: regularized coefficients are not comparable across specifications. Change \(\lambda\), change the candidate set, or change the scaling, and every coefficient moves — each is the solution to a different constrained problem. Reporting a LASSO coefficient without its \(\lambda\), its standardisation and its candidate set is reporting an uninterpretable number.

1.5 min, deliberately deflationary. Students finish a regularization chapter feeling high-dimensional problems are solved; these three reset that. The non-comparability point is the one they will need when reading applied papers, and it is rarely taught. CUT IF BEHIND — self-contained, and it survives in lecture-notes.md.

A Regularization Workflow

1

Decide what you want before you fit. Prediction, or a coefficient you intend to interpret? Most confusion comes from wanting both and admitting to neither.

2

Standardise, inside a pipeline. Scale-dependence and leakage, both handled in one line.

3

Fit Ridge and LASSO both and let CV compare them. The comparison is itself informative.

4

Look at the CV curve, not just its minimum. Flat across an order of magnitude means \(\lambda\) is not identified — say so.

5

Report the count, treat the list as a hypothesis. “Retained 44 of 500” is a finding. “Identified the 44 that matter” is not.

6

If a causal parameter is involved, stop and use post-double selection. Not a technicality — the difference between valid and invalid inference.

Step 1 is the one that matters. Steps 5 and 6 are where the undergraduate track stops and yours continues.

Five Things to Take Away

1. \(2^{500}\) makes subset selection impossible. Regularization replaces the search with a penalty and solves one convex problem.

2. Ridge's \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) makes \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) invertible for any \(\lambda > 0\) — \(p > n\) stops being fatal — and it zeroes nothing.

3. LASSO's zeros are geometry, not significance testing. It is a screening device: 44 kept to find 10, on the friendliest design there is.

4. The penalty is an assumption. Sparse truth: LASSO 28× better. Dense truth: LASSO worst of three. Correlated blocks: predictions fine, coefficients arbitrary.

5. LASSO-select then classical SEs is invalid — measured coverage 82.5%. PDS repairs it partly, and buys under 1% in randomised settings, because randomisation leaves its second stage nothing to select.

1 min. Read 3 and 4 aloud. Those are the two that survive the semester.
