Why the Classical Repairs Do Not Work

Forward stepwise adds the best remaining variable; backward elimination drops the least significant. Fast, and in every software menu you will ever open.

Unstable. Change a few observations, a different variable wins round one, the whole path diverges. Two analysts with 99% overlapping data report different models — the specification is a property of the sample, not the world.

Greedy. A variable useless alone but valuable alongside another is never added — forward selection only ever evaluates one addition at a time. Interactions and suppression effects are systematically invisible.

Every step tests a hypothesis suggested by the data. Chapter 8's multiple-comparisons problem, industrialised.

Regularization fixes the first two — one convex objective, one unique minimum, all coefficients determined simultaneously. It does not fix the third. That is Section 16.6, and it is where we finish.

2 min. Flag the third failure now and promise to return — it makes the last section land as a payoff rather than an appendix. Graduate students in particular assume LASSO solved this; it did not.

Misconception

“LASSO tells me which variables matter.”

It tells you which variables it kept, which is different. Here it kept 44 to find 10.

And its selections carry a formal caveat: LASSO recovers the true support only under the irrepresentable condition — irrelevant variables must not be strongly correlated with relevant ones. Macroeconomic series, asset-pricing factors, and anything with a factor structure violate it routinely.

Treat a LASSO selection as a hypothesis worth investigating. Never as a finding.

1.5 min. Name the irrepresentable condition here and promise the formal statement — we prove what it says in the Formal Sketch block. Graduate students will want the citation now: Zhao and Yu, JMLR 2006.

Modern solvers compute the entire path for barely more than the cost of a single fit. Reading left to right, the penalty weakens and variables enter one at a time.

The False Positives Are Not a Tuning Failure

Among 490 irrelevant predictors, each independent of the outcome, some will correlate with \(y\) in this sample by chance alone. With 490 draws, a few correlate quite strongly. LASSO sees only the sample — it cannot distinguish “correlates because it causes” from “correlates because we got unlucky.”

To exclude every lucky noise variable, the penalty would have to exclude anything with a modest sample correlation. But some genuinely relevant variables have modest sample correlations too — particularly those with smaller true coefficients. A penalty aggressive enough to remove all the noise removes real signal along with it.

So the choice is not between a clean list and a dirty one. It is between a short list that misses true variables and a longer one that admits false ones.

And these 34 occur on independent Gaussian columns — the friendliest possible design. This is a lower bound on the difficulty. Real economic data is worse.

2 min. LASSO is a screening device, not an oracle. Say the words "screening device" and write them down — it is the framing that stops students over-reading their own output for the rest of the course.

A Penalty Is Not a Neutral Technical Device

\(L_1\) believes

most coefficients are exactly zero.

When true, enormously powerful. When false, it is forcing zeros onto parameters that are not zero — paying in bias for nothing.

\(L_2\) believes

many coefficients are small but nonzero.

Right whenever effects are diffuse — which describes a great deal of economics, where outcomes are shaped by many weakly-acting factors.

So the choice is not statistical. It is a question about your subject matter: do I believe a small number of these variables do essentially all the work? Sometimes yes — a few genes, a few binding constraints. Often no.

1.5 min. Say "neither is a safe default" out loud. Students want a rule; the honest answer is that the rule is a claim about the economics of their problem.

Correlated Predictors: The Standard Claim Is Wrong

The textbook claim is that Ridge handles correlated predictors better than LASSO. The truth is more specific, and more useful. Five blocks of eight near-duplicates; all eight in block 1 have identical true coefficients of 0.5.

   OLS         test MSE 1.272
   Ridge       test MSE 1.116
   LASSO       test MSE 1.090
   ElasticNet  test MSE 1.090

Prediction is essentially identical — and LASSO is fractionally best, not worst. The standard claim does not appear in test error at all.

It appears in the coefficients.

1.5 min. Set the trap deliberately: ask the room to predict the ranking before you reveal it. Most will put Ridge first because that is what they were taught.

In an Orthonormal Design, Ridge Is a Single Division

Set \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{I}\). The solution becomes transparent:

$$\hat{\beta}_j^{\text{ridge}} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j^{\text{OLS}}}{1 + \lambda}$$

Reading it: every coefficient is scaled by the same factor, regardless of its size. Ridge does not decide which variables to keep — it applies one uniform discount to the whole vector.

Proportional shrinkage never reaches zero for finite \(\lambda\). Divide any nonzero number by \(1 + \lambda\) as many times as you like; you approach zero and never arrive. That is why Ridge does not select — and it is the algebraic restatement of “a circle has no corners.”

1.5 min. Connect explicitly back to the geometry slide: the two statements are the same fact, one drawn and one computed. Students who did not believe the picture will believe the division.

The \(L_2\) Penalty

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2 \right\}$$

Reading it: \(\sum \beta_j^2\) is the squared length of the coefficient vector. You are charged for how far the whole vector sits from the origin, and you are charged quadratically, so a coefficient of 4 costs sixteen times what a coefficient of 1 costs.

The intercept is never penalised. Penalising it would make the model sensitive to where you happen to put the origin of \(y\) — and that is arbitrary. Shifting every wage in your sample up by ten thousand dollars should not change the slopes.

1 min. The quadratic charge is worth one sentence: it means Ridge fights hardest against the largest coefficients, which is exactly what you want when the largest ones are the noisiest.

Economics Fails It Routinely

Macroeconomic indicators, asset-pricing factors, and anything with a factor structure have exactly the property the condition forbids: everything is well explained by everything else.

The 34 false positives of Section 16.3 are a mild instance — on independent Gaussian columns, where the condition holds comfortably.

The IMF's nonsensical selections are what failure looks like on real macro data. Not a bug in their code. The condition, failing.

Which is why sign restrictions worked and a better algorithm would not have. The design was the problem, and the repair had to come from outside the data.
