A Line Instead of a Lever

The design, the estimand, and the one thing it asks of you

Section 13.1. Roughly 11 minutes including the poll.

Draw It Before You Write It

Administrative rules are arbitrary at the margin. Nobody believes $74,999 and $75,001 describe meaningfully different families.

The number was chosen because a number had to be chosen. That arbitrariness is the resource.

Fit each side separately. If the outcome is smooth in the running variable except for a jump at the threshold, the jump is the effect — because nothing else about people changes discontinuously at an arbitrary number.

Draw this on the board before showing the slide if you can.

  • Ask what has to be true for the gap to be causal and let the class arrive at "nothing else jumps here." Other examples worth naming: class-size cap at 40 pupils, loan approval at a credit score of 680, a drug approved above a clinical threshold.
  • Do not touch bandwidth yet.

The Estimand Is a Difference of Limits

SHARP ASSIGNMENT

\[ D_i = \mathbb{1}[R_i \geq c] \]

In words: treatment is a switch, and the running variable $R_i$ flips it at the cutoff $c$. By universal convention the switch is on above the line.

THE ESTIMAND

\[ \tau_{SRD} = \lim_{r \downarrow c} E[Y_i \mid R_i = r] - \lim_{r \uparrow c} E[Y_i \mid R_i = r] \]

In words: walk in from the right, walk in from the left, measure the gap where the two arrivals fail to meet. These are limits, not group averages.

THE ASSUMPTION — CONTINUITY

$E[Y_i(0)\mid R_i=r]$ and $E[Y_i(1)\mid R_i=r]$ are continuous in $r$ at $c$

In words: absent treatment, outcomes would have moved smoothly through the threshold.

THE RESULT — HAHN, TODD & VAN DER KLAAUW (2001)

\[ \tau_{SRD} = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid R_i = c] \]

In words: the effect for people standing exactly on the line. Not for anyone else.

Say every interpretation line out loud — never present the math bare.

  • Put the direction convention on the board in red and say you will come back to it; the hook has primed them and the payoff is two slides away.
  • The conditioning on R_i = c in the bottom-right box is not a technicality: it is the whole character of the design and the last section of the lecture is about living with it.

Continuity, Not Comparability

RD does not assume the treated and untreated groups are similar. It assumes the conditional expectation function is smooth through the cutoff.

Richer families may have systematically higher retention — fine, if the relationship is smooth. Confounders may be arbitrarily strong — fine, if they vary smoothly with income. You are not claiming the groups are alike. You are claiming that whatever makes them differ does so gradually.

It fails in exactly two identifiable ways — and we test for both after the break:

Manipulation

People control which side they land on. Those just below are better informed, better advised, more able to restructure income. The sorting is not arbitrary, so the threshold stops being arbitrary.

Compound treatment

Something else uses the same cutoff. Your jump is the combined effect of both programmes, and no statistical care disentangles them. A design problem, not an estimation problem.

This is the most common misunderstanding among students arriving from matching, and it is worth the whole slide.

  • Note that "the groups are similar" is STRONGER than what RD needs, not weaker — students usually assume they are being asked for more, not less.
  • Compound treatment is the objection a good referee raises first; flag that we return to it at the very end because no test detects it.

Poll: What Does RD Actually Require?

A sharp RD design identifies the effect at the cutoff. Which of these does it actually require?

A. Households just above and just below the cutoff are similar on observables
B. The conditional expectation of the outcome is smooth through the cutoff
C. Treatment is randomly assigned within the bandwidth
D. The running variable is uncorrelated with the outcome

Students click to vote on their devices; results render live on the next slide.

  • Give them 60 seconds.
  • If the room splits between A and B, that split is the most useful diagnostic of the whole lecture — run the "richer families have higher retention and RD is fine with that" example on the spot before revealing.

Answer: B — Smoothness, Not Similarity

A is the tempting one. It is the matching intuition — and note that it is stronger than what RD needs, not weaker.

C is what RD approximates, not what it assumes. D is false and would be fatal — the running variable is supposed to predict the outcome.

Lee (2008) makes a local-randomisation argument, which is why C feels right — but the identification result of Hahn, Todd and Van der Klaauw rests on continuity, not randomisation.

On D: if the running variable did not predict the outcome you would not need to model the smooth part at all, and the whole apparatus of local polynomials would be unnecessary.

Estimating the Jump

Where the hook gets paid off — and why local beats global

Section 13.2. About 13 minutes including the discussion.

This section carries the hook's payoff; do not rush it.

Right Minus Left — Always

Our programme covers tuition at or below $75,000. Written the natural way — R = income - 75_000, D = (R <= 0) — it is substantively correct and computationally poisonous. rdrobust estimates $\lim_{r \downarrow c} - \lim_{r \uparrow c}$: it reports the jump as you cross upward into ineligibility.

The fix is one line. Define the running variable as distance below the threshold, so treatment lies above zero:

R = CUTOFF - income
eligible = (R >= 0).astype(int)

Here is the payoff. Go back to the guesses on the board first.

  • If you can run this live, do: the wrong-sign version, let the room sit with minus 0.0553, then change the one line and rerun.
  • The reaction is the point and no figure reproduces it.
  • This figure is the fallback.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“The package knows which side is treated.”

It does not, and it cannot. rdrobust sees a running variable, a cutoff, and an outcome. Every means-tested benefit, every low-income subsidy, every “you qualify if your score is under X” rule assigns treatment below the line.

Pass the raw variable and you get a correctly-computed number with the wrong sign, and no warning.

The plot is the check. If the estimate says positive and the picture shows the treated side lower, believe the picture.

Tie it forward explicitly: Lab 13 opens on exactly this trap, so anyone who sleeps through this slide meets it again with their own grade attached.

Also worth saying: this error is not hypothetical — it is what the automated research run that produced this chapter actually did.

Why Local, and Not Global

A global fit must explain the outcome across the entire support. Whatever form it adopts to fit households at $45,000 and $105,000 is then imposed at the cutoff, where you actually care.

A straight line cannot bend to follow an S-curve, so it does the only thing it can: it explains the curvature by inventing a jump.

RD is local twice over. Local in estimation — only observations inside $h$ contribute, nearer ones count more (kernel='tri'). Local in interpretation — the estimand is defined at the cutoff. You buy a very clean answer to a very small question.

Gelman & Imbens (2019): high-degree global polynomials are not the fix — degree three and above behave erratically near the data boundaries and give extreme weight to distant outliers.

Figure re-simulates the chapter's S-curve design with a fresh draw; the chapter's own draw reports a global degree-1 estimate of −0.0137 against the same true jump of +0.0500. The lesson is the sign, not the third decimal.

Show the global number and ask what went wrong BEFORE explaining — students usually spot that a line cannot follow the curve.

  • Note this is the second wrong sign of the lecture and it has a completely different cause from the first: the hook's was a convention error, this one is a functional-form error.
  • Both produce a confident number pointing the wrong way.

Bandwidth

The bias–variance tradeoff, made literal — with a knob attached

Section 13.3. About 14 minutes including the poll.

This is the section that seeds Chapter 15.

How Far Are You Willing to Look?

\[ \mathrm{MSE}(h) = \mathrm{Bias}(h)^2 + \mathrm{Var}(h) \]

In words: widening the window buys precision and sells comparability. There is a middle, and the selector is picking it.

Narrow $h$: only households very close to the line, who are very comparable — low bias — but few of them, so the estimate is noisy.

Wide $h$: more observations and more precision, but you drag in households the local linear fit only approximately corrects for — bias.

This is not an analogy for the bias–variance tradeoff. It is the tradeoff. Ch 15 gives the formal decomposition; here you meet it as a parameter you must choose.

One draw cannot separate bias from noise. 300 draws can — measured in Lab 13, true jump +0.0800:

Bias grows roughly with $h^2$; the sd falls roughly with $1/\sqrt{h}$.

Have the class read the MC sd column BEFORE the bias column — the two move in opposite directions and that is the whole slide.

Say plainly that this IS the bias-variance tradeoff and that Ch 15 will decompose it; graduate students appreciate knowing a formalism is coming rather than being told to take it on faith.

Both Ends of the Grid Are Wrong

At $h = 3$: bias is 0.3% of the truth — and the MC sd is 0.031, nearly 40% of the jump itself. You could not distinguish the effect from zero in most single draws.

At $h = 50$: the sd has fallen to 0.007 and the estimate is barely half the truth. Precisely wrong.

RMSE minimised near $h = 20$. On the single draw, rdrobust's data-driven selector chose 16.1 — the right neighbourhood, and slightly conservative.

Note the crossing point: |bias| and MC sd cross a little below h = 20, which is roughly where RMSE bottoms out.

That crossing is the visual signature of every bias-variance tradeoff they will meet for the rest of the course, and it is worth naming as such now.

Poll: Which Bandwidth Would You Use?

B. $h = 20$ — RMSE is minimised there

D is the answer most of them actually practise, and the debrief is aimed squarely at it.

Answer: B — And D Is the Dangerous One

A optimises bias alone and buys it with an MC sd of 0.031 against a jump of 0.08.

C optimises variance alone and returns half the truth. Precisely wrong.

D stings because it is what most people do — and it lands close to right here (16.1), which is exactly why it is dangerous.

The selector is optimising asymptotic MSE under its own assumptions about the DGP. That is a choice, not a discovery — and it is why you still report the sensitivity table.

The last box is the transferable lesson: an automated choice is still a choice, and the fact that it is defensible does not make it invisible.

Same point will return in Ch 15 for hyperparameter tuning and in Ch 24 for nuisance-model selection.

Why the Lab's Curvature Sits Off the Cutoff

If both sides curve identically, widening $h$ biases the left limit and the right limit by exactly the same amount — and the bias cancels in the difference. Every bandwidth looks unbiased.

The lab curves differently by side — -6e-5·x² below, +3e-5·x² above. That asymmetry is what makes widening the window actually cost you.

Think like an economist. When you build a simulation to study a method, ask what it has quietly assumed away. A symmetric DGP is not a neutral choice — it is a choice that makes the estimator look better than it is.

Ninety seconds, no more, but do not cut it: this is a real trap in designing your own Monte Carlos, and the lab was rebuilt for exactly this reason.

An earlier version put the curvature at the cutoff and the bandwidth exercise showed nothing at all — every h came back unbiased and the tradeoff was invisible.

The Bandwidth for the Estimate Is Not the Bandwidth for the Interval

CALONICO, CATTANEO & TITIUNIK (2014)

A data-driven bandwidth minimising the asymptotic MSE of the point estimator. rdrobust computes it by default.

CALONICO, CATTANEO & FARRELL (2020)

At $h_{MSE}$ the bias shrinks at the same rate as the standard deviation, so it never vanishes relative to the noise — and a nominal 95% interval can cover the truth substantially less than 95% of the time.

Robust bias correction. Estimate the bias with a second, wider bandwidth $b$; subtract it; inflate the standard errors to account for having estimated it. That is what the “Robust” row reports — and why it differed from the conventional row earlier (+0.0548 vs +0.0541) and carried a wider interval.

Report the robust row. The conventional row is the point estimate; the robust row is the one whose confidence interval means what it says.

Always report a sensitivity table. 0.054 at the optimal $h$, 0.055 at half, 0.053 at double → you have a result. 0.054, 0.011, 0.089 → you have an artifact and no finding. The table is not an appendix. It is the result.

This is the most technically exposed moment in the lecture.

  • State the result and the intuition — the bias has to vanish faster than the variance for the interval to be honest — and do NOT derive it.
  • Point the student who asks to Calonico, Cattaneo and Farrell (2020) and move on.
  • Also worth naming the common misconception here: "a wider bandwidth uses more data so it must be better" — the extreme case is a bandwidth wide enough to include everyone, which is just the global regression that returned the wrong sign two sections ago.

Testing a Design That Can Be Tested

Density, balance, placebos — and how to read a false positive

Section 13.4. About 14 minutes including the discussion.

This is where RD earns its reputation.

Test 1 — Manipulation, via the Density

Sorting leaves a fingerprint: a pile-up on the favourable side, a hole on the other. McCrary (2008) required binning, and the bin width was a researcher degree of freedom. Cattaneo, Jansson & Ma (2020) replaced it with a local polynomial density estimator on the raw variable — no binning.

When manipulation is implausible, say why. Financial aid uses prior-prior-year tax data, so a family applying in 2026 cannot restructure its 2024 income. That argument is worth more than any $p$-value — and you get it by reading the programme's rules, not its data.

Run the test on clean data first, then on manipulated data, and let the jump from p = 0.76 to p = 0.000 make the case.

  • The diagnostic works in both directions, which is what you want.
  • The `rddensity` call is one line: `rddensity(X=R, c=0)`, and you select `t_jk` / `p_jk` explicitly because the asymptotic rows come back NaN.

Test 2 — Covariate Balance at the Cutoff

Under continuity, variables determined before treatment must not jump at the cutoff. So run the same RD with a pre-determined covariate as the outcome.

balance = rdrobust(y=hs_gpa, x=R,
                   c=0, p=1, kernel='tri')
HS GPA jump: +0.0286
             (SE 0.0272)  p = 0.409

GPA does not jump. Retention does, large and precisely estimated.

That contrast is the evidence — not the $p$-value on its own. A failure to reject can always reflect low power.

Callback to Ch 9. This is the balance table you run after a randomised experiment, applied at a threshold instead of across arms. The logic is identical because the claim is identical: assignment is as good as random here.

In the lab you write this test yourself — then deliberately break a covariate and confirm the test catches it.

Emphasise the contrast framing. A student who reports "p = 0.409, balance passes" and stops has missed the argument; the point is that the covariate is flat where the outcome is not, and the two were estimated the same way on the same data with the same bandwidth.

Test 3 — Placebo Cutoffs, and an Inconvenient One

Run the analysis at thresholds where nothing happened. A design that finds effects at fake cutoffs is finding noise or misspecification.

At $c = -10{,}000$: $p = 0.036$, on data where by construction nothing whatsoever happened.

That is not a flaw in the demonstration. It is the demonstration.

Ch 8, inside a robustness check. Four tests at the 5% level on null data reject about one time in five. Read placebos as a pattern, not a series of hypothesis tests.

The question is not “did any placebo reject?” but “is the real effect distinguishable from the distribution of fake ones?” Here: +0.054 against a spread of roughly ±0.01.

Highest-value teaching moment in the section.

  • Ask why a cutoff with nothing at it returned p = 0.036 and WAIT — someone will get to multiple testing, and the Ch 8 callback lands much harder coming from the room than from you.
  • Then go straight into the discussion.

Discussion: The Inconvenient Placebo

Your placebo at $c = -10{,}000$ returns $p = 0.036$ — on data where you know nothing happened. Your referee will find it.

What goes in the paper?

Think about:

How do you present the placebos so the pattern is what the reader sees?

Consider:

What placebo result would make you abandon the design?

2 min pairs → 3 min share

Push for three things. (1) Report it — a reader who finds it themselves assumes you hid it, which is a worse paper than one with an awkward number in it.

  • (2) Interpret the pattern: show the distribution of placebo estimates with the real one against it; magnitude does the work p-values cannot.
  • (3) Name what would change your mind — a placebo comparable in MAGNITUDE to the real effect, or one that survives varying the bandwidth, or several rejecting in the same direction.
  • That third one is the honest answer and students rarely reach it unprompted.

Real Data, and Real Limits

What the design buys, and what it refuses to tell you

Sections 13.4b through 13.6. About 20 minutes including Poll 3 and the closing discussion.

A Real One — Incumbency Advantage

DATA SNAPSHOT

U.S. Senate elections, 1914–2004. 1,297 state-level races. Running variable: the Democratic party's margin of victory in the previous election for that seat, centred at zero. Outcome: Democratic vote share in the next election for the same seat. Distributed with rdrobust; design descends from Lee (2008).

Conventional: +7.414  (SE 1.459)
Robust:       +7.507
      95% CI [+4.094, +10.919]
h = 17.75 margin points
n used: 360 left, 323 right
density test p = 0.447

The naive comparison is hopeless: parties that won last time are stronger, better funded and more popular — and all of that persists and also predicts winning next time. Among races decided by a hair, which party ended up ahead is close to a coin flip.

The density test is the less important half of the argument.

The important half is the mechanism: a candidate cannot fine-tune their vote margin. You can campaign harder, spend more, turn out more voters — you cannot arrange to win by 0.4 points rather than lose by 0.4.

That is what a strong continuity argument sounds like. A test that fails to reject can reflect low power — exactly as with pre-trends in Ch 11. The statistic supports the argument; it does not substitute for it.

Run this live if you can — it is real data over a network in two lines, and the contrast with the simulation is the point.

  • Ask BEFORE running the density test whether they expect manipulation, and make them articulate why not.
  • "You cannot fine-tune your vote margin" is the single clearest institutional continuity defence in the book.
  • Then ask what population the 7.5 points describes, which sets up the poll.

Poll: Which Population?

Answer: C — The Bandwidth Is Not the Population

B is the one worth the time. Those 683 races are the observations used in estimation. But the estimand is defined at $R_i = c$ — a limit, a point. The bandwidth is a computational device, not a description of the population.

A and D are the extrapolation error in two flavours — and D is the one that shows up in policy memos, because it is what somebody wanted the answer to be.

Whether incumbency is worth 7.5 points in a race won by 30 is a question this design does not answer and was never going to.

  • Landslide seats differ from marginal seats in ways that go well beyond the margin itself.
  • Hold that thought — it is exactly the closing discussion.

Economist at Work: The Elite Illusion

Boston's exam schools admit by entrance-exam score, so every year some applicants clear the threshold by a point and others miss by a point. Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist & Pathak (Econometrica, 2014) ran the fuzzy RD in Boston and New York.

They found essentially nothing — across state tests, PSAT and SAT participation and scores, and AP outcomes. Students just above the line attended schools with dramatically higher-achieving peers, and did not on average learn measurably more.

The graduates really are outstanding. The schools really do produce them. But the schools select them rather than making them — and for the marginal applicant, the only one this design speaks to, the elite school added no measurable value.

Set that against Chapter 12's Boston charter lotteries, which found large positive effects — same city, overlapping research group.

The two results are not in conflict. Different designs, different populations, different assumptions, answering different questions that sound identical in English.

Learning to hear the difference is most of what this module has been for.

Ninety seconds by design, but expect pushback and let it run for a moment if it comes — arguing about which population an estimate describes IS the skill.

Do not develop the charter-lottery result here; it belongs to Ch 12 and one sentence of contrast is the right dose.

Fuzzy RD, or IV at the Cutoff

Some eligible students never claim the grant; some ineligible ones win an appeal. Crossing the cutoff changes the probability of treatment. You already know how to handle this — it is Chapter 12 in different clothing.

\[ \tau_{FRD} = \frac{\lim_{r \downarrow c} E[Y_i \mid R_i=r] - \lim_{r \uparrow c} E[Y_i \mid R_i=r]}{\lim_{r \downarrow c} E[D_i \mid R_i=r] - \lim_{r \uparrow c} E[D_i \mid R_i=r]} \]

In words: the jump in the outcome divided by the jump in the treatment probability. Numerator is the reduced form, denominator the first stage — Ch 12's Wald estimator, evaluated at a threshold. In rdrobust it is one argument: fuzzy=D_actual.

Everything from Ch 12 transfers. Monotonicity is required. The estimand is a LATE for compliers — now doubly local, since they must also be at the cutoff. A treatment probability that rises from 0.62 to 0.71 at the threshold is a weak-instrument problem wearing a cutoff: report the first-stage jump alongside the estimate, exactly as you would an $F$-statistic.

Fuzzy RD is not a weaker sharp RD. Sharp gives the effect of crossing the threshold; fuzzy gives the effect of treatment for compliers. A sharp specification on imperfectly-complied data estimates an intention-to-treat effect — legitimate, often the policy-relevant quantity, but attenuated toward zero by everyone who did not comply.

Angrist & Lavy (1999). Israeli schools followed Maimonides' rule, a twelfth-century principle capping class size at 40: 40 pupils form one class, 41 split into two of roughly 20. Schools deviate, so crossing 40 changes the probability of a small class. The instrument's credibility rests on a religious ruling applied administratively eight hundred years later — which is the flavour of most good RD work. You are not designing an experiment; you are noticing that somebody else's arbitrary rule already ran one.

Lead with the observation that the fuzzy formula IS the Wald estimator and write both on the board together.

  • Students who understood Ch 12 will find this section easy and should be TOLD so.
  • The ITT-versus-LATE distinction is the piece most often missed in applied work — a sharp specification on fuzzy data is not wrong, it answers a different question, and papers frequently fail to say which they intend.

What RD Cannot Tell You

Everything so far delivers $E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\mid R_i=c]$: the average effect for units at the threshold. Almost nobody wants that number.

1. Extrapolation

A legislator asking about raising the threshold $75k → $90k wants the effect inside the region RD is silent on. Extrapolating requires assumptions about how the effect varies with $R$ — exactly the assumptions the design was built to avoid.

Cattaneo, Keele, Titiunik & Vazquez-Bare (2021): multiple cutoffs partially identify effects away from any one of them. Most designs have only one.

2. Discrete running variables

The limits presume you can get arbitrarily close to $c$. With integer test scores or age in whole years you cannot, and no amount of data closes the gap.

Kolesár & Rothe (2018): clustering SEs by the running variable substantially over-rejects. Use honest confidence intervals that bound the CEF's second derivative — paying for discreteness in width.

3. Compound cutoffs

If a second programme shares your threshold, every diagnostic passes — density smooth, covariates balanced, placebos null — and the estimate is still the combined effect of two interventions.

No statistical test detects this. Only reading the institutional rules does.

Before running an RD, find out what else happens at your cutoff. Read the statute, the eligibility manual, the internal policy document. Programmes cluster at round numbers, and administrators reuse thresholds because it is convenient.

Belabour the extrapolation limit — it is where RD results get misused in policy debates and students will encounter that misuse.

Discrete running variables matter more than they sound: exam scores, ages, household size, days since an event are all discrete, so the default output is invalid in a great many real applications.

Discussion: Raise the Threshold?

Your RD says the grant raises retention by 5.4 points at $75,000, with a tight interval. A state senator wants to raise the threshold to $90,000 and asks what your estimate implies about the households who would newly qualify.

Think about:

What do you tell her — in one sentence she can put in a memo?

Consider:

What would you need in order to say anything at all about those households?

2 min pairs → 3 min share

Where to land: the estimate is silent on those households, and saying so is the professional answer, not a failure.

  • What would help — multiple cutoffs across regions or cohorts; an explicit, defended structural assumption about how the effect varies with income; or a different design.
  • What does not help: assuming a constant effect because it is convenient.
  • Push on the fact that "I cannot answer that" is a complete and useful answer when it is followed by "here is what would let me."

Key Takeaways

Continuity is the whole assumption. Weaker than parallel trends or exclusion — and, uniquely in this module, it has observable implications you can test.

Right limit minus left limit, always. Treat people below the threshold and you get a correctly-computed number with the wrong sign. The plot is the check.

Estimate locally. A global polynomial must explain the whole support and imposes that structure at the cutoff — which flipped the sign at degree one.

Bandwidth is bias–variance with a knob. Bias −0.0003 → −0.0382, sd 0.0309 → 0.0071, RMSE minimised near $h=20$. Report the robust row.

The estimand is the effect at the cutoff — which is almost never the population a decision-maker is asking about. Every design in this module ultimately rests on knowing how the world that produced your data works, and that knowledge does not come from the data.

The capstone line closes the identification module, not just the lecture.

  • Deliver it slowly.
  • Chapters 10 through 13 gave four ways to identify a causal effect, each with a characteristic assumption and a characteristic failure — and the tests get better every year, but none of them substitutes for reading the institutions.

Lab 13 — Diagnosing a Jump

45 min core + 20 min extension  •  deliverable: src/rd_tools.py  •  three diagnoses

1. The estimate that is a bandwidth

A grant at a need index of 50, true effect 8 points; an analyst's global quadratic reports 3.6. You run the 300-replication Monte Carlo and read the tradeoff off your own output.

2. The two tests that can refute you

Density test on clean data, then on a population that sorts. Then you write the covariate continuity test yourself — and break a covariate to confirm it catches it.

3. A real one

Lee, Moretti & Butler (2004), “Do Voters Affect or Elect Policies?”, QJE 119(3). US House, 1946–1995, at the 50% Democratic vote-share threshold.

Get the attribution right. The lab's outcome is the representative's inflation-adjusted ADA score (Americans for Democratic Action — the share of ADA-selected roll-call votes cast the liberal way, adjusted across congresses). Higher = more liberal; the estimate is about +46 ADA points. That is a test of partisan divergence against median-voter convergence — not Lee (2008)'s incumbency advantage, even though it uses Lee's close-election design and the same threshold.

Extension: fuzzy RD as IV — simulate a threshold that raises treatment probability from 0.2 to 0.7 and show the sharp estimate is attenuated by roughly the compliance rate.

Say the attribution warning out loud.

  • Students conflate these two every year, and the give-away is the OUTCOME: today's Senate example was vote share in the next election (incumbency advantage); the lab's is how the winner votes once seated (policy divergence).
  • Same design, same threshold, completely different question.
  • Also warn them the ADA score runs from about -28 to +129 in this sample rather than 0 to 100, because the Groseclose-Levitt-Snyder adjustment rescales it.
