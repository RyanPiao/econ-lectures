Four Numbers

Section 1 • LO 18.1 • and the ratios you can build from them

The Denominator Is the Question

Pred. positivePred. negative
Actually positiveTPFN
Actually negativeFPTN

Four counts. Every metric you will meet is a ratio of some of them, and the metric's meaning is fixed by its denominator.

$$\text{Recall} = \text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \qquad \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}$$

$$\text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN}$$

Recall divides by everything that actually happened. "Of the 36 recessions, how many did we catch?" The denominator is fixed by the world.

Precision divides by everything we predicted. "Of our alarms, how many were right?" We choose the denominator, and it moves when the threshold moves.

FPR divides by everything that did not happen. "Of the 487 calm months, how many did we wrongly flag?"

3 min. Derive all three from the table rather than presenting them as definitions — students who can rebuild the table can rebuild any metric. Then ask which of recall or precision a central banker cares about, then an asset manager, and let the disagreement stand. That disagreement is the chapter's thesis in miniature and you will cash it in Section 5.

Every Metric Contains a \( \beta \)

$$F_\beta = (1 + \beta^2)\cdot\frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\beta^2 \cdot \text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$$

Where: \( \beta \) is how many times more you value recall than precision. \( \beta = 1 \) recovers \( F_1 \); \( \beta = 2 \) weights recall four times as heavily; \( \beta = 0.5 \) does the reverse.

Accuracy fixes an implicit weighting by class size.

\( F_1 \) fixes \( \beta = 1 \), visibly and adjustably.

AUC integrates over a whole distribution of weightings. Section 6 shows that distribution is stranger than it looks.

The advice is not "use \( F_2 \)." If you can state \( \beta \) you can state a cost ratio; if you can state a cost ratio, skip the summary metric and optimise expected cost directly. \( F_\beta \) is most useful as a diagnostic that you have an opinion about the asymmetry.

2 min. The interpretation line matters more than the formula — never leave the algebra bare. The three cards are a ladder from "assumption invisible" to "assumption averaged over", and Section 6 will knock the top rung out from under them.

The One Interpretation That Is Exactly Right

AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive case receives a higher predicted score than a randomly chosen negative case.

So 0.811 means: pick a month that preceded a recession and one that did not, and the model scores the right one higher about 81% of the time. A measure of ranking quality, nothing more.

Threshold-independent. It summarises the whole curve, so it is unaffected by the 0.5 default that ruined last week's accuracy figure. A genuine strength — it is why AUC survived that demolition while accuracy did not.

Invariant to any monotone transformation. Halve every probability, square them, apply any order-preserving function — the ranking is unchanged, so AUC is unchanged. That is why AUC cannot see the hook's failure.

Misconception: "An AUC of 0.81 means the model is 81% accurate." It says nothing about accuracy at any threshold, nothing about whether the probabilities are believable, and nothing about behaviour on the cases you act on.

3 min. Have the room repeat the boxed sentence out loud — it feels silly and it works; nearly every misuse of AUC traces to not knowing it. Flag the second property as "remember this for ninety seconds" and then deliver.

The Proof Takes Ten Seconds

Multiply every predicted probability by 0.5. The ranking is untouched, so AUC is identical. Every probability is now half what it should be, so calibration is destroyed.

Discrimination and calibration are independent properties, and any metric that reads only the order is blind to one of them by construction.

Groups are defined once, on the original predictions, so each point simply slides left. The two curves describe the same months.

2 min. Groups are defined ONCE on the original predictions, so each point simply slides left — do not let anyone think the two curves compare different populations. AUC is equal to every decimal Python prints, not "approximately equal". Next slide explains the Brier row, which is the graduate half of this argument.

The Curve, and Its Moving Baseline

A random model has AUC 0.5 — a fixed, familiar anchor. A random model's PR-AUC is the base rate, here 0.069.

So 0.188 is 2.73× random. A real but modest achievement, and one that 0.811 makes sound far grander.

2 min. The vertical spike at recall 0 is the standard PR artefact (one alarm, one hit) — name it before a student does. The baseline point is the one to insist on: ROC's constant 0.5 is convenient and is exactly what makes ROC flatter rare-event problems, because the reference point does not move as the problem gets harder.

Precision Moves When the World Does

Recall and FPR condition on the truth. Denominators are "all positives" and "all negatives", so they are properties of the model and do not change when the base rate changes.

Precision conditions on the predictions. So it does change. The same model, applied where recessions are twice as common, delivers higher precision with no improvement whatsoever in the model.

A fraud model validated in a market with a 0.5% fraud rate and deployed in one with 0.1% shows the same recall, the same FPR, and much worse precision — and the analytics team reports a model that has "degraded" when nothing about it has changed. The base rate moved.

So precision figures are not portable. "Our model achieves 40% precision" without the base rate it was measured at is close to meaningless — the same sentence describes an excellent model in one market and a poor one in another.

2 min. This is the slide that saves a graduate a bad quarter in industry. Name the reporting rule out loud: report the base rate alongside precision, always, for exactly the reason you report it alongside accuracy. The next slide gives them the identity that makes the claim exact rather than qualitative.

Advanced Treatment: ROC and PR Are Deterministically Related

Write \( \pi = P(Y=1) \) for the base rate, and let a threshold produce true positive rate TPR and false positive rate FPR. Then

$$\text{Precision} = \frac{\pi \cdot \text{TPR}}{\pi \cdot \text{TPR} + (1-\pi)\cdot \text{FPR}} = \left(1 + \frac{1-\pi}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\text{FPR}}{\text{TPR}}\right)^{-1}$$

Interpretation. The factor \( (1-\pi)/\pi \) is the whole story. At \( \pi = 0.5 \) it equals 1 and precision responds to the FPR/TPR ratio directly. At our \( \pi = 0.0688 \) it equals 13.53, so any given FPR is amplified more than thirteenfold in its effect on precision.

A respectable-looking ROC point — FPR 0.19, TPR 0.75 — therefore has precision \( \left(1 + 13.53 \times 0.2533\right)^{-1} = \mathbf{0.226} \). The two curves were never in conflict. They are one object, read through a base rate.

Davis & Goadrich (2006), first result. A curve dominating in ROC space dominates in PR space, and conversely. The two never disagree about which of two models is uniformly better — only about how much, and about where the useful operating points are.

Second result. PR-AUC has no fixed baseline: a random classifier traces a horizontal line at \( \pi \). ROC's constant 0.5 is convenient, and is exactly what makes ROC flatter rare-event problems.

3 min. Graduate-only block — the 3916 edition stops at "ROC flatters, PR does not". State the identity, do the one substitution, do NOT derive it live. Saito and Rehmsmeier (2015) is the citation for the practical upshot: on rare-event biological data, ROC curves for models of visibly different quality are nearly indistinguishable while their PR curves separate cleanly.

The Amplification Factor, Drawn

Top panel. Hold the operating point fixed. Recall and FPR are flat lines — they are properties of the model. Precision is a curve, because it is a property of the model and the world.

Bottom panel. \( (1-\pi)/\pi \) is what does it. Below about \( \pi = 0.1 \) it is steep, which is why rare-event precision is so unstable across markets and so easy to misread as model drift.

At small \( \pi \), \( (1-\pi)/\pi \approx 1/\pi \). Halve the base rate and you roughly halve precision at a fixed operating point. Hold that thought for ninety seconds.

2 min. The last box is the poll's answer, stated in advance on purpose — graduate polls should test whether they can apply a stated result under pressure, not whether they memorised it. If nobody gets it, that is diagnostic and worth knowing before Section 5.

Ranking Is Not Level

Section 4 • LO 18.3 • the hook, developed properly

Two Achievements, Not One

Discrimination

The ability to rank: do positives receive higher scores than negatives? AUC measures it. Our model is good at it — 0.811.

Calibration

The accuracy of the level: among cases assigned probability \( p \), does the event occur a fraction \( p \) of the time? Our model fails it badly, in the worst possible place.

Different uses need different ones. If you only rank — triage a queue, prioritise audits, order applicants — discrimination is sufficient. If the probability enters a calculation — expected loss, expected revenue, a capital requirement, a policy trigger — you need calibration, and AUC will not tell you whether you have it.

Report a reliability diagram alongside any AUC you publish. It costs five lines.

2 min. The rank-versus-level distinction is the single most transferable idea in the chapter and it is what Discussion 1 turns on, so land it cleanly. Misconception to name out loud: "a high AUC means the probabilities can be trusted" — AUC is mathematically incapable of assessing that, and our model is the proof.

The pattern is instructive rather than uniformly bad, and reading it properly is the skill.

Bottom two bands (296 + 107 months): close to the diagonal and slightly conservative.

Middle two bands: under-predict. When the model says 18%, recessions followed 38% of the time.

3 min. Both routes found the same 2022-24 episode and neither would have found it alone: the calibration table says THAT the confident predictions fail without saying why; the term-premium argument explains why without establishing how badly. Good practice runs both, because they are cheap and they corroborate each other.

The Caveat, and Do Not Skip It

Twenty-one months is a small bin, and they are not independent — consecutive months of one macroeconomic episode. A calibration table computed on autocorrelated data overstates its own sample size.

Wrong reading. "The failure rate at high probabilities is precisely 0%."

Right reading. "This model's confident predictions are dominated by a single regime in which it was wrong."

The effective sample size behind that row is closer to one than to twenty-one. Any confidence interval computed as though those months were independent would be far too narrow. Evaluation metrics have sampling distributions too, and on dependent data those distributions are wider than the standard formulas assume. Chapter 20 builds the machinery.

2 min. A chapter about honest evaluation that oversold its own evidence would be self-refuting, so this slide is not optional. It also sets up the 5200 lab, whose whole spine is putting an interval on every evaluation number — and specifically on the DIFFERENCE between two models, which is the interval almost nobody reports.

What You Would Do About It

Recalibrate post hoc. Fit a second, monotone map from raw scores to observed frequencies on held-out data — Platt scaling (a one-dimensional logistic) for small samples, isotonic regression (a non-parametric step function) above roughly a thousand calibration points. Both are monotone, so they leave AUC exactly unchanged while fixing the levels — which is itself a neat demonstration that the two properties are separable.

But recalibration will not save this model. Post-hoc methods learn a correction from historical data, and our failure is not a stable bias — it is one regime in which the feature stopped meaning what it meant. A monotone correction fitted on 1982–2021 has no way to know 2023's inversions were different in kind. Recalibration fixes systematic overconfidence; it does not fix a feature whose relationship to the outcome changed.

So the honest response is narrower. Report the probabilities with an explicit regime caveat, widen the intervals, or — the answer the NY Fed effectively took — keep publishing the model unchanged while separately publishing the term-premium decomposition explaining why the current reading should be discounted. That preserves a thirty-year track record, which has real value, while telling users what they need. Not every model failure is repaired by changing the model.

3 min. The last sentence is the one to say slowly. Students trained to fix things reach for isotonic regression here, and it is precisely the wrong tool — ask someone to say why out loud before you advance the third fragment. Note the leakage requirement: calibration data must be held out, for the Chapter 15 reasons.

The Threshold

Three Decision-Makers, One Model

symmetric (1:1)        optimal t = 0.425   theory 0.500   caught  0/36   false alarms   0
central banker (1:9)   optimal t = 0.099   theory 0.100   caught 28/36   false alarms  97

The central-banker row confirms the theory almost exactly: 0.099 against a predicted 0.100, catching 28 of 36 recessions at the price of 97 false alarms over forty years.

The theoretical optimum is \( t^{*} = C_{FP}/(C_{FP} + C_{FN}) \). Read it backwards and every cutoff you have ever shipped is a claim about relative costs: $$\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}} = \frac{t}{1-t}$$

2 min. Do not gloss the two corner rows — the next slide is entirely about them and they are the more interesting result. Note the grid is np.linspace(0.01, 0.60, 200), so 0.425 is a grid point, not a coincidence.

One Interior Optimum, Two Corners

The model's highest predicted probability is 0.4231. Thresholds of 0.5 and 0.8 are therefore unreachable; the optimum saturates at the top of the achievable range and the classifier never fires at all.

That is not the formula failing. It is the formula reporting something true and useful: for a decision-maker with symmetric costs, or one who fears false alarms more than misses, this model should never trigger action.

A model can be genuinely informative and still be the wrong basis for a particular decision — and discovering that before deployment is worth more than any metric.

3 min, and give them. Students expect the formula to always produce an interior answer, and the honest reading is more useful than the arithmetic. Ask: what would you have to change to give the asset manager an interior solution? Answers: a better model, a different feature, or a decision that tolerates lower confidence — and note that only one of those three is your job.

When the Threshold Is Not Yours to Choose

Payment networks. Visa's Dispute Monitoring Programme placed merchants in a Standard tier at a 0.9% chargeback ratio and Excessive at 1.8%; from 2025 those consolidated into VAMP, with its own thresholds. The exact numbers move — and that is the point: the constraint is set by a network, not by your loss function.

A fraud team therefore does not choose the profit-maximising threshold. They solve a constrained problem: maximise expected profit subject to the chargeback ratio staying under whatever the current programme allows — a cap on the false-negative rate. The unconstrained optimum is frequently infeasible, and the binding constraint sets the threshold instead of the cost ratio.

Banking supervision. Under SR 11-7 — the Fed and OCC's model risk management guidance — a threshold sits inside the model's approved design. Changing it without revalidation is a control failure, not a tweak. The threshold is a governed parameter, and a data scientist who nudges it to improve a dashboard has made a regulatory filing inaccurate.

2 min. Graduate students go into these jobs, and several will discover this the hard way. The general form worth naming: real threshold choices are rarely unconstrained optimisations, and the constraint usually comes from outside the firm. Section 7 adds a third master — subgroup parity — so threshold selection ends the lecture with three.

Even read conservatively, merchants lose roughly four times more revenue to blocking legitimate customers than to the fraud the blocking exists to prevent. The direction is not contested, and the direction is what should move your threshold.

Think Like an Economist: The Implied Exchange Rate

$$\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}} = \frac{t}{1-t}$$

Interpretation. Whatever threshold you use implies an exchange rate between false positives and false negatives. A default of 0.5 asserts they cost exactly the same. A threshold of 0.1 asserts a miss is nine times worse than a false alarm.

Compute the implied ratio for whatever cutoff you are about to ship. State it in one sentence. Then ask whether you would defend it out loud to the person who bears the cost. If not, you have found your real threshold by working backwards from a claim you are willing to defend.

This is the single most portable habit in the course. It is also today's exit ticket.

2 min. Insist on this one. It costs nothing, it is defensible in any room, and it converts an unexamined default into an argument someone can disagree with — which is the whole professional point. Tell them now that it is the exit ticket so they think about a real cutoff during the next fifteen minutes.

Going Deeper: Decision Curve Analysis

The problem this chapter keeps hitting: costs matter, and nobody can state them in currency. Vickers & Elkin (2006) work around it by letting the threshold encode the cost ratio rather than requiring it as an input.

A decision-maker willing to act at threshold \( t \) is revealing that they regard a false positive as costing \( t/(1-t) \) times a false negative — the exchange rate, read backwards. Define net benefit:

$$NB(t) = \frac{TP(t)}{n} - \frac{FP(t)}{n}\cdot\frac{t}{1-t}$$

Interpretation: true positives earned, minus false positives weighted by the exchange rate that threshold implies. Plot it across all plausible \( t \) and compare against two references — treat everyone (\( t = 0 \)) and treat nobody (\( NB = 0 \)) — and you can see over which range of preferences the model beats both.

The strength: it requires no dollar figures, only a range of thresholds a reasonable decision-maker might hold. Standard in clinical prediction; transfers directly to credit, targeting and policy triage.

2 min. Graduate-only block. The word to stress is PREFERENCES: a decision curve is a map over what different users care about, not a menu of thresholds you may pick from freely. This is also Diagnosis 3 of the lab, so they will implement it on Thursday.

Our Own Model, on a Decision Curve

The recession classifier beats both references over $$0.010 \le t \le 0.186$$ and \( NB(0.099) = 0.0332 \).

Read that as the range of preferences over which this model is worth consulting — not a range of thresholds you may pick freely.

Outside it, "call every month a recession" or "call none" does better than the model. That is the same corner finding as the cost curve, arrived at without anyone naming a dollar.

Hand's Critique

Section 6 • Formal Sketch • AUC as an incoherent cost distribution

AUC Does Not Eliminate the Cost Assumption

Section 2 sold AUC as threshold-independent, which sounds like an unambiguous virtue. Hand (2009) shows it conceals a serious problem.

The argument. Any threshold choice is optimal under some ratio of misclassification costs. Integrating over all thresholds, as AUC does, is therefore equivalent to integrating over a distribution of cost ratios. AUC does not eliminate the cost assumption — it averages over an implicit one.

The difficulty is what that implicit distribution turns out to be. Hand derives it and shows it depends on the empirical score distribution of the classifier being evaluated. Two models with different score distributions are therefore evaluated under two different cost distributions.

Why that is incoherent. Comparing A and B by AUC asks: "is A better under A's implied costs than B is under B's implied costs?" That question has no decision-theoretic meaning. A coherent comparison holds the cost structure fixed and varies the model. AUC does the reverse.

3 min. Graduate-only. Do not derive the implied distribution — state the claim, name the paper, and let the reference carry the proof. The moment that lands is the rhetorical question in the last box; say it slowly and watch the room realise they have been doing this for two years. Worse still, the implied distributions are typically not ones any decision-maker would endorse if shown them explicitly.

The Fix, and the Honest Reading

The H-measure replaces the model-dependent implicit distribution with an explicit, standardised prior over cost ratios — Hand suggests a Beta distribution reflecting plausible asymmetries — and reports expected minimum loss under that fixed prior. Every model is then scored under identical assumptions, restoring comparability.

Do not abandon AUC. Its pairwise interpretation is exact, and it remains a useful, widely understood summary of ranking. But when AUC is used to choose between models whose score distributions differ markedly — which is exactly what your lab's three models do — the comparison is on weaker ground than it appears.

The more defensible procedure is the one this lecture has argued for throughout: specify the costs you actually face, and evaluate at the operating point they imply.

Hand, D. J. (2009), "Measuring Classifier Performance: A Coherent Alternative to the Area Under the ROC Curve," Machine Learning 77(1), 103–123.

Fairness Is a Constraint

The Four-Fifths Rule, as Arithmetic

New York City's Local Law 144 requires an annual independent bias audit of automated employment decision tools, published publicly, and it specifies the arithmetic: compute the selection rate per group, then the impact ratio — each group's rate divided by the highest group's.

ApplicantsAdvancedSelection rate
Group A1,0002400.240
Group B6001080.180

$$\text{impact ratio} = \frac{0.180}{0.240} = 0.75$$

0.75 is below 0.80, so this flags. Evidence of potential disparate impact, and under Local Law 144 it must be published.

Nothing here says the model is wrong. If group B genuinely has a lower rate of the outcome being predicted, a well-calibrated model should advance fewer of them — and the rule flags a model doing what a statistician would call correct.

Fixing it means moving a threshold for one group. Lower B's bar until its selection rate reaches 0.192 and the ratio clears 0.80 — but now a predicted 0.30 means "advance" for one applicant and "reject" for another.

3 min. Present the arithmetic first — it is concrete, it is law, and it gives students something to compute. Threshold selection now has THREE masters: expected cost, regulatory caps like the chargeback ratio, and subgroup parity. The second fragment is the calibration break the impossibility theorem describes, arriving as a concrete operational choice rather than a theorem.

Going Deeper: The Impossibility Theorem, Stated

Let groups \( A \) and \( B \) have base rates \( \pi_A \neq \pi_B \). A risk score cannot simultaneously satisfy:

1

Calibration within groups: \( P(Y=1 \mid \hat{p}=v, G=g) = v \) for both groups

2

Balance for the negative class: equal expected score among true negatives

3

Balance for the positive class: equal expected score among true positives

Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) show these are jointly satisfiable only if \( \pi_A = \pi_B \) or prediction is perfect. Chouldechova (2017) proves the closely related result relating positive predictive value, false positive rate and false negative rate.

The intuition. Calibration ties the score to the group's actual base rate. If the base rates differ, a fixed score means a genuinely different empirical frequency in each group unless the error rates absorb the difference — and forcing the error rates equal is exactly what breaks the calibration tie. The conflict is arithmetic, not political, though the choice it forces is political.

3 min. Take the time to make this feel genuinely uncomfortable rather than presenting it as a curiosity — the point is that a technical person cannot make this problem go away with a better algorithm. Economists are unusually well placed here, because the discipline is used to explicit trade-offs between incommensurable goods, and that is worth saying out loud.

Poll: What Can You Deliver?

Two groups, different base rates. Your committee insists on all three of: calibration within groups, equal false positive rates, and equal false negative rates.

What can you deliver?

A) All three, with a group-specific threshold for each group
B) All three, if you recalibrate each group separately with isotonic regression
C) At most two — the third is arithmetically excluded
D) All three, if the model is accurate enough
1 min. Click to vote. A and B are the two technical escapes people reach for; D is the most seductive and the most revealing.

Answer: C — At Most Two

A and B fail for the same reason. A monotone per-group correction restores calibration and thereby reintroduces the error-rate gap; adjusting thresholds to equalise error rates necessarily breaks calibration for at least one group. You can move the problem. You cannot remove it.

D is the one to name out loud. "A better model would not have this problem" is wrong unless prediction becomes perfect — not merely good. The exclusion is arithmetic. There is no technical escape, and pretending otherwise is the real failure. The professional obligation is to choose visibly, and to name who chose.

2 min. Also name the misconception the chapter attacks here: "a fair model just needs equal accuracy across groups." Equal accuracy is one criterion among several and conflicts with the others whenever base rates differ — a model can be equally accurate for two groups while producing far more false positives for one of them.

An Evaluation Workflow

Section 8 • what you do on Monday

Beyond Two Classes, in One Caution

With \( J \) classes there is no single cell called "false positive". Precision and recall are defined per class, treating that class as positive and pooling the rest — \( J \) precisions and \( J \) recalls, and the question becomes how to summarise them.

Macro — unweighted mean. Every class counts equally regardless of size, so a tiny class with poor performance drags it down hard. Use it when the rare classes matter as much as the common ones, which given why you are classifying is often.

Micro — pool the counts, then take the ratio. Large classes dominate, and for single-label problems micro-averaged precision, recall and \( F_1 \) all collapse to overall accuracy, inheriting every objection above.

Weighted — mean weighted by class frequency. Micro's problem in gentler form. And it is the library default.

The default quietly reproduces the accuracy trap on multi-class problems. If your rare classes are the ones you care about, write average='macro' deliberately. AUC extends too — one-versus-rest or one-versus-one — but its interpretation degrades: "the probability a random positive outranks a random negative" has no clean multi-class analogue.

2 min. For ordered outcomes, where the categories have a natural sequence, metrics that respect the ordering and penalise being wrong BY MORE are preferable to any of these three. Then run the poll immediately — it tests exactly this slide under time pressure.

C is close, and is the sharper trap elsewhere. On single-label problems micro-\( F_1 \) equals accuracy exactly — the metric this lecture spent seventy minutes dismantling, reappearing under a new name because nobody typed an argument.

1 min. The one-line takeaway: a default is a cost assumption someone else made for you. That sentence is the whole lecture, compressed, and it is worth being the last thing you say before the takeaways slide.

The Checklist

1. Report the base rate first. Accuracy has a floor at it, PR-AUC has its baseline at it, and precision changes with it. One number, three consequences.

2. Never report accuracy alone. If you report it, put the base rate beside it so a reader can see whether it means anything.

3. Choose the curve by class balance. ROC when roughly balanced, precision-recall when positives are rare.

4. Check calibration if the probability is used as a number. Five lines, and it catches failures no rank-based metric can. If the output only orders things you may skip it — but say that you did.

5. Derive the threshold from costs and state the implied exchange rate. Then check feasibility — caps and the model's own probability range may make the optimum unreachable, which is itself a finding.

6. Disaggregate. An aggregate that looks fine can conceal a group for whom the model fails — and in a growing number of jurisdictions checking is a legal obligation.

7. Report the operating point, not just the area. You deploy at one threshold. State recall and precision there.

8. Re-evaluate on a schedule. Today's failure did not appear in any metric until two years of data had accumulated.

Misconception: "Pick the metric that makes the model look best." Choosing the metric after seeing the results means choosing the cost assumption that flatters you. Fix the metric before you fit, for the same reason you fix a hypothesis before you collect data.

2 min. Present as a checklist; insist on step 5. If time is short, steps 1, 4 and 5 are the irreducible core. Step 8 connects back to the hook and forward to Ch 20 — a model evaluated once is evaluated at the moment it was least likely to be wrong.

Key Takeaways

The denominator is the question. Precision, recall and FPR share a numerator; what they divide by tells you whose problem the metric addresses.

AUC is exactly a ranking probability. Threshold-independent and invariant to every monotone transformation — which is precisely why it cannot detect miscalibration.

0.811 and 0.188 are the same model. ROC flatters under imbalance because \( (1-\pi)/\pi = 13.5 \) amplifies any FPR into precision.

Every metric encodes a cost assumption. Hand's critique shows even AUC's is there, is implicit, and depends on the model being scored.

Fairness criteria are mutually incompatible when base rates differ. The analyst's obligation is not to resolve the trade-off silently by picking a default, but to make it legible and name who chose.
