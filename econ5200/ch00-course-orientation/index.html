How the Course Works

Theory seminar, then a diagnosis-first lab — every session

The big idea: Concept Extension

You can specify a model, interpret a coefficient, and defend a standard error. What you have not been taught is what happens when the dataset has 800 columns instead of eight, when the treatment was never randomized, and when a stakeholder needs an answer by Thursday. That gap is where this course lives.

Every method arrives as an extension of econometrics you already trust —

Ridge

is penalized OLS.

LASSO

is OLS with feature selection.

Double ML

is Frisch–Waugh–Lovell with flexible nuisance estimators and a cross-fitting correction.

We run DiD and IV alongside regularization and cross-fitting; RD alongside Double ML. There are no discontinuities — only extensions of the intuition you brought with you.

Prerequisite — and one thing it is not: the formal prerequisite is ECON 5140 or 2560. It assumes you can run and defend an OLS regression. It does not assume you have written Python before — Labs 1–5 each open with a 15-minute guided on-ramp where you build the correct version yourself before you are shown a broken one. Our 100-minute sessions have room for that. Don’t skip those sections.

What you’ll be able to do by December

 Articulate an identification strategy

State the maintained assumptions, the exclusion restriction, the testable implications — then propose the falsification test that would break your own design.

 Implement the modern causal toolkit

DiD with pre-trends and staggered adoption, 2SLS with weak-instrument diagnostics, sharp and fuzzy RD, and Double ML with manual cross-fitting.

 Build pipelines that survive scrutiny

Regularized regression, calibrated classification, tree ensembles with SHAP attribution, evaluation against asymmetric business cost functions.

 Tell prediction from causation under pressure

Know when feature importance is a causal claim and when it is not — and say so out loud, in the room, to the person who does not want to hear it.

 Use AI as an accountable co-pilot

Apply P.R.I.M.E., build RAG pipelines grounded in retrieved evidence, and document every interaction to a standard that survives review.

 Deliver a quantified recommendation

“We estimate $X impact with a 90% CI of [$Y, $Z], and here are the assumptions that would invalidate it.”

A typical day: seminar, then a broken analysis

Each session divides into a Theory Seminar developing the estimator and its assumptions, and a Computational Laboratory where you implement it and diagnose what breaks. Bring a laptop every session.

 Before class

  • Read the topic chapter in the free ebook
  • Come with the assumptions in your head, not the syntax

 In class (100 min)

  • Theory Seminar — the estimator, its assumptions, its failure modes
  • Diagnosis-first lab — you are handed a flawed analysis, and you find the error, fix it, and extend it

 After class

  • Push the lab — the repository is the portfolio
  • Problem Sets and the two projects on their own schedule

Module 1 is diagnostic by design. Topics 1–6 are not introductory lessons — they are professional exercises built to surface gaps in your own preparation early, while there is still time to close them. Treat a slow Module 1 lab as a signal, not a verdict.

The AI policy: foundations first, expansion second

Northeastern gives you enterprise Claude free at claude.northeastern.edu. You will use it constantly — on a schedule.

 Manual phase — AI code generation prohibited

Every lab runs its manual parts first. Writing the estimator by hand — the cross-fitting loop, the first stage, the residualization — is what makes you able to audit the library later.

 Expansion phase — AI required

Then the boundary lifts and you extend the analysis with Claude, documenting each step with P.R.I.M.E.: Prep, Request, Iterate, Mechanism check, Evaluate.

The boundary is explicit and non-negotiable: if you cannot explain a line of AI-generated code, you cannot submit it. The standard is that your documented AI usage survives review by someone who did not trust it — which is the same standard your work will face in industry.

Topic 25 takes this further: RAG pipelines grounded in retrieved evidence, agentic workflows, and how you evaluate a generative system rather than merely admire it.

How to do well (the honest version)

Plan for roughly 12 hours/week outside class. That is what a 4-credit graduate course expects, and two 100-minute meetings are only a small part of it.

  • Do Module 1 honestly — it is diagnostic by design. If the Topic 1 lab takes you 90 minutes instead of 20, that is information. Act on it in Week 2, not Week 10.
  • Respect the manual phase — writing the estimator by hand is what makes you able to audit the library later.
  • Push every lab — the repository is the portfolio. By December it is also most of your interview.
  • Say the uncomfortable sentence — “This is predictive importance, not a causal effect.” Knowing when to say it is most of what this course teaches.
  • Ask for help — I debug both Python and economic intuition. If syntax is still the obstacle by Lab 3, come and see me.
