Your Distribution — and Two Words for the Middle

Mean — add every number up, divide by how many there are. Every value gets a vote, and a big one votes loudly.

Median — line everyone up in order, take the one in the middle. Only the rank counts, not the size.

The gap between those two markers is the rest of today.

The Mechanism, in One Equation

Take \(n\) observations. Replace exactly one of them with a value \(M\):

$$\bar{x}_{\text{contaminated}} = \bar{x}_{\text{original}} + \frac{M - x_{\text{replaced}}}{n}$$

\(M\)

the wrong value you put in — the typo, the fraud, the billionaire

\(x_{\text{replaced}}\)

the ordinary value it displaced

\(n\)

how many observations you have

\(\bar{x}\)

the mean — before, and after

The damage is \((M - x_{\text{replaced}})/n\). The denominator grows with your sample. The numerator has no upper bound at all.

What big data buys you
a smaller share of the damage per bad row

What it does not buy you
any bound at all on how bad a row can be

Misconception

“With enough data, outliers don't matter.”

This is an argument about the denominator only. At \(n = 1{,}000{,}000\), one $100 million fraudulent transaction still moves the mean by $100.

Whether $100 matters is a question about your application. It is not a question about your sample size, and no amount of data will answer it for you.

Large \(n\) shrinks the damage per bad row. It never bounds it.

2 min.

Usually most of the room. That is the point — it is a respectable-sounding sentence that is structurally wrong.

Breakdown Point

Breakdown point: the largest fraction you can replace with arbitrary values before the estimator goes anywhere.

Definition. The breakdown point \(\varepsilon^{*}\) of an estimator is the largest fraction of the sample you can replace with arbitrary values before the estimator can be driven anywhere at all.

Mean

\(1/n\)

One row. And this goes to zero as \(n\) grows — the mean gets less robust in the limit

Median

\(0.5\)

Until half the sample is corrupt, the middle rank is still held by a real observation

Trimmed mean

\(\gamma\)

A dial. At \(\gamma=0\) it is the mean; at \(\gamma=0.5\) it is the median

SAY: The breakdown point is the largest fraction of the sample an adversary can replace before the estimator can be driven anywhere at all. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: The mean's breakdown point is small.  |  A higher breakdown point makes an estimator better.

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • The third card is the one that pays off in Poll 1 in six minutes, so do not rush it.
  • Robustness is not a property you have or lack — it is a parameter you choose and then have to justify.

IF ASKED: “Why does 1/n going to zero matter?” → The mean gets *less* robust as n grows. More data is not protection — that is the whole misconception this section kills.

PODCAST 5:06 “What exact fraction of your data set needs to be completely corrupted by an adversary before your final estimate is driven to infinity.” The adversary is the bit students miss — it is a worst case, not a typical one.

Poll 1 — Where Does the Dial Sit?

What is the breakdown point of a 10% trimmed mean?

A) \(1/n\) — it is still an average, so one bad row still moves it
B) 0.10
C) 0.50 — trimming makes it behave like a median
D) It has none; trimmed means are fully robust

1 min.

Students click to vote on their devices; results appear on the next slide.

A is the productive error — it comes from thinking of trimming as averaging-then-hoping.

Answer: B — It Equals \(\gamma\)

A trimmed mean's breakdown point equals gamma. Trim ten percent and 10.1% contamination still drives it anywhere.

Trimming discards the top and bottom 10% before averaging, so up to 10% contamination is thrown away before it can touch the sum. At 10.1% the corrupted values survive into the average and it can be driven anywhere.

Common mistake: A — the extreme values never enter the sum at all. C is the \(\gamma = 0.5\) case, which is the median.

Robustness is a dial you set, not a property you either have or lack. And a dial setting is a decision you have to report.

SAY: The breakdown point of a gamma-trimmed mean is exactly gamma. At 10.1% the corrupted values survive into the average. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: Trimming makes it behave like a median.

BACKGROUND: 1 min. Whatever the split, name the A share out loud — it is the intuition that trimming is a softening rather than a deletion.

IF ASKED: “Why isn't it 0.5 like the median?” → The median is the gamma = 0.5 limit. Trimming 10% buys you 10% — you get exactly what you paid for.

Four statistics, one free diagnostic you should compute every single time.

The Trimmed Mean

The trimmed mean is a dial between mean and median, and the dial setting is the breakdown point.

$$\bar{x}_{\text{trim}}(\gamma) = \frac{1}{n - 2\lfloor n\gamma \rfloor}\sum_{i=\lfloor n\gamma \rfloor + 1}^{\,n - \lfloor n\gamma \rfloor} x_{(i)}$$

What you gain
a breakdown point of \(\gamma\) instead of \(1/n\)

What you pay
efficiency, if the data really was well behaved

scipy.stats.trim_mean(x, 0.10) — and the 0.10 goes in the methods section.

SAY: The trimmed mean is a dial: gamma zero is the mean, gamma one-half is the median, and gamma is also the breakdown point. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: Trimming is the same as deleting outliers.

BACKGROUND:

  • 2 min.
  • The efficiency cost is real but small: at gamma = 0.10 on genuinely normal data you lose only a few percent of efficiency relative to the mean.
  • That is a cheap insurance premium and it is worth saying so.

PODCAST 6:25 “A trimmed mean is brilliant because it allows you to dictate your own breakdown point based on your domain knowledge.” Better than the slide’s wording — the dial is a decision you defend, not a default.

Robust Spread: IQR and MAD

IQR is the width of the middle half. MAD is the median distance from the median.

$$\hat{\sigma} = 1.4826 \cdot \text{MAD}$$

Interpretation: on normal data MAD \(\approx 0.6745\,\sigma\), so scaling by \(1/0.6745 = 1.4826\) puts the MAD on the same ruler as a standard deviation.

IQR = interquartile range · MAD = median absolute deviation

SAY: IQR is the width of the middle half. MAD is the median distance from the median — doubly robust. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: MAD is the mean absolute deviation.

BACKGROUND: 3 min. Say the reciprocal out loud: 1 over 0.6745 is 1.4826. Your lab writes it the other way round, as 0.6745 times the deviation over the MAD. Same rule, and students lose time to this every year.

IF ASKED: “Why 1.4826?” → It rescales MAD to estimate the same sigma as s on normal data, so the two are comparable.

Poll 2 — Magnitude or Order?

Replace the single most expensive block group's value with $10 billion. Which statistics move by more than one dollar?

A) The mean only
B) The mean and the standard deviation
C) The mean, the standard deviation and the IQR

1 min.

Click to vote. D is the interesting wrong answer because it is true about the arithmetic and false about the conclusion.

Answer: B — Only the Two That Use Magnitude

Only the mean and s use magnitude. Rank-based statistics cannot see a large value become larger.

The mean and \(s\) use the raw magnitude, so both run off to infinity with \(M\). The median, IQR and MAD depend only on rank — and replacing the largest value with an even larger one changes nobody's rank. Nothing moves.

Common mistake: D. The median is computed from all the rows — but only their ordering survives the computation.

Magnitude versus order is the whole of robust statistics — and it is why the decision trees in Ch 19 get robustness for free.

SAY: Only the two that use raw magnitude move. Replacing the largest value with a larger one changes nobody's rank. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: The median is computed from all the data, so it must move a little.

BACKGROUND: 1 min. Plant the Ch 19 seed explicitly: a tree splits on "is x above this threshold", which is a rank question, so a random forest never cares how far above the threshold an outlier sits.

IF ASKED: “The median does use all the data though?” → It uses every observation's *rank*. Order is unchanged, so the middle value is unchanged. Nothing moves.

PODCAST 13:36 The thought experiment behind this poll, and it is worth doing out loud before the reveal: take the single most expensive neighbourhood in California and maliciously change its value to $10 billion. The mean and the standard deviation shoot for the moon. The median, IQR and MAD do not move one cent — because before the tamper that neighbourhood was ranked number one, and after it is still ranked number one. “The $10 billion house is just the largest house. The math doesn’t care how large it is.” Rank acts as a firewall against unbounded magnitude.

Advanced Treatment

What “robust” means when you write it down properly.

Finite-Sample Breakdown Point

QUESTION 1 OF 2How MANY bad points can you survive? Answer: one number between 0 and 0.5. \(\varepsilon^{*}=1/n\) means one row is enough; 0.5 means half your data.

$$\varepsilon^{*}_n(T, \mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{n} \max\left\{m : \sup_{\mathbf{y}} |T(\mathbf{y}) - T(\mathbf{x})| < \infty \right\}$$

\(T\)

the estimator — the recipe that turns a dataset into one number: mean, median, trimmed mean

\(\mathbf{x}\)

your real data

\(\mathbf{y}\)

the same data after the adversary has replaced \(m\) of the points

\(|T(\mathbf{y})-T(\mathbf{x})|\)

how far your answer moved because of them

Mean: \(m=1\) with \(M\to\infty\) already breaks it, so \(\varepsilon^{*}=1/n \to 0\)

Median: you need \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1\) points, so \(\varepsilon^{*}\approx 0.5\)

This is a worst-case guarantee. It says nothing about typical data — which is exactly why it is worth having.

2 min.

The supremum over y is the adversarial part and it is what makes this a guarantee rather than a description.

  • Contrast with an average-case argument, which is what most people are implicitly making when they say "in practice this is fine".

SAY IT THIS WAY IF THEY LOOK LOST. Forget the notation for a second. “You have your n numbers. I am allowed to walk in and replace m of them with anything I like — not plausible values, anything. A trillion. Minus a trillion. I get to choose, and I am choosing to wreck your answer. The question is: how many do I get before your statistic is worthless?” The sup over y is me choosing the worst values. The < ∞ is your statistic surviving. ε* is the largest share I can be given and still fail.
Then make it their number: for the mean I need one of you to mistype your inbox count — that is 1/17, and it goes to zero as the class grows. For the median I need nine of you to lie, and we would have to coordinate. That is the whole difference, and it is not about which is more accurate.

The Influence Function

QUESTION 2 OF 2How much damage does ONE bad point do? Answer: a curve. Flat means a single point can never matter much, however far out it sits.

$$\text{IF}(x; T, F) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{T\big((1-\varepsilon)F + \varepsilon \delta_x\big) - T(F)}{\varepsilon}$$

\(F\)

the clean distribution your data came from

\(\delta_x\)

one bad point, dropped in at position \(x\)

\(\varepsilon\)

how much of it you mix in — then shrink to nothing

\(\text{IF}\)

the damage per unit of that contamination

An observation at $1 trillion has exactly the same influence on the median as one at $500,001. Both are above — and above is all the median records.

SAY: Breakdown point is a global question: how much garbage before collapse. The influence function is local: the derivative with respect to one infinitesimal blob. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: They are two ways of measuring the same thing.

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min.
  • The sentence to land: unbounded influence means an adversary, a broken sensor, or a mis-keyed form can make your estimator return any number they choose.
  • If you are behind schedule, this is the slide to cut.
  • Hampel 1974;
  • Huber 1981;
  • Donoho and Huber 1983.

IF ASKED: “Which one should I report?” → Breakdown for an adversarial setting; the influence function when you want to know which observations are doing the work.

PODCAST 14:57 The cleanest way to separate the two formal definitions, and it takes ten seconds: the breakdown point asks how many punches the method can take before it is knocked out; the influence function asks how much damage one punch does. Then the consequence, which is the sentence this slide exists for: to the median, “a $1 trillion observation has the exact same influence as an observation just one dollar above the midpoint” — it only asks are you above me or below me, and once you are above, your influence is maxed out. Bounded influence is what insulates you from a catastrophic typo.

Two Rulers for the Same Rule

Same rule, different ruler: centre on the median, scale by MAD, and the tail cannot bend it.

$$z_i = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s}$$

Classical. Distance from a centre the outliers moved, in units of a spread the outliers inflated.

$$z_i^{\text{robust}} = \frac{x_i - \text{median}(x)}{1.4826\cdot\text{MAD}(x)}$$

Robust. Identical rule, a ruler that the tail cannot bend.

This is Bug 1 in your lab: a function called modified_zscore that quietly computes series.mean() and series.std(). The docstring is right. The code is not.

SAY: Identical rule, different ruler. The classical z measures distance from a centre the outliers moved, in units of a spread the outliers inflated. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: The robust z-score uses a different threshold.

Isolation Forest: Twenty Questions

Ordinary in every variable, strange as a combination — Isolation Forest finds those.

A Tukey fence asks one variable at a time. Sixty homes — bedrooms against total rooms.

The idea. Pick a variable at random, pick a cut point at random, ask which side this home is on — repeat. How many questions until it sits alone? One game is a tree; a hundred averaged is the forest.

WALK THE FIGURE IN THIS ORDER — it is built to be read in three moves:

  • The strip under the plot is bedrooms on its own. The red tick is our home. It sits in the middle of everybody — 19th percentile. Tukey flags nothing.
  • The strip left of the plot is rooms on its own. Red tick again, near the top but inside — 4 of the 59 other homes have more rooms. Tukey flags nothing.
  • Now the plot itself. The crowd forms a band because rooms grow with bedrooms. Our home is off the band, alone in a hole. Nothing changed about the numbers — only that we read them together.

Isolation Forest: Invert the Question

Invert the question: do not model normality — ask how few random splits isolate a point.

$$s(x, n) = 2^{-\,E[h(x)] / c(n)}$$

Interpretation: \(E[h(x)]\) is the point's average depth across the trees and \(c(n)\) the average depth of a typical point, so the exponent reads “how shallow was this point, relative to normal.”

\(s \to 1\)
isolated at once — anomalous

\(s \approx 0.5\)
typical

\(s \to 0\)
buried in a dense cluster

Linear time. No distance calculations. No density estimation. (Liu, Ting & Zhou 2008.)

Isolation Forest · contamination

SAY: Instead of modelling what normal looks like and measuring deviation, ask how easy each point is to isolate. Anomalies fall out in a handful of splits. ⏸ 3 s.

DON'T SAY: The forest learns which variable is anomalous.

BACKGROUND:

  • 3 min. c(n) is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree of n items — it is a normalisation so scores are comparable across sample sizes.
  • State that, do not derive it.
  • The conceptual inversion is what matters: isolatability instead of density.

3 min.

DON'T SAY: contamination is the share of outliers in the data.  |  The score is a probability.  |  A more negative score means further from the mean.

WALK THE FOUR LINES:

  • features = df.drop(columns=["MedHouseVal"]) — the target is dropped on purpose. We are not predicting price here; we are asking which rows are odd across the eight predictors. Nothing is labelled, so nothing is supervised.
  • n_estimators=100 — the same 100 trees they just watched average out on 33.2. One tree is luck; a hundred is a score.
  • iso.fit(features) — builds the trees by random cuts. It never sees a label and never computes a mean or an SD. Worth saying out loud, because every method before this one did.
  • labels = iso.predict(features) — −1 or 1. This is the only line that uses contamination.

WHAT contamination=0.05 ACTUALLY IS:

  • It is where you draw the line, not an estimate of anything. You are telling it: flag me the worst 5%. It obeys exactly — 0.05 gives 1,032 of 20,640, 5.00%; 0.10 gives exactly 10.00%.
  • The model does not change. Refit at 0.10 with the same seed and every raw score is identical and the ranking is identical — only the cutoff moves (sklearn stores it as offset_, −0.5135 at 5%, −0.4836 at 10%). So contamination never discovers different anomalies. It slides a line down an unchanged list.
  • Why 0.05 here: because Tukey flagged 5.2%, and comparing two lists of wildly different lengths would be meaningless. That is a defensible reason and it is the kind you must be able to give. There is no 5% in the data waiting to be found.
  • sklearn's default is contamination="auto", which picks a cutoff from the original paper rather than from your data — also a judgment, just someone else's.

HOW TO READ decision_function:

  • The sign is the verdict, the size is the rank. Negative = flagged, positive = not, and the threshold is exactly 0 — in this fit the worst inlier is +0.00001 and the mildest outlier is −0.00016.
  • It is a rescaled average number of cuts — 33.2 made visible. Few cuts to isolate → low score → negative. It is not a probability, not a distance, and not comparable across datasets.
  • Use it by sorting, not by reading. −0.21 versus −0.02 means “look at this one first”, not “ten times as strange”. The output is a triage queue: sort ascending and start at the top.
  • The range here is [−0.2116, 0.1565] — narrow, and that is normal. Nobody should read meaning into the absolute value.

IF ASKED “what is the right contamination?” — there isn't one. Pick it from how many cases you can actually afford to investigate, then say so in the write-up. That answer is the four-questions slide coming up. random_state=42 is the Chapter 1 reproducibility callback.

Key Takeaways

1. The mean's breakdown point is \(1/n\) and it goes to zero as your sample grows. Big data dilutes each bad row without bounding any of them.

2. The median is resistant, not immovable. It moved $1,600 under 1% contamination. Say the true thing.

3. Robust is not conservative. At the same threshold of 3.5, the robust rule flagged 400 where the classical one flagged 215 — because the classical rule let the tail set its own threshold.

4. 90% of California's “outliers” were a top-code. Detection tells you which rows are unusual. Only you can say whether unusual means wrong, censored, foreign, or interesting.

5. Three defensible methods agreed on 322 of 1,336 flags. No method decides for you, so make the decision explicit, parameterised and testable.

6. Dropping a point is a modelling choice. It belongs in the methods section with a number attached, not in a cleaning cell with a comment.
