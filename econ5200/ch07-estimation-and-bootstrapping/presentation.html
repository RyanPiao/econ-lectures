1  •  The Estimator Has a Distribution

Seven minutes. You have seen this; we need one sentence of it sharpened.

A Point Estimate Hides a Question

Point estimate

One number, from one sample. Tells you where you landed.

Sampling distribution

The distribution of that number across every possible sample of the same size. Tells you how much you could have missed by.

The framing that makes the rest of today possible. The bootstrap does not estimate a standard deviation. It estimates a distribution — and a standard error is just one summary of it. Once you hold that, quantiles, ratios, Gini coefficients and quantile treatment effects are all the same problem.

ACS puts median Massachusetts household income near $96,500 in 2024 — from about 100,000 sampled households out of 2.9 million. A different 100,000 gives a different number. How different is the entire question.

2 min.

Do not linger.

The graduate audience knows this; what they have often not internalised is that the object of estimation is a distribution, which is what licenses everything from Section 5 onward.

Standard Deviation Is Not Standard Error

$$\text{SE}(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Where: \(s\) is the sample standard deviation — the spread of the data; \(n\) is the sample size.

Interpretation. The standard error is the standard deviation of the estimator, not of the data. SD answers “how spread out are individual incomes?” SE answers “how uncertain is our estimate of the average?” They are different objects, and only one of them shrinks when you collect more data.

Misconception: “Standard error is standard deviation over root \(n\).”

True for the mean of i.i.d. draws. For the median, a correlation, a Gini coefficient or a quantile treatment effect the formula is different — and usually does not exist at all. That absence is the entire market for the bootstrap.

2 min.

Write both sentences on the board side by side.

The \(\sqrt{n}\) is the quantitative payoff of more data: not that the data gets less variable, but that the estimate gets more precise.

Lognormal(11.0, 0.8) population of 100,000. Draw 1,000 samples of size 100 and compare the spread of the data with the spread of the 1,000 sample means.

2  •  The CLT, and Its Rate

Not whether it arrives. How fast, and in what shape.

The Central Limit Theorem

$$\sqrt{n}(\bar{X} - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \sigma^2)$$

Where: \(\bar{X}\) is the sample mean, \(\mu\) the population mean, \(\sigma^2\) the population variance, and \(\xrightarrow{d}\) convergence in distribution.

Interpretation. Whatever shape the population has — skewed, bimodal, uniform — the average becomes approximately normal as \(n\) grows. Normality in applied work is not a property of data. It is a property of averages.

Chapter 5's LLN said \(\bar{X} \to \mu\). The CLT says how fast and in what shape — and that rate, \(n^{-1/2}\), is the number Section 5 of this lecture is going to try to beat.

Misconception: “The CLT means my data is normally distributed.” It applies to sample means and sums, never to individual observations.

2 min.

Say the last line out loud: the whole Advanced Treatment block is an attempt to beat the exponent in this theorem.

That framing makes Section 5 feel inevitable rather than ornamental.

Watch the Skewness Die at Rate \(n^{-1/2}\)

All three curves are standardised, so any departure from the dashed normal is pure shape error.

At \(n = 5\) the mode is pushed left and the right tail is dragged out. That asymmetry is the leading Edgeworth correction term — it is proportional to the population skewness and it dies at \(n^{-1/2}\).

By \(n = 500\) it is invisible to the eye. It is not zero. Coverage error at nominal 95% still runs at order \(n^{-1/2}\), and Section 6 measures exactly that at \(n = 50\).

2 min.

Exponential population, 6,000 replications per n.

  • Ask them to name the direction of the n=5 error before you point at it: on right-skewed data the standardised mean is right-skewed too, so the interval misses low more often than high.

Poll: What Does the Bootstrap Substitute?

The bootstrap principle says the relationship between a resample and your sample mimics the relationship between your sample and the population.

Stated formally: what is being substituted for what?

A) The normal distribution, for the unknown sampling distribution
B) The empirical distribution \(\hat{F}\), for the unknown population \(F\)
C) A larger sample, for a smaller one
D) A parametric model, for a non-parametric one

1 min to vote.

Do not reveal until the bar chart settles.

C is the answer that reveals someone thinks resampling manufactures information.

Answer: B — It Is a Plug-In Estimator

You want a functional of \(F\) — the sampling distribution of \(T(X_1,\dots,X_n; F)\). You do not have \(F\). So you substitute \(\hat{F}\), which puts mass \(1/n\) on each observed point, and compute the functional exactly under \(\hat{F}\) by simulation.

Why C is the dangerous answer: resampling manufactures no information. Every bootstrap sample uses only the original observations. If \(\hat{F}\) is a poor stand-in for \(F\) — a biased sample (Ch 6), or a tiny one — the bootstrap reproduces that faithfully and confidently.

2 min.

A is the parametric interval, i.e. the thing we are avoiding.

  • Push on C: the bootstrap has exactly the sample's information content, no more, and that is why Chapter 6 is a prerequisite rather than a digression.

3  •  The Parametric Interval, and the Wall

Six minutes to reach the place where the formula runs out.

Building the Interval from the CLT

$$\text{CI}_{95\%} = \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Where: \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean, 1.96 the standard normal quantile \(z_{0.025}\), and \(s/\sqrt{n}\) the standard error of the mean.

Interpretation. Across repeated application of this procedure, about 95% of the intervals it produces contain \(\mu\).

What it does NOT say. “There is a 95% probability the true mean lies in this interval.” \(\mu\) is fixed; this interval either contains it or it does not. The 95% is a property of the procedure.

Hold that sentence. In Section 8 the Bayesian bootstrap will offer you a different one — and the difference between the two sentences is the only thing that distinguishes them.

2 min.

Graduate rooms know the frequentist reading but state it loosely under pressure.

  • Making them hold the sentence now is what makes the Bayesian-bootstrap contrast land in Section 8 rather than sounding like trivia.

The Wall: What Is the SE of a Median?

$$\text{SE}(\text{median}) = \frac{1}{2 f(\text{median})} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Where: \(f(\cdot)\) is the population density evaluated at the median; \(n\) is the sample size.

Interpretation. The precision of the sample median depends on how densely observations pile up around the true median. A tall narrow peak (large \(f\)) means perturbing the sample barely moves the median. A flat region means small perturbations move it a lot.

And there is the wall. The formula needs \(f\) at the median — which requires knowing the population distribution, the very thing you were trying not to assume. For skewed, multimodal or heavy-tailed data it is unreliable.

The Fed's SCF reports median household net worth ($192,700) beside a mean of $1.06 million, precisely because the mean is a billionaire's plaything. Having reported the median, it then owes you an interval for it.

3 min.

Ask the room to extend the list: the 90th percentile, a Gini coefficient, a ratio of two estimated means, a quantile treatment effect.

  • All the same wall.
  • This is the slide that makes the bootstrap necessary rather than merely available.

4  •  The Bootstrap, and the First Way It Fails

Ten minutes. We build it, then break it on purpose.

The Algorithm, in English Before Any Code

1

Start with your sample \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\)

2

Draw \(n\) observations with replacement

3

Compute your statistic on that resample — any statistic

4

Repeat \(B\) times. The distribution of those \(B\) values approximates the sampling distribution

Why with replacement? Without it, every resample is the original sample reshuffled. With it, each resample holds roughly 63.2% unique observations and 36.8% duplicates — the same machinery that makes bagging work in Chapter 19.

3 min.

Deal five index cards face up, draw with replacement in front of the room, and let them see a card appear twice and another not at all.

  • That variation IS the sampling variability being estimated.

How Large Should \(B\) Be?

Purpose\(B\)Why
Standard error200A summary of the centre. It stabilises fast.
Confidence interval2,000–10,000Endpoints live in the tails, which need more draws to pin down.
BCa interval5,000–10,000Bias correction and acceleration add noise that averages out slowly.
Bootstrap \(p\)-value9,999 (odd)An odd count keeps the observed statistic off a percentile boundary. 19,999 for publication.

The distinction that matters, and we will test it later. \(B\) controls Monte Carlo error — the noise from simulating rather than enumerating. You can drive it to zero by computing longer. It does nothing at all to approximation error, which is a property of the method and of \(n\).

Practical check: run it twice with different seeds. If the endpoints move meaningfully, raise \(B\).

Plant the Monte-Carlo-vs-approximation distinction hard here.

Now Break It on Purpose: Median Age, Adult Census

An analyst reports a 95% CI for median age and gets [37.0, 37.0]. They conclude the median is known exactly and move on. Here is what the code actually prints:

n:                 32,561
Sample median:     37.0
Bootstrap SE:      0.000000
Percentile 95% CI: [37.0, 37.0]
Distinct values across 2,000 resamples: 1

This is not a bug. Age is integer-valued and heavily tied at the middle of the distribution. At \(n = 32{,}561\) the median is pinned: every one of 2,000 resamples returns 37. The bootstrap is correctly reporting that the estimator does not move.

What it is NOT reporting: that the population median is 37.0 with certainty. It is reporting that the resampling variability of this statistic, at this \(n\), is below the resolution of the statistic itself.

Lab 7, Diagnosis 1 — executed on this machine. Hours-per-week does the same thing: median 40.0, SE 0.000000, one distinct value.

3 min.

Let them sit with the zero before you explain it.

  • The generalisation to state aloud: a statistic that takes finitely many values on a large sample has an almost-degenerate sampling distribution, and no resampling scheme can manufacture variation that is not there.

Answer: B — The Jackknife Has Nothing to Divide

The acceleration is a ratio of third to second moments of the jackknife deviations \(\hat\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)}\). Every one of those deviations is zero here, so numerator and denominator both vanish. You will see the formula in ten minutes.

Why A is tempting and wrong: more resamples is the reflex, and it changes nothing. The degeneracy is in the statistic, not in the simulation. D is wrong in a useful way: integer data is perfectly fine for the bootstrap — what breaks it is ties dense enough at the median that the order statistic never crosses a boundary.

5  •  Advanced Treatment

Why the bootstrap is more accurate — and at what rate

Edgeworth expansions, asymptotic refinement, and where BCa's two constants come from

Three Intervals, as Three Slicing Rules

Percentile. \(\text{CI} = [\hat\theta^*_{0.025},\; \hat\theta^*_{0.975}]\) — take the middle 95% of the replicates. Assumes the bootstrap distribution is centred and symmetric about the truth.

Basic (reverse percentile). \(\text{CI} = [2\hat\theta - \hat\theta^*_{0.975},\; 2\hat\theta - \hat\theta^*_{0.025}]\) — reflect the distribution through the sample estimate. Better motivated: it uses the distribution of \(\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta\) as a proxy for \(\hat\theta - \theta\). Still assumes symmetry.

BCa. Slice at different percentiles — chosen from the data rather than fixed at 2.5 and 97.5.

All three read the same simulated distribution. They differ only in where they cut it. The question for the next ten minutes is which cut has the smaller coverage error, and by how much as \(n\) grows.

2 min.

Draw one bootstrap histogram on the board and mark the three cut rules on it.

Everything after this is about moving two vertical lines.

The Studentized Statistic

$$T^* = \frac{\hat{\theta}^* - \hat{\theta}}{\widehat{\text{SE}}^*}$$

Where: \(\hat\theta^*\) is the statistic on a bootstrap resample; \(\hat\theta\) the statistic on the original sample; \(\widehat{\text{SE}}^*\) an estimate of its standard error computed inside that resample — an inner bootstrap, or a delta-method formula.

Interpretation. Dividing by an estimate of its own scale makes the statistic asymptotically pivotal: its limiting distribution no longer depends on unknown population parameters. That is the entire trick. Everything on the next three slides follows from it.

Cost, stated honestly. You need a standard error per resample. An inner bootstrap makes it \(B \times B_{\text{inner}}\) work. That expense is why percentile and BCa are the defaults in practice and bootstrap-\(t\) is reserved for statistics with a cheap analytic SE.

3 min. “Pivotal” is the word to slow down on.

The analogy: a t-statistic is pivotal under normality, which is why Gosset's table works for every mu and sigma.

  • Asymptotically pivotal is that property recovered without the normality.

Edgeworth: Where the Extra Order Comes From

The normal approximation has coverage error of order \(n^{-1/2}\):

$$P(\mu \in \text{CI}) = 0.95 + O(n^{-1/2})$$

Hall (1992) writes the distribution of the statistic as the normal plus a series in powers of \(n^{-1/2}\), whose leading correction term depends on the population skewness — the very asymmetry you saw at \(n=5\) two sections ago. A properly studentized bootstrap statistic reproduces that leading term instead of ignoring it, so it cancels:

$$P(\mu \in \text{CI}_{\text{boot}}) = 0.95 + O(n^{-1})$$

This is what “asymptotic refinement” means. Not that the bootstrap is closer for your sample. That the error shrinks one full order faster as the sample grows. At \(n = 100\), \(n^{-1/2}\) versus \(n^{-1}\) is a factor of 10.

Hall, P. (1992), The Bootstrap and Edgeworth Expansion, Springer — full proof under regularity conditions.

3 min.

The sentence to repeat: the refinement is bought by CANCELLING a known term, not by simulating harder.

If they leave with only one line from this block, that is the line.

One Order, and What It Is Worth

Same constant, two exponents. At \(n=100\) the second-order error is 10× smaller; at \(n=1{,}000\), 32×.

The shaded gap is the refinement. It is bought by studentizing. It cannot be bought with more resamples, a bigger \(B\), or a faster machine.

Which curve is the plain percentile bootstrap on? The upper one. That is the next slide, and it is the most common overclaim in applied work.

Curves are illustrative of the two rates with a common constant — say so.

The Condition Everyone Skips

The refinement requires a pivotal statistic. The plain percentile bootstrap is not studentized. It is first-order accurate — \(O(n^{-1/2})\) — exactly like the CLT interval you were trying to improve on.

BCa's Two Corrections — the Idea, Before the Algebra

Bias correction \(z_0\)

“Is the bootstrap distribution centred on my estimate, or systematically off to one side?”

Measured by counting what fraction of replicates fall below \(\hat\theta\). If exactly half, \(z_0 = 0\).

Acceleration \(a\)

“Does the standard error itself change as the parameter changes?”

Measured by the jackknife: delete one observation at a time and look at the skewness of how the statistic responds.

When \(z_0 = a = 0\), BCa IS the percentile method. The corrections only bite where the bootstrap distribution is skewed or the SE varies across the parameter space — which is precisely where medians, ratios and quantiles live.

2 min.

Give the idea before the algebra or the next slide is impenetrable.

The one-sentence gloss on each constant is what they should be able to reproduce in an exam, not the formula.

BCa: the Adjusted Percentiles

$$\alpha_1 = \Phi\left(z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_{\alpha/2}}{1 - a(z_0 + z_{\alpha/2})}\right) \qquad \alpha_2 = \Phi\left(z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_{1-\alpha/2}}{1 - a(z_0 + z_{1-\alpha/2})}\right)$$

Where:

\(z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{\#\{\hat\theta^*_b < \hat\theta\}}{B}\right)\) — bias correction, read straight off the bootstrap replicates.

\(a = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^3}{6\left[\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^2\right]^{3/2}}\) — acceleration, where \(\hat\theta_{(i)}\) deletes observation \(i\) and \(\hat\theta_{(\cdot)}\) averages them.

\(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF; the interval is then \([\hat\theta^*_{\alpha_1}, \hat\theta^*_{\alpha_2}]\).

Interpretation. Both formulas do one thing: take the percentile you wanted, push it through a normal-scale correction, and come back with the percentile you should actually cut at. Set \(z_0 = a = 0\) and both collapse to \(\alpha/2\) and \(1 - \alpha/2\).

DiCiccio, T.J. & Efron, B. (1996), “Bootstrap Confidence Intervals,” Statistical Science 11(3), 189–228.

3 min.

Point at the numerator of a and say: cubed deviations, so it is a third moment, so it is a skewness measure.

  • Then point at the denominator and note it is a squared-deviation sum to the three-halves — a scale normalisation.
  • That reading is worth more than the derivation.

What Those Constants Did to Our Interval

Same 10,000 replicates. Two different pairs of cut points.

Skewness 3.41 makes \(z_0\) and \(a\) both non-zero, and they move both endpoints the same way: +$43 at the bottom, +$57 at the top.

BCa shifts; it barely widens. Students expect a correction to mean “a wider, safer interval.” This one is an aim correction, not a caution margin.

2 min.

If someone asks whether the shift is big enough to matter at n = 2,712: no, and say so.

  • It matters at small n and for skewed non-linear statistics.
  • The point of the slide is the DIRECTION and mechanism, not the magnitude.

And Now the nan Makes Sense

Twenty minutes ago BCa refused to give an interval for median age. You now have the reason in front of you:

$$a = \frac{\sum_i (\hat\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^3}{6\left[\sum_i (\hat\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^2\right]^{3/2}} = \frac{0}{6 \cdot 0^{3/2}}$$

Delete any one of 32,561 ages and the median is still 37. Every jackknife deviation is exactly zero. Numerator zero, denominator zero, and scipy declines to invent a number.

The transferable habit. When a library returns nan with a warning, the warning names a step in an algorithm you can write down. Find that step, and the nan stops being a nuisance and becomes a diagnosis of your data.

2 min.

This is the payoff of holding the mystery.

Say explicitly: you could not have debugged this from the error message alone; you needed the formula, which is why graduate courses teach it.

Poll: What Actually Buys the Refinement?

You want a bootstrap interval with second-order accuracy — coverage error \(O(n^{-1})\) rather than \(O(n^{-1/2})\).

Which change buys it?

A) Raise n_resamples from 999 to 100,000
B) Studentize the statistic so it is asymptotically pivotal
C) Use the basic method instead of percentile
D) Collect a larger sample

1 min.

A is the trap and it is the answer a well-prepared student gives, because more computation feels like more accuracy.

Watch the bar chart before you say anything.

Answer: B — Pivot, Do Not Compute Harder

A reduces Monte Carlo error — the noise from simulating rather than enumerating — which you can drive to zero by computing longer. It does nothing to the approximation error, which is a property of the method and of \(n\). C shifts the interval but stays first order. D reduces error under either rate without changing the rate.

The practical rule. Percentile for exploration. BCa for anything published — second-order accurate and scipy's default for exactly that reason. Studentized bootstrap-\(t\) when an inner SE is cheap and the stakes justify it.

6  •  Coverage, and Its Own Error Bar

The theory of Section 5, measured — including the part that says the bootstrap wins nothing.

The Only Test That Matters

Across many repeated experiments, does a nominal 95% interval actually contain the true parameter 95% of the time?

The design. 400 experiments. \(n = 50\) per experiment, drawn lognormal(3.5, 0.5). 499 resamples per interval. True mean 37.525, true median 33.115, both known by construction. Four arms: two targets × two methods.

And one thing most write-ups omit — a coverage simulation is itself an experiment, so its output is an estimate with noise:

$$\text{MC SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{T}}$$

Where: \(p\) is the measured coverage and \(T\) the number of trials. Interpretation: the standard error of the coverage number itself. Any gap smaller than this is not a finding.

2 min.

For the Median: 28 Points, and It Is Not Noise

66.8%

the mean's interval, asked about the median  (MC SE 2.4)

92.8%

bootstrap interval for the median  (MC SE 1.3)

There is no clean parametric interval for the median, so an analyst reaches for the one they have: the interval built around the mean. It covers the true median two times in three.

The mechanism is simple and it does not go away. On right-skewed data the mean sits well above the median, so an interval centred on the sample mean is aimed at the wrong target. More data narrows it around the wrong place; it never recentres it.

The bootstrap interval, built around the statistic actually being asked about, covers 92.8% — the same first-order performance as everything else on this slide, which is exactly the right benchmark.

Economist at Work: Netflix's Quantile Bootstrap

Play delay — the gap between pressing play and video starting — is aggressively right-skewed across a subscriber base that passed 300 million paid memberships at the end of 2024. Most stream instantly; a tail buffers badly. An average hides exactly the users you need to hear about.

Netflix evaluates quantile treatment effects: the difference in the 50th, 75th and 95th percentiles between treatment and control. Non-parametric quantiles have no closed-form standard error — so the interval has to come from a bootstrap.

The engineering trick. Compress the metric into (value, count) pairs, then bootstrap the counts using Poisson-approximated multinomial draws. Computational cost becomes independent of the original sample size — which is what turns a textbook method into production infrastructure.

Keep two Netflix results apart when you cite them. The quantile bootstrap is about computing an interval cheaply. Chou et al. (2025) — replaying 123 historical A/B tests under better decision rules for roughly a 33% improvement in cumulative north-star returns — is about what to ship, not about the bootstrap.

Netflix Tech Blog, “Reimagining Experimentation Analysis”; Chou, Gray, Kallus, Bibaut & Ejdemyr (2025), KDD Best Paper, arXiv:2502.08763.

Dependence, and the wild cluster bootstrap

The failure mode that will actually bite your own research

The Expensive Failure, Measured

1,000 workers across 25 firms. Firms differ a lot; workers within a firm resemble one another. True mean wage $60,000 by construction. The analyst bootstraps them as 1,000 independent draws.

Between-firm SD:    7,689          Within-firm SD:  2,946
Sample mean wage: $59,588          (true mean $60,000)

Naive (i.i.d.) bootstrap 95% CI: [$59,092, $60,121]   width $1,029
Clustered      bootstrap 95% CI: [$56,624, $62,465]   width $5,841
                                  the honest interval is 5.7x wider

The naive interval happens to contain the true mean this time. That is not a defence — it is one draw. The question is what the procedure does over repeated application, which is the next slide.

2 min.

Note the between-firm SD is nearly three times the within-firm SD: that ratio is the whole story.

The effective sample size is far closer to 25 than to 1,000.

Repeat It 200 Times and Count

Nominal is 95%. The naive interval is not slightly optimistic. It is wrong four times out of five, and it never says so.

Why. Resampling workers destroys the firm structure. It treats 1,000 observations as 1,000 independent draws when the effective sample size is closer to the number of firms. The fix is to resample whole firms with replacement.

Compare with the degenerate median: that failure announced itself with a zero and a warning. This one returns a beautiful, narrow, confident, wrong interval.

3 min.

This is the most expensive of the three failures and the most common in applied micro.

Overconfidence is worse than imprecision: it licenses rejecting nulls you have no business rejecting.

The Regression Version: Wild Cluster Bootstrap

Resampling clusters works for a mean. For a regression coefficient with few clusters, Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008) showed standard cluster-robust (Liang–Zeger) variance estimators over-reject badly — 10% or more against a nominal 5% when clusters number below roughly 50.

1

Estimate the model under the null: \(Y = X\hat\beta_0 + \hat{u}\)

2a

Draw one weight per cluster \(g\) from the Rademacher distribution: \(P(v_g = 1) = P(v_g = -1) = 0.5\)

2b

Build \(Y^*_i = X_i\hat\beta_0 + v_{g(i)}\hat{u}_i\), re-estimate, compute \(T^*_b\)

3

Bootstrap \(p\)-value: \(\frac{1}{B}\sum_b \mathbb{1}(|T^*_b| > |T_{\text{obs}}|)\)

Cameron, A.C., Gelbach, J.B. & Miller, D.L. (2008), Review of Economics and Statistics 90(3), 414–427.

3 min.

Read step 1 twice. Imposing the null is the step people omit, and omitting it costs exactly the size control they came for.

Why Each Moving Part Is There

Per cluster, not per observation. Every residual in a firm gets flipped together. That is what preserves the intra-cluster correlation the naive bootstrap destroyed.

Rademacher specifically. Mean zero, unit variance, and symmetric. The symmetry is what delivers the best size control when clusters are few.

Residuals from the restricted model. Imposing the null is what makes the resulting distribution a null distribution — the thing a \(p\)-value is computed against.

Very few clusters (5–10 states in a DiD). MacKinnon & Webb (2018): use the subcluster wild bootstrap and Webb six-point weights instead of Rademacher. MacKinnon, Nielsen & Webb (2023) give the modern treatment and the fast algorithms in boottest.

2 min.

Connect back to Chapter 11: their DiD designs cluster on state, and a US-state DiD with a handful of treated states is exactly this regime.

This is not a curiosity; it is their midterm.

8  •  Going Deeper

The Bayesian bootstrap

Same numbers, different sentence

Reweight Instead of Resample

Rubin (1981). Instead of resampling observations with probability \(1/n\), give each observation a random weight:

$$(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n) \sim \text{Dirichlet}(1, 1, \ldots, 1)$$

Where: the weights are non-negative and sum to 1. A Dirichlet(1,…,1) draw is uniform over the simplex.

Interpretation. The ordinary bootstrap draws counts from a multinomial — an observation appears 0, 1, 2, … times. The Bayesian bootstrap replaces those integer counts with continuous weights from the closest continuous analogue. Compute the statistic as a weighted version, repeat, and the resulting distribution approximates the posterior under a non-informative prior.

Why an economist should care. Model-free Bayesian inference with no MCMC: trivially parallel, no chain to tune, no convergence diagnostics. And because the weights are continuous, it does not suffer the lumpiness that produced our degenerate median.

Rubin, D.B. (1981), “The Bayesian Bootstrap,” Annals of Statistics 9(1), 130–134.

3 min.

The Dirichlet(1,...,1) is the limit of the normalised multinomial counts, which is why the two agree asymptotically.

Lab 7's extension has them implement it in three lines and compare.

Discussion: The Word That Cannot Move

At \(n = 500\) the frequentist and Bayesian bootstrap intervals are numerically almost identical. They license different sentences.

Think about:

Frequentist: “across repeated application, 95% of such intervals contain \(\theta\).” Bayesian bootstrap: “given the data, \(\theta\) lies in this interval with 95% probability.” Mark the word that cannot be moved between them.

Consider:

At what sample size would you expect the two intervals to visibly disagree, and why? What feature of the data would make the gap appear sooner?

2 min pairs → 1 min share

3 min.

Debrief: the word is "probability" attached to theta.

  • The frequentist statement puts the randomness in the procedure; the Bayesian one puts it in the parameter.
  • Numbers agreeing does not make the sentences interchangeable, and a referee will notice.
  • They come apart at small n and where the discreteness of multinomial counts bites.

Comparing Two Groups, Assumption-Free

Compute \(\hat\delta = \hat\theta_A - \hat\theta_B\). Resample each group separately at its own size. Recompute the difference. Build the interval from the bootstrap distribution of differences.

Three Flavours of Resampling

MethodResamples fromAnswers
Monte Carlo (Ch 5)a known distribution“what does this model imply?”
Bootstrap (today)your data, with replacement“how uncertain is my estimate?”
Permutation (Ch 8–9)label shuffles, without replacement“could this difference be chance?”

All three are the same computational logic — repeated random sampling — pointed at three different questions. Choosing the wrong one is not a coding error; it is answering a question nobody asked.

The wild cluster bootstrap of Section 7 is a fourth member of the family, and it sits between rows two and three: it resamples signs rather than observations, and it does so under an imposed null — which is why it produces a \(p\)-value rather than an interval.

Key Takeaways

It is a plug-in. \(\hat{F}\) for \(F\). It creates no information and inherits every bias in the sample.
