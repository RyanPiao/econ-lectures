The Thesis

An index is a compression algorithm for reality.

Compression always discards.

The only question worth asking about any number:
what did it discard, and does that matter for the question I am asking?

1 min. This sentence is the spine. You will return to it four times — at the Dow, at the substitution wedge, at the free-good problem, and at MSI. Tell them that now so they hear the callbacks.

1  •  What an Index Is

The weighting scheme is the measurement.

The Laspeyres Price Index

$$CPI_t = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_{t,i} \, Q_{0,i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_{0,i} \, Q_{0,i}} \times 100$$

Interpretation. It answers exactly one question: what would it cost today to buy what a typical consumer bought in the base year?

What freezing $Q_{0,i}$ buys
Comparability over time. Simplicity. One basket, one story.

What it costs
People substitute away from what got expensive. The index assumes they did not. Substitution bias, and it is upward.

2 min. Do not derive anything — they can read a summation. Spend the time on the two-column trade-off, because the entire next section is the attempt to buy back the right-hand column without giving up the left.

The Dow: Same Companies, Different Answer

The CPI is expenditure-weighted. The Dow is price-weighted — a \$200 stock carries four times the weight of a \$50 stock regardless of company size.

Two stocks: A at \$200, B at \$100. Sum \$300, divisor 0.152 → index 1,974.
A splits 2-for-1. Nothing economic changes. Sum drops to \$200 → 1,316. A 33% crash caused by arithmetic.

So the committee resets the divisor to 0.101, and the index reads 1,974 again. It has been adjusted hundreds of times since starting at 30 in 1928.

Your Index BS Detector

1. What is in the basket?

CPI: 80,000 items. Dow: 30 stocks.

2. How are components weighted?

CPI: spending shares. Dow: share price.

3. When was it last updated?

CPI: every two years. Dow: at committee discretion.

4. Who benefits from it being high or low?

Recipients want high. Employers want low.

Not a CPI trick. You will apply these to $R^2$ in Ch 12 and to feature importance in Ch 19.

Poll: The Base-Period Trap

A colleague rebases the CPI to 2019 = 100 before deflating a wage series, instead of leaving it at 1982-84 = 100.

What happens to the real wage growth rate between 2019 and 2024?

A) It rises — a later base raises measured real growth
B) It falls — a later base lowers measured real growth
C) Nothing — rebasing is a constant rescaling
D) Undefined — you cannot deflate with a rebased index
1 min. Students vote on their devices; results on the next slide. Expect a real A/B split — base periods feel consequential because they are prominent.

Answer: C — Nothing Happens

Rebasing multiplies the whole index by a constant, and a constant cancels in any ratio of two deflated values. The base period sets the units your real series is quoted in — “2020 dollars” — and nothing else.

Why A and B are tempting: in the Laspeyres formula the base period also fixes the quantity weights — and that genuinely does change the number. Two different jobs, one name. Separating base period as unit of account from base period as basket vintage is the whole question.

2  •  Which Index Answers Which Question

Four formulas. Four questions. One theorem that says when the choice stops mattering.

The Obvious Repair: Use Today's Basket

$$P_t^{\text{Paasche}} = \frac{\sum_i P_{t,i} \, Q_{t,i}}{\sum_i P_{0,i} \, Q_{t,i}} \times 100$$

Interpretation. How much less would it have cost in the base year to buy what people buy today? A different question, not a better answer to the same one.

Laspeyres
overstates
frozen basket ignores substitution

Paasche
understates
today's quantities already embody the switching

Two indexes. Known, opposite biases. That is an opportunity.

2 min. The key framing, and it is the one they will get wrong on the exam: Paasche is not "the correct version of Laspeyres." It answers a different counterfactual. Both are right; they are right about different things. Hold the "opportunity" line — it sets up Fisher.

Two Ways to Split the Difference

$$P_t^{\text{Fisher}} = \sqrt{P_t^{\text{Laspeyres}} \times P_t^{\text{Paasche}}}$$

Interpretation: the geometric mean of the upper and lower bound.

$$\ln P_t^{\text{T}} = \sum_{i} \tfrac{1}{2}\left(s_{0,i} + s_{t,i}\right) \ln\frac{P_{t,i}}{P_{0,i}}$$

Interpretation: each good's log price change, weighted by its average budget share across the two periods. The geometric analogue of Fisher.

$s_{0,i}$ and $s_{t,i}$ are good $i$'s share of total spending in the base and current periods. Where Fisher averages two indexes, Törnqvist averages two weighting schemes.

3 min. Read every symbol out loud — s is a share, the sum of s over i is one in each period. The half is a simple average of the two share vectors. Do not prove anything. The punchline is on the next slide and it is empirical, not algebraic.

Diewert (1976): Superlative Indexes

Fisher and Törnqvist are both superlative: each is exact for a flexible cost function, so they agree to second order whatever the true preferences turn out to be.

Do not over-claim this. Superlative is a statement about approximation order, not about being right. The precise claim: a superlative index approximates any twice-differentiable homothetic cost function to second order.

It does not say the index equals the true cost-of-living index. It says the choice among superlative indexes stops mattering before the measurement error in the underlying price quotes does.

2 min. If a student pushes on this, the sentence in the red box is your answer verbatim — twice-differentiable, homothetic, second order. The last line is the practically useful one: once you are inside the superlative family, stop arguing about the formula and go worry about the 80,000 price quotes instead.

Poll: Why the Geometric Mean?

Fisher is the geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche, not the arithmetic mean. Why?

A) The arithmetic mean of two biased indexes stays biased; the geometric mean does not
B) Diewert's exactness result — Fisher is superlative for a flexible cost function; the arithmetic mean has no such property
C) Computational convenience with log-differenced data
D) Only the geometric mean is guaranteed to lie between the two
1 min. D will collect real votes. That is the productive error and the reveal is built around it.

Answer: B — It Is a Theorem, Not an Intuition

The justification is a result about which functional forms an index is exact for — not an averaging intuition.

Why D is the good wrong answer: it is a true statement about Fisher — but the arithmetic mean also lies between the two bounds, so “lies between” cannot be the reason for preferring one. Distinguishing true of X from the reason for X is the entire question.

Quality Adjustment: Hedonic Regression

$$\ln P_i = \alpha + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \beta_j z_{ij} + \epsilon_i$$

Interpretation. Regress log price on product characteristics. $\beta_j$ is the implicit price of one more unit of characteristic $j$ — screen size, processor speed, RAM. Rosen (1974).

$$\Delta P^{\text{adj}} = \left(P^{\text{new}} - P^{\text{old}}\right) - \sum_j \hat\beta_j \left(z^{\text{new}}_j - z^{\text{old}}_j\right)$$

A laptop costs \$50 more but carries \$120 of estimated quality improvement → the CPI records a \$70 price cut while the sticker price rose.

3 min. This is the one place a graduate audience relaxes, because it is just a regression they already know — use that. The unfamiliar part is not the estimator, it is that the fitted coefficients are then used to SUBTRACT a counterfactual quality gain from an observed price change. Hold the fragment until they have read the second equation.

Choosing a deflator is choosing a question. The ruler is part of the finding.

The Operation You Already Know

$$\text{Real Value}_t = \frac{\text{Nominal Value}_t}{CPI_t} \times 100$$

Interpretation. Divide by the price level to express purchasing power in constant units. Multiplying by 100 rescales into base-period dollars.

Against \$55,000 today that is a 36% real gain — not the 1,000% the nominal comparison suggests.

4  •  Goodhart as Measurement Design

Not a cautionary tale. A design constraint.

Goodhart's Law

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

Charles Goodhart, 1975 — from watching UK monetary aggregates stop being informative the moment the Bank of England targeted them.

This is not about dishonesty. It is about optimisation. Agents optimise the measured proxy, not the concept — and the two come apart.

2 min. Ask the room for numbers that stopped being useful once people maximised them — GPA, standardised test scores, follower counts, citation counts, h-index. Take three or four. Do not rush this; their own examples are what makes the Meta case land later.

Every KPI Is an Index

DAU / MAU — the “stickiness ratio.” Facebook's ran around 0.65, best in class. But “active” counts a one-second notification dismissal identically to a thirty-minute session.

It measures frequency. Never quality.

$$LTV = \frac{ARPU}{\text{monthly churn rate}}$$

Interpretation. \$12.99/month at 5% monthly churn implies \$259.80 of expected lifetime revenue. It assumes churn and ARPU are constant — and both are false. Churn is front-loaded.

The trap: teams improve “LTV” by redefining churn — a paused subscription counted as active — rather than by retaining anyone.

2 min. Run the four questions from Section 1 against DAU/MAU out loud. What is in the basket? Every session, weighted equally. How is it weighted? It is not — it is a count. When was it updated? Never. Who benefits? The growth team whose bonus depends on it. Same detector, entirely different domain.

Three Mechanisms — the Fix Depends on Which You Have

1 · Optimisation
against the measure

The index has fiscal consequences, so its construction comes under pressure. CPI ↔ COLAs, TIPS, tax brackets.

Fix: institutional independence; publish the method

2 · Gaming
the measure

Agents inflate the number without improving the thing. Meta's notification tuning; UK REF star-researcher poaching before assessment.

Fix: counter-metrics

3 · Obsolescence
of the measure

The basket stops matching the world. Gym memberships still weighted during lockdown; home office equipment underweighted.

Fix: reweight more often; population-specific indexes

2 min. The diagnostic discipline is the whole point — naming "Goodhart" is useless, naming the mechanism tells you what to do. Mechanism 3 is the callback to the two-year CEX weight lag from Section 1.

Meta's MSI: the Counter-Metric That Was Goodharted

January 2018 — the repair

Meta re-ranks News Feed around Meaningful Social Interactions, explicitly to move off passive consumption toward real interaction between people.

Exactly what the theory prescribes: a counter-metric.

2021 — the second failure

MSI weighted reshares and comment threads heavily. The ranking system learned the reliable route to “meaningful interaction” is content that provokes argument.

Internal research found it amplifying divisive material. Meta's own data scientists had flagged it.

A counter-metric is still a metric. It compresses, it becomes a target, and it can be optimised into a failure of its own.

Index repair is not a state you reach. It is maintenance you keep doing.

Poll: What Does MSI Prove?

Meta built MSI as the counter-metric to passive consumption — and MSI was Goodharted in turn.

What follows for measurement design?

A) Counter-metrics do not work; rely on qualitative judgement instead
B) A counter-metric is itself a metric — it needs its own guardrail; repair is maintenance, not a terminal state

1 min. Expect D to poll well — it is the engineer's instinct.

Answer: B — Repair Is Maintenance

Why A is wrong: the counter-metric did work — it detected and corrected the original passive-consumption problem. It then acquired a gaming surface of its own.

Why D is tempting: more metrics do raise the cost of gaming. But every metric you add is another target, and a system optimising against ten proxies is not obviously better aligned than one optimising against two. You have multiplied the surface, not removed it.

The Vocabulary to Leave With

North Star

The one metric best proxying delivered value. Spotify: time listening. Airbnb: nights booked. Choosing it is as consequential as the BLS choosing the basket.

Guardrail

Must not degrade while the North Star is optimised. If “time listening” rises because autoplay queues unwanted songs, “skips within 10 seconds” should catch it.

Counter-metric

Built specifically to detect gaming — and, per MSI, itself gameable. The immune response, not immunity.

Metric tree

$\text{Revenue} = \text{Users} \times \text{Conversion} \times \text{AOV}$. Decomposition makes explicit which sub-metrics drive the aggregate — and which are ignored.

Ch 1 callback. DAU/MAU is ratio-scale — 0.5 really is half of 1.0. NPS is ordinal: the 6→7 gap is not the 9→10 gap. Averaging NPS is averaging zip codes.

Key Takeaways

An index is a compression algorithm. Compression always discards. Ask what it discarded and whether that matters for your question.

Laspeyres and Paasche bound the answer. Our wedge: 9.0 points, with Fisher and Törnqvist 0.05 apart. Superlative is a claim about approximation order, not truth.

The deflator is part of the finding. “Real wages were flat for fifty years” is a joint claim about wages and the CPI. At 0.45 pp/yr, the ruler is doing real work.

Goodhart is a design constraint. Diagnose the mechanism — optimisation, gaming, or obsolescence — because the fix differs for each.

The counter-metric is not immunity. MSI was the repair, and it was Goodharted. Measurement is maintenance — and you have to ask “what did this discard?” of the guardrail too.
